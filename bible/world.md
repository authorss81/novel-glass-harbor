# World Bible: The Glass Harbor

## World at a glance

The inhabited world is a chain of old harbors around the **Vesper Sea**, a cold, tide-driven ocean bordered by salt marsh, kelp shelves, basalt reefs, and wind-cut islands. The sea is the region's food supply, road system, and political boundary. Most people do not think of it as empty space; they think of launch times, harbor mouths, weather windows, debts, and the families waiting on the other side of a crossing.

The Serein Dominion is a large maritime empire that governs the eastern and central coasts. It controls charts, customs courts, naval rescues, the Lantern Registry, and the legal definition of a wreck. Its authority is strongest where people need a safe crossing and weakest where a harbor can feed itself through salvage, fishing, and local craft. The western free ports are politically unruly but materially poor. The coastal communities are not empty political abstractions: they have canneries, boatyards, schools, kitchens, graveyards, mutual-aid societies, and long memories of which official reports arrived late.

The drowned city of **Orison** lies seventy fathoms below the western shelf in a trench of tilted streets and silent shipyards. Its transparent memory vessels remain in several layers, held together by a material called **echo-glass**. Some are nearly intact. Some have been stripped for panels, bell-buoys, or weapons. Some are ordinary wrecks that have become tangled with the glass fleet. The city is not a magical afterlife and has no population waiting to be freed.

## Geography

### Morrow Quay

Morrow Quay is the story's home harbor and Calder's base. It is a weathered stone-and-timber port with a small commercial fleet, a public dry dock, two fish markets, and a free salvage association called the **Free Keel**. Its charter lets local crews work wrecks below the imperial line in exchange for a share of recovered material. The charter is legal only because the Serein court has not decided that the harbor is strategically important enough to revoke it.

Morrow Quay's people depend on salvage, fishing, rope-making, kelp harvesting, and occasional imperial contracts. The harbor is politically independent in speech and economically dependent in practice. Its citizens argue about whether independence is worth the cost of keeping their own boats seaworthy.

### Veyr

Veyr is the Dominion capital on a high island inland from the main naval yard. It contains the Lantern Registry, the Court of Wreck Claims, the Ministry of Returning Memory, and the Crown Council's maritime offices. Veyr is not a fairy-tale palace: it is a bureaucratic city of waiting rooms, copying rooms, naval supply depots, rain tunnels, and officers who speak in carefully limited language.

The Office of Returning Memory, led later by **Magister Ilya Serr**, decides what counts as a stable echo, a valid claim, a recoverable panel, and a dangerous contagion. The office claims that its work is the reason the coast has not repeated the Quietening.

### Ashtide

Ashtide is a crowded cannery port. Its warehouses, fishers, coopers, and shipwrights make it the strongest manufacturing base between Morrow Quay and Veyr. Ashtide's council wants the freedom to keep local testimony but will trade with the Dominion if the alternative is starvation. It is an important test of whether an open memory network can support ordinary commerce rather than only heroic emergencies.

### Ternwatch

Ternwatch is a fortified naval town. Its harbor faces the central sea and its forges make pumps, pressure bells, signal buoys, and armor for the Crown Navy. The people there are not uniformly loyal to the empire. Many have relatives in independent ports and know that the navy's food and ore shipments depend on the same contested waters the Dominion claims to protect.

### Lowfen

Lowfen is a chain of reed islands, floating markets, and old drainage works. Its people navigate by shallow channels and oral markers more than by imperial charts. The islands contain older civic marks that predate Orison, but the water around them is unstable and difficult for large crews. Lowfen becomes the model for a distributed route system that does not require a central city.

### Saltmarch

Saltmarch is a storm-belt island where pilots, glassworkers, and weather readers maintain old channel buoys. Its people are practical and superstitious in different ways. They believe a storm remembers the paths taken by ships that did not return, but they do not treat the sea as consciously speaking.

### Tidewatch

Tidewatch is a small independent listening port on the western reef line. Its people repair signal bells, maintain the first public route codes, and distrust both the Crown's charts and the salvagers who treat every buoy as a souvenir. Mina Kett works from a workshop there. Tidewatch becomes the bridge between Morrow Quay's crews and the wider Nine Harbor network.

### Cinder Bay

Cinder Bay is a soot-stained industrial port between Ashtide and the old salt mines. Its furnace crews heat tideglass, its shipwrights build pressure housings, and its council trades heavily with the Dominion. Cinder Bay is the network's material bottleneck: the open routes need its glass, fuel, and maintenance even when its politics remain uncertain.

### Orison Deep

The drowned city lies in a faulted trench: former streets, quays, bell towers, and shipyards are scattered across the shelf. The transparent vessels are concentrated in the **Old Keel Field**, the **Crown Stair**, and the **Quietening Cut**, a deep trench where the worst cascade occurred. The bottom is not empty. It contains ordinary bodies, tools, cargo, bones, broken glass, and dangerous pockets of air. No salvage operation can treat the site as a single treasure room.

## The nine final routes

The historic Nine Harbors correspond to nine present-day coastal route names. These are not nine new kingdoms and not nine permanent representatives. They are maintained approaches, each controlled by the communities that use them and each able to refuse a shared route without surrendering its local archive.

1. **Morrow Line:** the harbor line and outer shoals around Morrow Quay.
2. **Ashtide Run:** the cannery channel and the southern trading approaches.
3. **Ternwatch Passage:** the navy-facing channel used for both rescue and blockade.
4. **Lowfen Reed:** the shallow route through the floating islands.
5. **Saltmarch Weatherway:** the storm-belt route maintained by pilots and bellwrights.
6. **Cinder Bell:** the industrial approach between the mines, furnaces, and Ashtide.
7. **Tidewatch Channel:** the western reef route linking the independent listening bells.
8. **Pale Shoals:** a long, shallow route with few reliable public buoys.
9. **Orison Descent:** the final approach into the drowned city's trench. It is the most dangerous route and the one most often refused.

A route can be active, restricted, or dormant. No route is a permanent claim over the whole sea.

## Working waters and the old network

- **Gannet Shoal:** a shallow, shifting sandbar outside Morrow Quay. The *Aster Gannet* seized there thirteen years ago, and the current has since moved the old wreck marks across the channel.
- **Bramble Strait:** a storm channel between Saltmarch and the western shelf. Its remembered route is useful but never current enough to replace a survey.
- **Unlit Shelf:** a deep shelf below Orison where the old civic network stored experimental and emergency vessels. A trench on the shelf contains the **Unlit Keel**, a blank hull and later mechanism designed to remove testimony from a living crew.
- **Ninth Bell:** a submerged bell-tower in Orison's civic center. It was the old network's final separator and is now the physical interface where the Crown Keel tries to make one witness out of many. The bell has no consciousness; its danger comes from the networks connected to it.
- **Crown Bell:** a branch relay in Veyr. It amplifies a controlled claim through seized vessels and is not the final central mechanism. Anyone who calls it “the central bell” is speaking from inside the Crown's administrative fiction.

## The sea and its dangers

- **Pressure:** Echo-glass and old streets behave differently at depth. A safe descent is a physical job involving air, pumps, lines, helmets, and a watch. Calder's magic does not remove those requirements.
- **Returning storms:** Storm patterns tend to follow old navigation channels. A memory vessel can preserve the route a crew once used, but the route may be decades old. A remembered current is not a guarantee of safe water.
- **Tideglass grit:** Fine glass sand can cloud water, cut pumps, and irritate lungs. Repeated work around a wreck leaves crews vulnerable to a chronic condition called tide-sickness.
- **Pressure bells:** A bell-shaped air chamber is lowered with a diver. Its signal and air supply are ordinary engineering; no magic substitutes for a damaged gasket or a trained tender.
- **Anchor lines:** Every working diver has an independent line and a tender above. A line is not a magical leash; it is a tool that fails, tangles, and sometimes saves a life.
- **Glass storms:** When a connected hull takes in contradictory or forced testimony, a local pressure field can reorder sound and visibility. People may hear voices from the wrong direction or lose the ability to distinguish an echo from their own thought. This is a physical and psychological hazard, not a generic monster.

## History

### Before Reckoning: the civic fleet

More than six centuries ago, the communities around Orison developed a common method for preserving testimony. They used tideglass from the deep shelves, shaped it into panels, and connected panels with metal keel cords. A vessel was not a single person's recording. It was a distributed record built from witnesses: a fisher's storm, a child's evacuation, a doctor's testimony, a builder's explanation of a wall, or a family's account of leaving a village.

The **Nine Harbors** were not nine identical cities. They were a loose civic compact linking ports, fisher cooperatives, repair crews, and bell builders. Their central rule was **no harbor may claim sole custody of another harbor's testimony**. The compact was imperfect and frequently disputed, but its purpose was stewardship rather than empire.

### The Quietening, 312 years before the present

A state archive called the **Crown Office of Returning Memory** predates the Dominion by a generation. Its engineers believed that a single agreed testimony could prevent communities from weaponizing conflicting records during war. They built the **Crown Seal**, a linked keel mechanism intended to make compatible witnesses reinforce one another.

The seal did not remove disagreement. It forced incompatible experiences into the same pressure field. Witnesses began borrowing one another's fear, grief, and certainty. Some people could not distinguish a remembered event from an imposed one. Several harbor crews died by panic, suffocation, or preventable mistakes. The full disaster became the **Quietening**.

The Nine Harbors voted to break the keel-lines and scuttle the transparent fleet. The choice was collective, costly, and deliberately imperfect. They saved the wider coast by abandoning much of the record and losing the people who remained in Orison. Some accounts say the vote was unanimous; later evidence shows that several delegates had already died, fled, or been overruled. The truth is a civic failure, not a perfect moral act.

### The Serein Accord and the modern Dominion

Thirty-two years after the Quietening, surviving coastal authorities formed the **Serein Accord**. They needed charts, courts, naval protection, and a shared system for handling dangerous glass. The Accord gradually became the Serein Dominion. Its founders built a useful public archive, but they also made a political error: they treated custody as ownership and consolidated every major wreck under Crown law.

The Dominion's official history calls Orison a city destroyed by a sea catastrophe. It calls the old fleet a dangerous ruin. The Lantern Registry labels unclaimed testimony **unsettled** and can quarantine it until a recognized claimant appears. In practice, “unsettled” often means “valuable to someone who has enough power to claim it.”

### The Gannet Cut, thirteen years before the present

The imperial mnemonic cutter **Vigil** intercepted the ferry *Aster Gannet* near Morrow Quay. Oren Marr, Calder's older brother, was aboard as a deck apprentice. The cutter was carrying a sealed glass cargo whose manifest did not match the public registry. During the seizure, a keel-line tore. The ferry went down in a storm with sixty-three workers aboard.

The official report calls the crossing illegal and the loss unavoidable. The surviving pressure log contains a deliberate three-second gap. Calder's father, Alden, was pressured into signing a salvage report that omitted the cargo. The investigation was closed before most families could question it. Ilya Serr was a junior registry official at the time; he signed the transfer of the cutter's evidence and later used the disaster to support stricter custody laws. Calder does not know this until late in the series.

## Factions and institutions

### The Serein Dominion and Crown Navy

The Crown is a governing alliance of a council, naval command, courts, and ministries. It is capable of building large infrastructure and is not simply a villainous army. Its strength is that a single order can move ships, seize a wreck, close a harbor, or declare an echo unsafe. Its weakness is that centralized control requires people to trust one another's reports.

The Crown Navy's **Admiral Soren Vey** enforces the blockade during the middle volumes. The **Office of Returning Memory** handles glass, testimony, and claims. The navy and the office often distrust each other, but both benefit from keeping the public from direct access to unclaimed vessels.

### Lantern Registry

The Registry records salvage claims, ship manifests, glass provenance, and legal custody. Registry staff are trained to distinguish a settled witness mark from a forced or altered one, but their certification is still a bureaucratic act rather than proof of truth. The Registry employs **Mara Venn** before she becomes a public conservator.

### Free Keel

Free Keel is a loose association of independent salvage crews based in Morrow Quay. It shares tools, rescue plans, and legal challenges. It has no central leader. Free Keel believes a wreck should go to the community that risks the recovery unless a clear claim is proven, and it is frequently broke, suspicious, and protective of its members.

### The Nine Harbor Assembly

The Nine Harbor Assembly begins as a historical name used by archivists and illegal civic organizers. It becomes an informal alliance of coastal communities, fisher cooperatives, bell builders, conservators, and salvage crews. It has no permanent central government. Its rules are debated, and its members can withdraw. The formal **Open Keel Assembly** is chartered later, when the informal network can make a public procedure for claims, repairs, and withdrawal rather than relying on Calder's personal leadership.

### Bellwrights

Bellwrights are the craftspeople who work tideglass into panels, buoys, pressure-safe cases, and listening bells. They are engineers, artists, and maintainers. Their guilds have been co-opted by the Registry, but independent workshops in Lowfen, Ashtide, and Morrow Quay keep older techniques alive.

## Everyday life

People do not discuss memory magic every day. They discuss wages, boat repairs, missing relatives, insurance, tide windows, school schedules, and whether the Crown will reopen a harbor. At funerals, families may place a salt-bowl near the door so a departing memory is not lost; at weddings, a couple may choose a small bell rather than a vow written on paper. These customs vary by port and are not proof that the sea is sentient.

Salvage crews are paid in shares, not guaranteed wages. A successful recovery pays for pumps, repairs, funerals, and the harbor's legal fees. A bad recovery can bankrupt a household. Diver families know the ordinary dangers: bad air, flooding, debt, lung disease, and the pressure to keep working after an injury. Memory magic adds emotional danger, but it does not replace those economic facts.

Children learn the bell code before they learn the names of distant capitals. They learn that a red cord on a buoy means a dangerous seam, a white cord means a remembered route, and a black cord means no one is permitted to touch the panel until the local assembly speaks. The meaning of these signals is social as much as magical.

## The moral and material rules of the setting

1. A memory vessel can be physically recovered; a witness's consent cannot be recovered by force.
2. A stable echo can still be wrong, partial, or self-serving. Settled does not mean true.
3. A single government can own a legal claim while lacking moral or practical custody.
4. The sea is a common resource, but no one is automatically entitled to every wreck or every memory.
5. Public access can cause harm; secrecy can cause greater harm. The story must make both costs visible.
6. The city of Orison was destroyed by a real cascade, so the ending cannot dismiss the fear of uncontrolled access as irrational.
7. No event opens a portal to another world, summons a god, or introduces a new final enemy.

## Consent in ordinary practice

A witness chooses the scene, the anchor, the storage, and the permitted uses when making a settled echo. A claim seal can mark a panel “private,” “family,” “route use,” “public witness,” or “sealed.” The mark records those limits, even when the Crown later disputes them. A living witness can withdraw future use; a narrowly defined emergency may permit temporary access to save a life or prevent immediate physical harm, but requires two independent custodians, a written reason, and a restored seal. A dead witness cannot be asked, so a community may interpret a record or restrict it without claiming a perfect substitute for consent.

The final network succeeds with permitted testimony and explicit gaps. It does not overcome refusals by opening sealed panels. That distinction is a hard rule of the plot, not a decorative promise.

## Reveal discipline

The first volume may establish the existence of the Gannet Cut, the glass fleet, and Ilya Serr's office. It should not explain the Quietening, identify the Crown Seal, or prove who altered the ferry manifest. Each later answer must be earned by a physical artifact, a conflicting testimony, a labor-based expedition, or a political document whose limitations are understood.
