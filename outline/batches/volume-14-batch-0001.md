## Chapter 651 — *One Line on a Wall, and a Blank Beside It*

**Monday the second of May. A rota day and the hundred and forty-sixth charge. The bench stone, the shed, and the wall at the back of that shed.**

**Goal:** put one line on a wall that this yard did not write, and find out what this party is when it has nothing on paper. **Resistance:** the line is short, it is somebody else's, and nobody in that yard is entitled to add a word to it — and the yard is asked, in the same nine people, whether it has a line of its own, and it has not got one ready and does not write one. **The turn of the chapter:** the answer to a yard that will not say what it will not carry is not a better sentence. It is a blank space on that wall with the yard's name at the head of it and nothing under it, and the reason is entered in the second column and not in a speech.

**Beats.** That cistern is charged in eleven trips by hand and walked on by none of it, and it is the hundred and forty-sixth charge and nobody walks on the water after it. At about two in the afternoon a man of about forty-one of the third lay of that port four days down that coast, who came down that coast road in the morning and is about forty-one and has a burn on the flat of his right hand from a line he has held too long, stands in about nine people at the back of that shed and reads out one line and puts it on the wall himself with four tacks. **The line is what the Saltmarch Weatherway will not carry, and it is eleven words, and it is that they will not carry an echo that nobody of that route can answer for.** Nobody read it out twice, because it is one line and it is his, and about four people in that nine heard the whole of it and the rest of that market was not told. Nobody agreed with him, nobody argued with him, nobody thanked him. At about half past three the man who keeps the leaf asks, in the same nine people, whether this party has a line of its own, and the yard does not write one before six o'clock and nobody in that nine fills the silence.

**Ending:** a wall with one line on it and a blank space beside it, and the reason in the second column with an hour against it, and the wooden tally slate still lying flat on the bench stone where it was put down on the first of May and not picked up again. **Not in this chapter:** the Crown boat; the requirement; the fouled buoy; the woman's four lines about her record.

## Chapter 652 — *The Eighthieth Wage Tuesday, and an Account of a Boat That Was Not Answered*

**Tuesday the third of May. The eightieth wage Tuesday. The lid of that step, and the boards at the back of that market.**

**Goal:** read out the column and get to the bottom of what a boat saw. **Resistance:** the man of about fifty-three's column has been read out eighty times and does not move, and the account that comes in at about eleven is an account of a Crown boat taking up the moorings on the only route anybody is holding, and it is worth nothing as evidence. **The turn of the chapter:** the fouling is the oldest fact in the chapter and it is in the mouth of a woman of about twenty-four of the third lay who can tell March from May by the colour of green on a shackle, and the thing that happened on Sunday is the thing that happened after the fouling, and a boat that takes up a mooring does not make itself into an accusation by being written down.

**Beats.** The lid is read out at about ten in four minutes by a man of about thirty-one of the second lay and stands at twenty-six lines and one hundred and four marks and does not move for the eightieth time, and the chest with the twenty envelopes in it is shut from ten until four, and the eightieth week stands fallen due at the close of that day behind the seventy-ninth. At about eleven a woman of about twenty-four of the third lay comes into that yard with a wet sleeve and puts an account down and it goes into the first column with the day and the hour against it: a close-sea boat of about forty tons went alongside the bell buoy on the Saltmarch Weatherway at about six in the morning on Sunday the second, put a boat's crew on it, and took the moorings up, and went off about half past seven. **She does not give the name of the boat and she will not be asked for it twice.** And she says the thing that holds the chapter together: the buoy was fouled before that boat came and not after, and she can tell the difference because the green was on the shackle in March and the shackle was bright where the green was not, and a bright shackle under a belt of green is a shackle somebody handled. At about four in the afternoon the man of about thirty-two says out loud, in about nine people, in about sixty-one words, what this yard is not, and it is that this party does not hold the Morrow Line either, and that a buoy on a route nobody here holds is not this yard's to take back, and that this yard is not the body that decides where any boat goes. Nobody agreed with him and nobody argued with him.

**Ending:** an account in the leaf with the day and the hour against it and no boat's name on it, and a bright shackle under a belt of green. **Not in this chapter:** the requirement; the line on the wall; the woman's four lines; the hire.

## Chapter 653 — *Four Lines That Say What a Record Carries, and a Disagreement in Nine People*

**Goal:** get four lines onto that wall saying what a record of a line on a wall will carry, and stop there. **Resistance:** the man of about thirty-two disagrees out loud, in the same nine people, and the disagreement is the chapter. **The turn of the chapter:** both of them are right about their own half and neither half can be taken off the page, so the page goes up with four lines under it carrying neither of them, and the second column says the yard wrote both of them down and settled neither.

**Beats.** At about eleven in the morning, in about nine people at the flat of that bench stone, a woman of thirty of no office reads out four lines and then four more. **The four that say what it carries:** the name of the route, the day, the line itself in the words of the people who wrote it, and whether a name is on it or no name is on it. **The four that say what it does not do:** it does not say why a community wrote that line, it does not carry a figure of persons, it does not say whether anybody agreed, and it does not stand in for anybody who wrote nothing. **Ending:** eight lines on a wall and a blank under them where the argument would go, and the form with two limits in print still in the inside of a coat and on no sheet and on no wall. **Not in this chapter:** the requirement; the hire; the buoy; the wipe.

## Chapter 655 — *Eight Lines at the Head of a Sheet, and a Name Under a Seal Nobody Reads*

**Goal:** get a Crown requirement read out once without arguing with it. **Resistance:** it is not wrong about anything anybody has checked, and its cost is a person on a line all the time, and there is no version of this yard that can pay it. **The turn of the chapter:** nobody is asked to put a name down, and the sheet that would hold a custodian's writing is a sheet this yard does not have and does not set up, and the leaving of that alone is the chapter's real act.

**Ending:** a sheet under a stone at the head of those boards with the stone put back on it, and no page of anybody's set up beside it. **Not in this chapter:** the answer to it, which is Chapter 659; the water; the wipe; the night's work.

## Chapter 656 — *About Two Fathoms, a Tender With a Watch, and a Seam Not Used*

**Saturday the seventh of May. Nothing on the calendar but the day itself. In about two fathoms off the Weatherway buoy, and on the deck of a boat of about thirty tons.**

**Goal:** get a fouled shackle off a buoy that a Crown boat has already taken the moorings off. **Resistance:** the route's own people give the order and not this yard, and the diver can see a join in the ring that would tell him the moorings were cut and not slipped, and using it is the shortcut and it is not used. **The turn of the chapter:** the fouling comes off by hand in about eleven minutes and it is a belt about an inch thick and it has been there longer than a season, and the bright shackle under it is the same bright shackle the woman of about twenty-four described on Tuesday, and the finding costs nothing and settles nothing.

**Beats.** Out at about seven with a tender on the surface and not in the water, an independent line, a fixed air limit of twenty-five minutes written down with the hour against it before anybody goes down, and a cutoff the tender can pull without asking. **The reason is written down first and it is not a reason about a join, and it says that the shackle is fouled and that a fouled shackle answers wrong.** At about two fathoms, about eleven minutes, one handhold and no second line's worth of hurry, and he does not use the join in the ring and says in the second column afterwards why, and what he says is that a join shows that two things were made separately and it does not show which of them was made first. Nothing comes up but a shackle, a pin and about an inch of green in a bucket, and the buoy is not lifted and the moorings are not retied and the ring is left as it is.

**Ending:** a bucket with a belt of green in it and a ring left as it was, and a man on the deck of a boat of about thirty tons whose hands were not steady enough to hold a wedge afterwards and who said so out loud in about forty-one words and was not argued with. **Not in this chapter:** the wipe; the line down; the requirement's answer; anything about Orison.

## Chapter 657 — *The Hundred and Fifth Time, and a Bell Hung by the People Who OWN It*

**Goal:** put the wall back together after a Sunday and let the route hang its own buoy. **Resistance:** nobody asked for this and the wall takes longer than it did a fortnight ago, and the buoy is hung by the route's own hands and this yard stands on the deck. **The turn of the chapter:** the second line goes up on that wall on that Sunday and it is the route's and not this yard's and it is in different words from the first, and the page of hands gets its second figure written against it with the day and the hour against it.

**Beats.** Two men begin to take that wall down at about ten past ten and it is bare by about half past ten and it is back on its holders at about eleven at the angle it has carried since the twelfth of January, and it did not go up whole. **Twenty-two minutes to get it off the wall, nine standing bare, twenty-two to get it back, being fifty-three the whole of it**, and that is a minute longer each way and a minute longer bare than the first of May, and nobody in that harbor has said why it is getting slower and this yard has stopped asking the two men who do it. At about three in the afternoon a bell buoy of about two tons is hung by about six men of the third lay of that route on that route's own moorings and this yard sends a hand and does not touch the shackle. **And the second line goes up on that wall in about nine people at about five o'clock, and it is the Lowfen Reed's, and it is in different words from the first, and it is nine words, and nobody read it out twice because it is one line and it is theirs.**

**Ending:** a wall with two lines on it and a blank beside them, a buoy hanging on somebody else's moorings, and fifty-three minutes the whole of it. **Not in this chapter:** the requirement's answer; the page of hands; the third line.

## Chapter 658 — *The Morrow Line's Own Page, Read Out to About Eleven People Who Cannot Read a Notice*

**Goal:** get a line on that wall for the only route in this harbor, and get it there honestly. **Resistance:** the Morrow Line is dormant, and a dormant route has fewer people who will put a name to anything, and about eleven people of that harbor cannot read a notice. **The turn of the chapter:** the line that goes up is longer than the other two and is in the plainest words anybody has put on that wall, and it is read out twice, and the second reading is not for the nine people who can read.

**Beats.** That cistern is charged in eleven trips and walked on by none of it, the hundred and forty-eighth. ## Chapter 659 — *A Second Page Beside It, and Not One Name on Either*

**Tuesday the tenth of May. The eighty-first wage Tuesday. The bench stone and the boards.**

**Goal:** answer a requirement that is not wrong without saying that it is wrong. **Resistance:** the only answer that works is expensive, because a coast that cannot pay a requirement has to publish what it did instead, and publishing what it did instead is a record somebody has to keep. **The turn of the chapter:** a second page goes up beside the requirement, carrying the eight lines of the requirement and beside each of them what this coast actually did, and the page says on its own face that it does not say the requirement is false, and not one name goes on it.

**Beats.** The lid of that step is read out at about ten in four minutes by a man of about thirty-one of the second lay and stands at twenty-six lines and one hundred and four marks and does not move for the eighty-first time, and the chest with the twenty envelopes in it is shut from ten until four, and the eighty-first week stands fallen due at the close of that day. At about three in the afternoon the second page goes up beside the sheet under the stone. **It carries the eight lines of the requirement on the left and, against each of them, what the coast did instead, in about nine words a line, and it says on its own face that it does not say the requirement is false and does not say the requirement was wrong about anything anybody has checked.** And it says the thing that is the whole of the chapter's answer, in fourteen words, that a requirement which cannot be paid is not a rule and is an argument, and an argument has to be had in nine places at once. **Not one name goes on that page and the man of thirty-two asks, in about nine people, that none do, and nobody puts one on.**

**Ending:** two pages on two surfaces, one under a stone and one with a stone beside it, and neither of them carrying a single name, and the disagreement of Chapter 653 still open on that wall and answered by nobody. **Not in this chapter:** the count of hands, which is Chapter 660; the line down off the wall; the Moorings.

## Chapter 660 — *Nine Figures at the Head of Nine Names and a Sum That Is Not Said Out Loud*

**Goal:** find out how many names there are against nine limits, and write the answer on a page where anybody can add it up. **Resistance:** the answer is not a good one and the fall in it over the last three days is not explained, and the man who keeps the leaf is not going to guess at a figure and is not going to say it out loud. **The turn of the chapter:** nine figures are written at the head of the nine lines of that page with the day and the hour against them, and the sum is written under them, and the sum is nineteen, and nobody says nineteen out loud and nineteen is not in the slip.

**Beats.** At about three in the afternoon the man who keeps the leaf writes nine figures at the head of the nine lines of that page of days, being four, three, three, two, two, two, one, one and one, in the order the nine names are always read in, and puts the day and the hour against them. **Four plus three plus three plus two plus two plus two plus one plus one plus one is nineteen, and nineteen is written under them.** A man of about fifty-three with a boat of about fourteen tons adds those nine up with a leaf and a pencil in about a minute, gets nineteen, reads the nine names down the side of that sheet and gets nine, and gives the sheet back and goes to work.
