## Chapter 861 — *Seven Sundays in It and No Figure for an Eighth*

**Goal:** get a fifty-day run of working days into the yard's own ordinary shape and have it say what it is out loud. **Resistance:** a run that begins on a Monday carries seven Sundays in it and not eight, so that wall comes down seven times in the next fifty days instead of eight, and **this harbor has a figure for the hundred and fortieth time and a figure for the hundred and forty-first time and no figure of any kind for an eighth, and nobody has ever needed one before, and about four people find the shape of the thing they cannot fill interesting and go on with their work.** **The turn of the chapter:** the run says out loud what it is and nobody fills in the gap. **About four people in that shed put one question out loud, in the ordinary way and to nobody in particular, being what the seventh Sunday of that run is for when there is no figure of any kind for an eighth, and nobody in that shed knows and nobody gives them a figure, and the keeper says out loud that a run of days is a run of days and that he is not going to give it a number until somebody tells him what the number is for. THAT IS THE ONE QUESTION OF THIS DAY AND IT IS NOT ANSWERED.**

**Beats.** That cistern went its fourteenth trip at about eleven for the two hundred and fifth time and the covers went on at about eleven and came off at about four and nobody walked on the water after it. Nine counters were carried to the close of that Monday and each one of them is its own figure from the Sunday before plus one. **Ending:** a run of ten days written on the back of something that is not a page, seven Sundays in it, and one question about the seventh of those seven put out loud in about nine people and answered by nobody. **Not in this chapter:** the count of the asked, which is not taken until the tenth day; the line on the fitting-out sheet; anything said to the man who puts the wall up; the page of routes; the first of the hundred and thirty-fifth wipe; **and any statement out loud about that wall being quicker last year, which is not said on this day and is said on the sixth.**

## Chapter 866 — *Line Two Worked and a Man Says Out Loud That the Wall Was Quicker Last Year*

**Saturday the third day of December. Nothing on the calendar but the day itself. The mole, the fitting-out shed, and the boards at the back of that market.**

**Goal:** work line two and get it on the sheet with the day it settles written on the face of it. **Resistance:** the coal and oil and the sixty yards of line and the six men are the ordinary figures, and **a lamp glass for that mole head is two marks and it is the second one bought this quarter, and nobody in that yard is asked whether the light is wanted by anybody who uses it.** **The turn of the chapter:** a man of about twenty-seven of that second lay, at about one, in the ordinary way and without stopping what he was doing, says out loud that that wall was quicker last year, and about four people agree with him, and **not one of those four has a figure for last year and not one of them ever had, and the man of about thirty-two does not say that he has one and does not say that he does not.**

**Beats.** Six men went out at about eight and the mole-head lamp glass went up in a hand and cost two marks and came out of the yard's own working money and not out of the bag. The line stands at one thousand and sixty-eight pence, being six marks and nine shillings, in five parts with the multiplication printed at every one, **and the eighth of December is written on the face of it and is the day it settles.** The man of about twenty-seven put down a length of coil he had cut and said the one thing and picked it back up and went on with it, and he did not say it twice and did not say it louder. **About four of that nine agreed with him out loud in four different ways and about four of those nine said out loud that they had not counted that wall themselves and were going on believing him, and the man of about twenty-seven said out loud that nobody has to, and picked the coil back up.** **One of about four of those nine then asked him, in the ordinary way and to nobody in particular, by how much it was quicker last year, and he said out loud that he did not know and did not measure it and was not going to, and he put the coil back down and went on with it and did not say it twice, and nobody asked him a second thing about it and nobody agreed with him out loud a second time. THAT IS ONE QUESTION ON THIS DAY AND IT IS ABOUT LAST YEAR AND NOT ABOUT A MINUTE A SIDE AND IT IS NOT ASKED OF THE MAN WHO PUTS THAT WALL UP.** The man of about thirty-two heard all of it and said nothing about it then and said something about it on the Monday.

**Ending:** a second worked line with its own parts printed, and a statement out loud that a wall was quicker a year ago, agreed with by four people, none of whom can say by how much.

## Chapter 867 — *The Hundred and Thirty-Fifth Time and a Man Who Says Nothing for the Fifth Sunday Running*

**Sunday the fourth day of December. A wipe and the hundred and thirty-fifth time. The lane the reeve's bench stands in, and that bench, and that wall.**

**Goal:** stand in front of a wall while it is off and take the figures off it. **Resistance:** **about four people are in front of it from the moment it comes off and about four of those four are the same four as last Sunday, and one of them is the man of about thirty-two, and one of them is a man of about thirty-one of the Lowfen Reed's own place who has now stood in that lane on five Sundays and said nothing on any of the five.** **The turn of the chapter:** the figures on the face of that wall are a minute longer each way than the figures off it the Sunday before, and **nobody in that lane asks anybody anything about that wall, including the man who put it back up, who was in the lane for the half hour and who was gone before the four of them compared anything, and who is not named by anybody and whose age is not said by anybody.**

**Beats.** That wall came off at about half past ten and came back up at a quarter to one with the top course of it left short and one whole face of it showing the mortar behind, and that is the hundred and thirty-fifth time anybody has done it to it. Fifty-two minutes off, thirty-nine minutes standing bare, fifty-two minutes back, one hundred and forty-three the whole of it, and **each of the two ends ran a minute longer than it ran the Sunday before, and nobody in that lane said a word about either end of it.** **The man who puts it up is not described in this chapter. He is a man and he is in that lane for the half hour and he does not speak to any of the four and none of the four speaks to him, and about four people in that lane could not tell you this afternoon what he looks like.** The man of about thirty-two said out loud, to nobody in particular, that it was a minute each end again, and the man of about thirty-one of that place said nothing, which is the fifth Sunday. **THREE QUESTIONS GO INTO THE COLUMN OF THE ASKED ON THIS DAY AND NONE OF THEM IS ABOUT THAT WALL AND NONE OF THEM IS ANSWERED: one man of about thirty-one of that place asks whether that bench is due to be painted and nobody answers him; a second man asks whether that cistern's covers come off at four or at half past four this time of year and nobody answers him; and a third asks whether the tide turns before that wall goes up or after, and nobody in that lane knows and nobody says, and they go back to counting. THEY ARE ASKED IN THE ORDINARY WAY AND TO NOBODY IN PARTICULAR AND THEY ARE THREE AND NOT NINE AND NOT TWELVE.** A man of about thirty-four of the second lay came through that lane at about eleven and was not asked anything by anybody in it, and it is the hundred and seventeenth day of that run.

**Ending:** four people counting a wall a minute slower each way than last week, one of them for the fifth Sunday saying nothing, three of them asking about a bench and a cistern's covers and a tide and getting nothing, and a man who put it back up and cannot be described by anybody who watched him do it. **Not in this chapter:** any explanation; the count of the asked, which is not taken until the tenth day; the settlement; the claim that the wall was quicker last year being repeated; anybody's name.

## Chapter 868 — *Line Two Settles and the Foot Read After the Settlement*

**Monday the fifth day of December. A rota day and the two hundred and seventh charge on that cistern. The boards at the back of that market, and the fitting-out shed.**

**Goal:** charge that cistern, settle line two, and read the foot after the settlement. **Resistance:** the two hundred and seventh trip is the same fourteen trips by hand as every one of the two hundred and six before it, and a settlement moves two figures and neither of them is a figure of anybody's labour. **The turn of the chapter:** **about four people in that shed say out loud, in the ordinary way, that a foot that is read after a settlement and a foot that is read before a settlement are two different numbers and that nobody in that lane has ever known which one the figures on that wall were counted against.**

**Beats.** The fourteenth trip went at about ten past ten and the covers went on and came off and nobody walked on the water after it. Line two settled at about four at one thousand and sixty-eight pence and the keeper read the whole of the foot out loud after that settlement and not before it, standing at one hundred and thirty-five thousand and fifty pence out, being eight hundred and forty-four marks and tenpence, and one hundred and twenty-four thousand four hundred and ten pence short, being seven hundred and seventy-seven marks, seven shillings and sixpence. **About four of that nine had that figure before he said it and about four of that nine did not, and nobody wrote it down out loud and nobody repeated it after him.** The keeper said out loud that a column gets added up once and in writing and a foot gets read once and out loud, and that the two of those are different, and that this shed has been mixing them up since September. ****ONE QUESTION GOES INTO THE COLUMN OF THE ASKED ON THIS DAY: about four of that nine ask, in the ordinary way and to nobody in particular, whether that two hundred and seventh charge is written down anywhere, and the keeper says out loud that it is on a rota and that a rota is a rota and not a page, and about four of that nine say out loud that they had not known that and did not say what they thought a rota was for.** A man of about thirty-one of that market said out loud that he did not know which of those two the figures on that wall were, and nobody answered him, and he did not ask again, and that was a statement and not a question and is not counted.**

**Ending:** a cistern charged for the two hundred and seventh time, a line settled, a foot read after the settlement, and a man saying out loud that nobody knows which of two kinds of reading the figures on that wall were counted against.

## Chapter 869 — *The Hundred and Tenth Wage Tuesday and a Column With Nothing in It*

**Tuesday the sixth day of December. The hundred and tenth wage Tuesday. The step outside the fitting-out shed, and that shed.**

**Goal:** have the hundred and tenth Tuesday go past with nothing on it but the week. **Resistance:** **the column of the asked is one column down the side of that page of days and it is empty, and about four people in that shed do not know it exists, and the keeper has not told them and is not going to tell them this month.** **The turn of the chapter:** about four people in that shed, in the ordinary way and to nobody in particular, ask the keeper how many wage Tuesdays there have been since the wages stopped, and he says out loud that he is going to add it up once, in writing, and that he is not going to say the number out loud to anybody this month and is not going to read it at that stone standing up.

**Beats.** The chest was on that step from about ten until about four and twenty envelopes were in it and none was opened and the column did not move a mark, and the hundred and eleventh week stands fallen due on all twenty-six. **A hundred and sixty-eight pence came in and a hundred and sixty-eight went out and the twenty-eight is still under that slate and that shed is two hundred and eighty-second days without a tin in it.** The keeper wrote the week's number in the column at the head of that sheet and wrote the fallen-due week underneath it and neither figure was read out to anybody. **A man of about thirty-one of that market asked at about one, in the ordinary way and to nobody in particular, whether anybody in that lane knew who counted the figures on that wall, and about four of that nine said out loud that it was nobody's business and one of that nine said out loud that it was nobody's job, and the man who said that said it about himself and about three other people and nobody asked him which.** The man of about thirty-two came into that shed at about three and stayed until about five and said nothing about any of it. **A man of about thirty-four of the second lay was on that bench from about ten until about two and was not asked anything by anybody in that shed or in that lane, and it is the hundred and nineteenth day of that run, and nobody in that lane has ever once asked him anything and that is the whole of what this volume has to say about him.**

**Ending:** the hundred and tenth Tuesday gone past, a column added up once in writing and not said out loud, and a man who said out loud that counting that wall was nobody's job.

## Chapter 870 — *A Man Asks Out Loud Whether That Wall Is a Figure on a Page Anywhere in This Harbor*

**Wednesday the seventh day of December. Nothing on the calendar but the day itself, and line three worked. The fitting-out shed, the boards at the back of that market, and the bench stone at the end of the mole.**

**Goal:** work line three and get it on the sheet with the day it settles written on the face of it. **Resistance:** the parts add up to one thousand two hundred pence and the three days of a boat hired at the figure in chalk is the biggest single part of that line and there is no figure anywhere in that yard for what a boat's day ought to be. **The turn of the chapter, and it is the turn of this movement: a man of about fifty-eight of that market asks, in about nine people and in the ordinary way, whether that wall is a figure on a page anywhere in this harbor. He is told, out loud, that it is not, and that the man telling him knows every figure on every page in that room and this one is on none of them. About four of that nine say out loud that a figure nobody has written down is not a record and one of that nine says out loud that a figure somebody has written down on a piece of wood in his own coat is not a record either, and nobody says which of those two is right.**

**Beats.** Five men went out at about eight and came in at about half past three and the line stands at one thousand two hundred pence, being seven marks, six shillings and eightpence, in five parts with the multiplication printed at every one, **and the twelfth of December is written on the face of it and is the day it settles, and its figure is not in the foot of that sheet and does not go into it until that day.** The keeper wrote the parts down in their own order and read the total back to nobody and did not add it to the foot of that sheet and said out loud why not. **The wooden slate was in that man's coat and stayed in that man's coat and nobody knew it was there.** **THE SECOND QUESTION OF THIS DAY GOES INTO THE COLUMN OF THE ASKED ALONG WITH THE FIRST: about four of that nine asked, in the ordinary way and to nobody in particular, who puts that wall up, and about four of that nine said out loud that they did not know, and the man of about thirty-two said nothing at all to that and nobody put it to him and nobody asked a second man the same thing. NEITHER QUESTION OF THIS DAY IS ANSWERED WITH A NAME AND NEITHER IS PUT TO THE MAN WHO PUTS THAT WALL UP, WHO IS NOT IN THAT SHED AND IS NOT NAMED.** About four people in that shed said out loud that a wall does not get counted, a bench does, and nobody answered that either. A man of about thirty-four was on that bench from about ten until about two and was not asked anything and it is the hundred and twentieth day of that run. **At about six that evening the keeper took a column and added it up once, in writing, and said out loud that he was not going to say the number out loud to anybody this month and was not going to read it at that stone standing up, and about four of that nine said out loud that they would come back and read it off the side of that leaf themselves.

**Ending:** one worked line not yet in the foot, a man told out loud that a figure about that wall is on no page in that harbor, a second question about who puts it up that nobody answers, and a column added up once in writing at about six and not read out in that shed and not in the half-past-five slip.

**The column of the asked on this day stands at two, and the ten days of it are the ten days printed at § *The things this batch fixes once*, and the whole of it is taken once in writing at about six and is in no mouth and is in no slip.** **Not in this chapter:** what the column came to; the slate; the hundred and thirty-sixth wipe; any explanation of a minute longer each way; the page of routes.**
