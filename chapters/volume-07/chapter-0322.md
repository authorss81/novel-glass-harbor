# Chapter 322

*Sunday the Sixth of June, a Chandler's Wife Who Counts, a Man of About Thirty-Six With No Candles, Eleven Weeks of a Season, and the Second Price Named in the First Column Before Anybody Says Yes*

Sunday the sixth of June, in the front street in the morning, and in the shed on the mole in the evening.

**The said Sunday was not a wage Tuesday. The wall behind the reeve's bench did not come down on it and the cistern was not charged on it, the cistern standing on a rota of a Monday and a Thursday. Nothing came into this yard on the said day and nothing went out of it, and there are twenty pence in the bag, being one shilling and eightpence, and a man of about forty-one of the second lay is not to put weight on the left foot for one more day and is not to be on the water for five more. The far bench was occupied on the said Sunday from about ten in the morning until about four in the afternoon and at no other hour of it.**

**The holder of that writ did not go over to the man on that bench at any hour of the said day, and a man of sixty-one was not asked for anything and asked nothing, and nothing was put on that bench and read as a gift. He was in that harbor all day and went up no coast road on any day of these ten, and neither brother has said why. Mara Venn is in that harbor and going nowhere on any day of these ten, of no office, not on the crew, and her forty marks stand first after food and are not reduced and are not paid.**

---

The chandler's wife was at her own counter at about ten in the morning on the sixth of June, counting candles into a box for a woman from two streets over, and the man of about thirty-six of the third lay came past the shop with an empty basket and did not come in.

She watched him go past, and she finished counting, and she put the box on the counter, and she said one thing to the woman from two streets over that was not about candles, and the woman carried it down the street with her.

"He goes past every morning now," she said. "He goes past with a basket and he does not come in, and I do not call him in, and my husband does not call him in. He has a boat of about thirty tons lying off that mole with eleven weeks of a season on her, and he has not been out since the second of June, when he shifted nine tons of salt inside the line, and he will buy no candles at this counter."

The woman from two streets over asked whether the man had quarreled with the shop, and the chandler's wife said he had said a sentence in a market with her trade in the first four words of it, and that her husband is not going to sell that man candles again, and that a candle at that harbor is fourpence and has been for as long as she has been in that shop.

"It is not the fourpence," she said. "A man in a wheelhouse burns one a night in this weather, and that is about sixty of them between the twenty-ninth of May and the end of June, and that is four shillings, and four shillings is not a hardship and he knows it is not a hardship. It is the nought. It is a price of fourpence and it is going to be a price of nought, and it is the nought that sits on that counter every morning when he goes past."

Nessa Pike heard that at the end of the street, where she had gone to buy nails, and she wrote it down in the small book she carries, and in the shed that night it went in the second column, because a thing a woman says at her own counter about her own trade is a thing nobody in this yard can check from a mole, and the reason is that this yard was not in the shop.

---

In the afternoon the man of about thirty-six came up the mole to look at his boat lying off, and Calder Marr was mending a warp on the crown, and the two men stood about twenty feet apart and looked at the same water.

The man is of the third lay, and he has already hired this yard's boat once, on the thirty-first of May, and gone out on the second of June with nine tons of salt and nothing happening, and he named a trade in a market on the twenty-ninth of May and bought no candles since, and he has eleven weeks of a season on his own boat and has not been out in her since the seventeenth of May.

"You are not out," said Calder Marr, because the man was standing there and it would have been strange to say nothing.

"I am not," said the man of about thirty-six. "There is nothing to go out for. The third lay fills on the first of April and one berth is short, and there are seven boats lying off where there were eleven on the twenty-fourth of May, and not one of them has passed the line. I have eleven weeks of a season and I am spending them standing on a mole."

"You shifted salt on the second," said Calder Marr.

"I did," said the man. "Nine tons, inside the line, on your boat, for a mark and eightpence. And I was not asked for a declaration and was not stopped and was not followed, and I came back in on the third. That is a Tuesday on which nothing happened, and your ship's hand says there are two of those now, and she says she has no column for what it costs to be one."

He picked up a stone and threw it down the slope toward the water, and it rattled and stopped.

"I hear there are old books in your shed," he said.

"There are four," said Calder Marr. "They came with boats this yard broke up. They are in a drawer. They have not been opened past the first page."

"My father kept a book," said the man of about thirty-six. "He kept soundings in it that no chart has. He spelled the same rock three ways in three years and my mother used to laugh at him. He said a spelling is a year, and that is all a spelling is."

He said nothing else, and he went back down the mole, and Calder Marr wrote down that he had said it, and that this yard had asked him nothing and told him nothing, and that the man had not asked to see any book.

---

That night in the shed, the holder of that writ said the second price out loud, and it went in the first column beside the first one, before anybody had agreed to anything, because a price has to be named before anybody agrees to anything, and nobody has said yes.

"The first price is that the soundings are of a coast that has moved," he said. "It went in last night. The second price is this, and it goes in tonight. Any old route across that strait can be used only if a crew gives up a safer supply crossing, and the price of that is a named thing. The safer crossing is the Ternwatch road inside the line, the run this harbor lives on for flour and coal and oil, the run of about eleven tons to a lighter that a boat can do without passing any line. A crew that takes the strait gives up that road for the season. That is the figure. That is the named thing. It goes in before anybody says yes, and nobody has said yes, and nobody has been asked."

"Write that as what it is," said Nessa Pike.

"Write it as what it is," said Calder Marr, "and put the sixth of June against it, and no name in it, and do not have anybody agree to it in a room and do not have anybody refuse it in a room either."

The woman of no office looked up from the bench, where she was still working at the cuff.

"That is a hard price," she said.

"It is the price," said Calder Marr. "A price is a price. I am not going to make it softer by saying it in a shed. The coast has moved and the road has to be given up, and both of those go in before the first use and not after it, and there has been no first use."

---

---

*First column, entered on Sunday the sixth day of June next at about half past nine in the evening in this shed: that a man of about thirty-six of the third lay, with a boat of about thirty tons lying off that mole and eleven weeks of a season on her, went past a chandler's counter in the front street on the said morning with an empty basket and did not go in, and has bought no candles at fourpence at that counter since the twenty-ninth day of May past; and that any old route across Bramble Strait that may stand in any old vessel book can be used only if a crew gives up a safer supply crossing, being the Ternwatch road inside the line for flour and coal and oil, of about eleven tons to a lighter, for the season, and that this is the named price and goes in the first column before anybody says yes and nobody has said yes.*

*Second column, not checkable from a mole, entered on the said Sunday at about half past nine in the evening in this shed, and no name is against it: that the chandler's wife said out loud at her own counter on the said morning that the said man goes past every morning and does not come in, and that a candle is fourpence and about sixty of them between the twenty-ninth of May and the end of June is four shillings, and that it is a price of fourpence and is going to be a price of nought; and that the ship's hand heard it at the end of the street and wrote it in the small book she carries.*

*And that the said man of about thirty-six came up that mole in the afternoon and stood about twenty feet from the holder of that writ and said that his father kept a book with soundings in it that no chart has, and spelled the same rock three ways in three years, and said that a spelling is a year and that is all a spelling is; and that this yard asked him nothing and told him nothing and he asked to see no book; and that this yard did not go back to the copyist's counter in the room off the fish market on the said day and did not send anybody there.*

*And in the fourth column of the leaf ruled on the twenty-seventh day of March past, that night, in the ship's left hand, the forty-seventh line:*

*What it is not: — that a man going past a shop is not a quarrel and a counter shut to one man is not a villainy and has not been asked for by anybody; that a spelling is not a truth about a sea and three ways of spelling one rock in three years is a difference in a book and not a finding about a coast; and that a safer road given up is not a road taken, and no crew has given up anything and no crew has been asked.*

---

**Nothing came into this yard on the said Sunday and nothing went out of it, and there are twenty pence in the bag, being one shilling and eightpence, and the figure the bag is short of the wage of four marks is six hundred and twenty pence, being three marks, eleven shillings and eightpence, and it is not going to be paid and nobody is to write one word under it saying anybody is going to. The wall did not come down and the cistern was not charged. The far bench was occupied from ten until four and was not approached. The two objects of nobody's are three hundred and sixty days up the crown of that mole and the bench under that pump is bare for the hundred and twenty-sixth night, and neither of them has moved. The man of about thirty-six walked down that mole at about five with nothing in his hands, and the stone he threw at about three was still in the grass by the slip when the tide turned, and neither of those is a thing this yard is going to keep, and a man of sixty-one with a bandaged right hand worked on that crown and was not thanked and is not going to be.**

**Nothing in that shed was signed. Nobody in that yard was thanked. Nobody in that harbor got a penny out of it.**
