# Chapter 329

*Sunday the Thirteenth of June, About Forty People on a Mole's Crown, Four Minutes Looked At, and a Man of Sixty-One Who Refuses to Say Either of the Two Things He Could Say*

Sunday the thirteenth of June, on the crown of that mole in the afternoon, and in the shed on the mole in the evening.

**The said Sunday was not a wage Tuesday. The wall behind the reeve's bench did not come down on it and the cistern was not charged on it, the cistern standing on a rota of a Monday and a Thursday. Nothing came into this yard on the said day and nothing went out of it, and there are twenty pence in the bag, being one shilling and eightpence. The far bench was occupied on the said Sunday from about ten in the morning until about four in the afternoon and at no other hour of it.**

**And the holder of that writ was in that harbor on the said day and went up no coast road on any day of these ten, and neither brother has said why. Mara Venn is in that harbor and is going nowhere on any day of these ten, and is of no office, and is not on the crew, and her forty marks stand first after food and are not reduced and are not paid.**

---

About forty people came up to the crown of that mole on the thirteenth of June at about three in the afternoon, because the man of about thirty-six had said in the market on Saturday that his father's book would be on the crown on Sunday for any man to look at who wanted to, and the word had gone round the front street the way words go.

He laid the book open on a sailcloth on the stone, at the twine-marked page. Nessa Pike laid this yard's four books open beside it on a second sailcloth, at the pages with the soundings on them. Nobody's book touched anybody else's book. The five lay side by side in the sun, and about forty people stood round them in a ring, and nobody was asked for anything.

Tobias Wren was there because he works on that crown every day from ten until four, and the ring had formed round his bench, and he did not leave it. He is sixty-one, bandaged in the right hand, with a boat's hook in his left, eleven pieces of a boat behind him, hurt in the spring, not thanked and not going to be, not sent for, not asked, not made a teacher and not made a door and not made a sacrifice.

Calder Marr stood on the other side of the ring and did not go over to him at any hour of that afternoon. The holder of that writ does not go over to him, and did not then, and neither brother has said why.

What happened was given the opportunity, in front of about forty people, and nothing more. The man of about thirty-six stepped back from his father's book and said, out loud, so that all of them heard it:

"Any man who knows that water can look at these pages. They are open. I am not asking anybody. They are open."

Nobody moved for a while. Then Tobias Wren stepped forward two paces with the hook in his left hand, and stood over the five books, and looked at them for about four minutes, while about forty people watched and nobody said a word.

He looked at the *Morrow Lass* page first, then the *Gull's Rest*, then the *Tern's Debt*, then the stitched book, then the father's book last. He bent once, stiffly, to see a date at the foot of a page. He did not touch any page with either hand. At the end of about four minutes he straightened up and stepped back to his bench, and sat down on it, and put the hook across his knees.

He refused to say either of the two things he could say about it. Neither of the two things is named on any page of this work, and no later writer may name them. He said nothing that was either of them, and he said nothing that was near them.

What he said instead was one true thing, and he said it because he has seen it before and not because he is explaining it.

"That spelling," he said, pointing with his chin at the stitched book, where *Skorr Depe* stands under the date twenty-seven years past. "I have seen that water."

That was all. Eight words. He said nothing else that afternoon, and nobody asked him for anything else, because he may be looked at and he may not be asked, and he was looked at by about forty people and asked by none of them.

The ring stood for a while longer, and then people began to go down the hill in twos and threes, and the man of about thirty-six shut his father's book and picked it up, and Nessa Pike shut this yard's four books one at a time and carried them back to the shed, and Tobias Wren stayed on his bench with the hook across his knees until four o'clock, when he always goes.

---

In the shed that night, nobody was comforted by anybody, because there was nothing to be comforted about.

"He looked at the thing for about four minutes," said Nessa Pike, writing it down in the second column, "and he refused to say either of the two things he could say about it, and what he said instead is true because he has seen it before. That is the whole of it."

"That is the whole of it," said Calder Marr. "He was given the opportunity in front of about forty people and he refused, and he may refuse, and the hold stands and is not improved on. He is not this work's injury and not this work's sacrifice, and this yard knows the difference and has entered it and is not going to spend him."

"Then the route stands as it stands," said the woman of no office from the bench. "Five books holding one water, and a chart with nothing across it, and a man who has seen that water and will not say whether the soundings are any use. The soundings are of a coast that has moved. The safer road has to be given up. Nobody has said yes. Nobody has refused in a room."

"Nobody has said yes," said Calder Marr, "and nobody is going to be asked in a room. A route remembered across several vessels is checkable by going there, and a route that cannot be checked at all is the only kind that could get a blockade broken, and this yard has always said that the second kind is worth less than the first. That is the reversal, and it is on the page now, and it is not a refusal on the old reason, and it is not an acceptance for free, and no stage has been taken, and the Storm-Reader stage is taken in shared work with a crew at a cost and not before."

"Write that as what it is," said Nessa Pike.

"Write it as what it is," said Calder Marr, "and put the thirteenth of June against it, and no name in it."

She wrote it, and the pencil moved slowly across the page in her left hand, at about twice the time it takes her with the other, and the four stitches in the right hand showed white where the skin pulls.

When it was done she looked at the entry for a long time, and then she said the thing that neither of them had said on the crown, because on the crown about forty people had been watching and in the shed there were three.

"He pointed with his chin," she said. "At the stitched book. At Skorr Depe, twenty-seven years past. He bent to see the date. He knows that hand, or he knows that water, or he knows both. And he gave us eight words and nothing more. I have seen that water. That is what a man says when he has been somewhere and is not going to say he has been there."

"That is what he said," said Calder Marr. "Because he has seen it before and not because he is explaining it. If he had wanted to explain, he could have said either of the two things he could say, and he refused both, and neither is named on any page, and no later writer may name them. That is the hold, and it is not improved on, and he is not a teacher and not a door and not a sacrifice."

The woman of no office folded the mending she had finished and laid it on the bench, and said that forty people had watched a man look at pages for four minutes and heard eight words, and that no person in that ring had asked for a ninth word, and that this harbor had done one thing right in ten days.

*First column, entered on Sunday the thirteenth day of June next at about half past nine in the evening in this shed: that about forty people stood on the crown of that mole at about three in the afternoon of the said day round five open books on two sailcloths, being this yard's four old vessel books and the father's book of a man of about thirty-six of the third lay, holding one water of Bramble Strait under ten spellings, and that a man of sixty-one at a far bench stepped forward and looked at the said pages for about four minutes and touched none of them, and refused to say either of the two things he could say about it, and said instead one true thing because he has seen it before; and that nobody has agreed to any use of the said route and nobody has refused any in a room.*

*Second column, not checkable from a mole, entered on the said Sunday at about half past nine in the evening in this shed, and no name is against it: that the said man is sixty-one, bandaged in the right hand, hurt in the spring, in eleven pieces of a boat on that crown, not thanked and not going to be, not sent for, not asked, not made a teacher and not made a door and not made a sacrifice; and that the holder of that writ did not go over to him at any hour of the said day and was in that harbor on the said day and went up no coast road, and neither brother has said why; and that neither of the two things the said man could say is named on any page of this work.*

*And that this yard did not go back to the copyist's counter in the room off the fish market on the said day and did not send anybody there; and that no stage was taken in this shed on the said day and the Storm-Reader stage is taken in shared work with a crew at a cost and not before and not as a solution to a problem of police or price.*

*And in the fourth column of the leaf ruled on the twenty-seventh day of March past, that night, in the ship's left hand, the fifty-fourth line:*

*What it is not: — that a man looked at for four minutes by forty people is not a man asked and not a man thanked; that one true thing said because a man has seen a water before is not an explaining of it; and that a yard handed the best record it has ever been offered on condition it stops checking it has not refused it and has not accepted it for free, and no man at a far bench has made it safe by saying anything.*

---

**Nothing came into this yard on the said Sunday and nothing went out of it, and there are twenty pence in the bag, being one shilling and eightpence, and the figure the bag is short of the wage of four marks, being six hundred and forty pence, is six hundred and twenty pence, being three marks, eleven shillings and eightpence, and it is not going to be paid and nobody is to write one word under it saying anybody is going to. The wall did not come down and the cistern was not charged. The far bench was occupied from ten until four and the holder of that writ did not go over to the man on it at any hour of the said day. The two objects of nobody's are three hundred and sixty-seven days up the crown of that mole and the bench under that pump is bare for the hundred and thirty-third night, and neither of them has moved.**

**Nothing in that shed was signed. Nobody in that yard was thanked. Nobody in that harbor got a penny out of it.**
