# Chapter 209

*A Letter With No Threat in It, and the One Place a Man in a City Can Look*

Tuesday the twenty-second and Wednesday the twenty-third of December, in the shed, and on the mole.

**On Tuesday the twenty-second of December the wage of four marks did not go in, and the envelope was not made, and the number of weeks fallen due this year with no envelope standing against them is now NINE, being the fifteenth day of August past, the fifth of September past, the sixth of October past, the third of November past, the seventeenth of November past, the first of December past, the eighth of December instant, the fifteenth of December instant, and the twenty-second of December instant, and all nine are entered in this book in the same breath and not one after the other, because a yard which is asked how many weeks it has paid has to say all nine at once rather than the one that is easier.**

The wage column is where it was in August and it is not going anywhere before the first of March: twenty-six lines, one hundred and four marks, the fifth of March the oldest. Twenty envelopes. The bag is seven marks, six shillings and four pence, which is one thousand one hundred and ninety-six pence, and a wage of four marks is six hundred and forty of those, so the bag is short of it by five hundred and fifty-six pence, and that figure is on the page and will be on the page every Tuesday until it is not.

**And the board in that shed has nothing in it for five days after this one, and the party is one and a half until the first of March, and the two of them entered on the Tuesday night that a board with nothing in it for five days is a figure anybody can add up, and that a figure anybody can add up is the first one this year that this yard has not been able to stop happening.**

---

**And at about ten on the Wednesday morning one sheet came up this hill in the ordinary post, and it is one sheet and it is not thin and it is dated the twentieth instant, and it came in on the twenty-third because that is what the post does between here and a town of about two thousand up this coast.**

It is a printer's letter. The hand is a printer's hand, which is a hand that has been taught to be even and has not been taught to hurry.

> *To the party of Morrow Quay whose acknowledgment is printed on a board in this harbor's market of the first day of December instant, and to anybody who has it, —*
>
> *I will write plainly because I am a printer and this is my trade and not a favor, and if it is a favor then it is not a favor, because I am not asking you for anything.*
>
> *Here is what I have. On the sixth day of December instant a man who sells ice up this coast bought a sheet at a counter off the fish market in Morrow Quay. He paid a penny for it. He put it in a box of fish and he sent it up this coast to me and it came in my hand on the ninth. It is a half sheet. It has the name of an office at the foot of it and no man's name anywhere on it. I keep a list of everything that comes into my shop that anybody means to keep, because in twenty-two years I have been wrong about that twice and I was wrong about it expensively both times, and the sheet is on that list with the day and the price and the name of the man who brought it, and the man's name is on the list because a man is a person and I can ask him things.*
>
> *Here is what has happened since. On the ninth day of December a man came to my counter and bought type. That is all he did. He is not an office and he is not a lawyer and he is a man buying type. He asked me whether I could say where a copy of a record was, that a copy had been made in Morrow Quay and acknowledged on the twenty-ninth of November, and that the acknowledgment is in my hand-list. I said I could not say. He did not argue and he did not press me and he was not rude, and he wrote down the name of the town the sheet came from and he wrote down the day and he said thank you and he went out and bought his type.*
>
> *So I have a line in my own book with a town on it and no address on it, and a man in a city has a line in his own book with my shop on it, and one of those two lines is going to be put in a letter inside a fortnight and I would rather it went to you from me than find you out by asking a man who sells fish.*
>
> *Here is my question, and it is one question and I am not dressing it up. Where is the copy of the record.*
>
> *I am not asking you to send it. I am not asking for the sheet. I am not asking you to be examined about it or licensed or enrolled or paid, and I am not asking on behalf of anybody, and if a man from a city comes up your hill in January you will know a printer's hand from the shape of it.*
>
> *And one more thing, because I would rather say it than have you wonder. I am not going to write to you again until you write to me. And I am not going to put this letter in anybody's book but my own, and if it is in mine on the day it is in somebody else's, then somebody else has been into my shop, and there is a lock on that drawer and it is a good lock and it is a lock and not a promise.*
>
> *Jonas Peake, printer. This is my trade and not my office.*

---

**And the sheet went onto the bench in that shed at about a quarter to eleven on the Wednesday morning and lay there beside a form with no date on it, and neither of them read anything else out loud that morning, and the acknowledgment of the twenty-ninth of November was taken off the shelf and put down flat on the bench with the other two, and the three of them lay there in a line about a foot apart for about four minutes and nobody said anything.**

Then Calder Marr said: "**Read me the fourth sentence of the printer's letter.**"

Nessa Pike found it and read it. "**A sheet with the name of an office at the foot of it and no man's name anywhere on it is on my list with the day and the price and the name of the man who brought it.**"

"**Now read me the last thing the woman at the counter said on Friday.**"

"**That a sheet with an office's name at the foot of it and no man's name on it anywhere is the easiest thing in the world for a copyist to sell, because there is nobody on it for her to ask where it came from, and that those go out of a shop and into somebody's buyer's book and nobody ever knows.**"

"**She said that about sheets in general.**"

"**She said that about sheets in general and she said it on Friday and she asked us to notice that she had not said a word about ours, and we wrote her sentence down in our own hand on Saturday night, and I have read it about four times since and I did not see it until this morning.**"

Neither of them said anything for a while. Out past the door it was raining on the mole in the way it does in December, which is not much and does not stop.

"**Then say what it is,**" said Nessa Pike, "**because you have got that face on and I do not want to be the one who says it.**

"**It is this,**" said Calder Marr. "**This yard did not write that acknowledgment. This yard did not pay for it. This yard did not ask for it. This yard did not allow it — a woman of fifty-eight took it up in a market of her own account on the thirtieth of November and she told us to keep away and we kept away. And it is the single most useful page in this country for a man in a city who wants to know where we are.

"**It has an office's name on it and no man's name on it. So it can be bought at a penny by anybody and resold to anybody and there is nobody on the paper for a copyist to ask. And it was read out in about forty people's hearing in this market on the first of December, and a man of the first lay bought four copies and put them inside a coat, and one of them went up a coast road in a box of fish, and a printer kept it because his trade keeps things, and a man buying type asked about it, and that is the whole of how this yard's name got onto a line in a printer's book in a town of about two thousand and onto a line in a man's book in a city two hundred miles from here.

"**And there is no office on the coast that could have stopped that, and no clerk, and no form, and no clause. The instrument was a sheet with a name at the foot of it and nothing else, and it did the whole of the work in nine days.**"

"**And the copy of the record is in this shed,**" said Nessa Pike.

"**And the copy of the record is in this shed.**

"**Both of those are true and neither of them cancels the other.** This yard asked for that copy in its own left hand and put a penny in the post to get it, and this yard wants it, and this yard is the reason there is a line in a printer's book with a town on it and a man in a city who is going to be asked a question about it. And I am not going to stand in this shed and tell you the first of those is the real one, and I am not going to stand in a market in four years and say we did not know."

"**Then are we going to answer him.**"

"**No.**"

"**No,**" said Calder Marr, "**and I am going to say the whole of why, and it is one sentence and I have been standing here since the letter came off the hill building it.

"**A reply is a document with this yard's name at the foot of it. That is the whole of the instrument. If I write to that printer then this shed has an address that it made itself, on our own paper, in our own hand, dated, and the office whose name is on the foot of the form on that bench will find that in about four years in about nine seconds, and everything the printer has done will be done twice, and the second time it will be our fault instead of theirs.

"**A copy which has been made cannot be unmade. And an acknowledgment with an office's name at the foot of it and no signature on it is the one place in this country where a man in a city can look to find out where this yard is. That is what that page is. It is not a threat and it is not a lie and it is not anybody's fault, and it is ours, and we made it, and we made it on purpose, in July, because a record a man can check is the only kind worth having.

"**So: no reply. And the reason goes in the first column in my own hand tonight, and it goes in the first column and not the second, because a man in this harbor can come up this hill on any day of a week and look at that acknowledgment on this bench and see the name of an office at the foot of it and no man's name on it anywhere, and can go down to a counter off the fish market and ask a woman of fifty-eight whether she read it out and in what market and on what day, and can go to the post and ask on what day a letter came up this road on Friday last. All three of those are checkable by any man who can get off a mole. I have never had a fact I could stand on so square. And I am not going to improve on it, and I am not going to argue with it, and nobody in this shed is going to tell me it is not true.**"

"**Write it,**" said Nessa Pike, "**and put the printer's letter in the same drawer as the form and not in the same place, because they are two different instruments and one of them has a question in it and one of them has a hole cut in it, and a man in a city wants the first and does not care a button about the second.**

"**And do not tell the harbor authority. Do not tell the clerk. Do not tell the copyist, and she will be selling sheets on Monday and she is not part of this and she told us so herself.**"
