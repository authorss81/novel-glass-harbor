# Chapter 247

*Five Hands, and Why the Thing Has No Name on It*

Monday the eighth and Tuesday the ninth of March, in the shed, and at a counter in a room off the fish market.

**At eight in the morning on the said Monday the wall behind the reeve's bench came down and it came down bare, and that was the forty-fourth Monday since the first of May past, and the counter did not restart, and the clerk kept his own page and did not mention it, and no sitting was held in that room on that day or on any other day of this book.**

**And on the said Monday the cistern behind the engine house was charged at no figure by four men this yard does not know and this party walked none of it, and that is the fourth of the six rota days that this party has entered walked on none of since the twenty-fourth day of February past, and the walk is nil and it stays nil, and that is the twenty-seventh of the charges this party has entered walked on none of since the third day of August past.**

**And the far bench was occupied on the said Monday from about ten until about four and was not occupied on the said Tuesday at any hour, and this party entered both of them and set nothing forward to either line, and the holder of that writ is up that coast road and did not go over to him on either of the said two days.**

**And no day of work was booked on the said Monday and no day of work was booked on the said Tuesday and the board was not written on either of them, and the reason against the first of those two lines is that a Monday is the day the wall comes down and the cistern is charged and nothing is worked in this harbor on a Monday by this party, and the reason against the second of them is that the holder of that writ is not in that harbor.**

**And the said Tuesday was a wage Tuesday, and a wage of four marks, being six hundred and forty pence, was not paid on it, and no envelope was made, and the column stands at twenty-six lines and one hundred and four marks with the fifth of March the oldest and did not move, and the count of envelopes on that bench in that chest stands at twenty and did not move, and the reason is the reason entered on the twenty-ninth of December past in the holder's own left hand and it is not improved on.**

---

**And at about a quarter to nine on the said Monday morning the ship's hand went down that hill to a counter in a room off the fish market, and a counter in that room is open to anybody from eight until the market is off the boards, and a copyist of fifty-eight kept that counter for thirty-one years and has no office above it and is not of this party and is not on a ladder and is not going to be asked about a page in her own back room, and this party asked her the other question.**

"**We have put a page in a drawer in this town on Saturday afternoon and it is in my hand and I am the person who keeps it, and four sheets went in the post on Saturday to four places and we shall not know in four years whether three of them are in a drawer or on a fire.**

"**And we want to know one thing and we are going to ask it before we start, and then you are going to say no to it, and then we are going to stop.**

"**The name.**"

"**For what.**

"**For what we are about to do.**"

Pernel Saye put her pen down on the counter and did not take her hand off it.

"**I have kept this counter for thirty-one years,**" she said, "**and I have put a name on about nine things in that time, and every one of them had a name somebody had already given it, and I wrote it down, and that is what my trade is.

"**And a name is the last thing a thing gets and the first thing it can be asked for, and I do not know a single thing in this country that is called anything by anybody that cannot be asked about by somebody with a form. A name is a thing an office can be asked for, and there is no office to ask. That is the whole reason and there is not a second reason behind it. You cannot go to a town and say *I am from an office and show me yours*, because there is no office and you are not from one. If you name it, then in about four years some clerk in some city will write it on the back of a sheet, and then there will be a file, and then a question about whether it still stands, and then somebody will want to know who holds up its end, and you have spent five volumes teaching that coast that a thing with a name on it can be asked about. So it does not get one.

"**And I will say the rest of it because you came in at a quarter to nine and the market is off the boards at ten on a Monday and I have not got anybody to ask.**"

---

"**Then say the part about the limit,**" said Nessa Pike, "**because it is not ours and you are not us and it is in our first column and it stands. I have had it said to me out loud once and I am not going to say it out loud again in a room I chose.**

"**Four people on a mole in August wrote down, in their own words, that they will not say what a bell-archive is to a man with a seal, and they signed it, and that is the only reason anything you have done in five volumes has a floor under it, and it is not yours. *We will not say it in a shed. We will not say it into a book that goes out of this harbor, and we will not say it to a man with a seal, and we will not say it again in a market.* A page about a place is the place's and is not to be carried out of the place it is about.

"**That is a wall with no door in it. You have spent five volumes saying a door is the only thing that makes a wall worth building, and you are right, and here is the other half: a wall with no door in it is the only kind four people of this coast can stand behind, because there is no office on this coast that can put a door in it. They gave you that for nothing in August without knowing what it was worth, and it is the only thing in this business nobody in it had to be paid for.

"**And now the price, because you are not leaving here without it. A page in a drawer in five towns is a page nobody can check. A record a man can check is worth what the man who checks it is worth; a record nobody can check is worth what the person who keeps it is worth; and there is no figure for either and there never has been. I have met about nine people in thirty-one years I would trust with a page and about four hundred I would not, and the nine are not better than the four hundred.

"**You have spent five volumes telling this harbor that the only thing that makes a page worth having is that somebody can be asked a question about it, and you have just built five places where nobody can be asked a question about anything. I am not going to tell you that is a contradiction, because it is not one, and I am not going to tell you it is a good trade, because I do not know and have never known. It is worth what the four of them are worth, and that is the price, and it is not a figure and you cannot make it one. I have a copy in a back room I made nine years ago for a man who did not come back, and I have never looked at it, and I told two strangers about it in February and would not have told one, and I am not going to be asked about it again, and I am not going to ask you about your four envelopes either.**"

---

**And at about half past seven on the said Monday night, in that shed, with the lamp lit and the lamp turned round, five people wrote five things on one sheet of paper, and one of the five was not in the room, and the other four were, and the holder of the writ's hand is not on it and his name is not on it and he is up a coast road and is not going to be asked to sign it when he comes back.**

The ship's hand wrote the first line. The man of about thirty-four who sells lime and chalk was not in the room and wrote the second and he is in the country and not on this coast and cannot be in this room and wrote it in this harbor on the Wednesday before he went, and his name is not on it and the reason is entered. A man of about sixty of this town who keeps a day-book of his own that his father began, and who was in the room, wrote the third. A woman of about thirty-one of this town who has kept a page of her own since she was nineteen, and who was in the room and who was not asked a question about it, wrote the fourth. The copyist of fifty-eight wrote the fifth, at about half past seven, and she came up that hill for nothing and was not paid and did not ask.

The five lines are five and they are not numbered and there is no heading and the sheet has nothing at the top of it, and the five of them are these, and they are in five hands and each hand is in a town:

*That a page about this place is in this place's hand on this place's paper, and is kept by a person, and is not a copy of a record and is not a record and is not a document and is not evidence.*

*That any person the page is about may read it, and nobody else may be told it exists, and the person who keeps it may say yes or no at any time to any one of them and cannot be made to say yes.*

*That nobody may be asked for it, in a shed, in a market, in a room, or to a man with a seal, and that a party which does not produce it is not fined, because a court does not fine a poor man, and the matter simply goes on.*

*That a person may be asked to take a page out of a drawer, in their own town, in their own words, and may say no, and that nobody may be asked to take it out twice.*

*That these five towns are parties and that there are five pages and one page to a drawer, and that nobody has to add a name to the thing for it to be a thing.*

"**Read them back,**" said Nessa Pike, "**and read back after her whoever is holding the pencil, and the man of about sixty will read them twice because he is sixty, and then do not put a name on it.

"**And write under the five of them the day, and the five towns, and the five hands, in the ship's hand. The reason there is no name on the top goes in the second column and not the first, because a man on a mole can check the five lines and cannot check the reason there is no name on them, and in about four years somebody is going to read those five lines and say *that is not an agreement, that is five people being kind to each other,* and the answer to that is a counter off a fish market and it is not going to be improved on.**"

*First column, entered on Tuesday the ninth day of March instant at about half past nine in the evening in this shed: that at eight in the morning on the said Monday the wall behind the reeve's bench came down and came down bare, and that that was the forty-fourth Monday since the first of May past, and that the counter did not restart, and that the clerk kept his own page and did not mention it, and that no sitting was held in that room on that day or on any other day of this book.*

*And that on the said Monday the cistern behind the engine house was charged at no figure by four men this yard does not know and that party walked none of it, and that the walk is nil and stays nil, and that that is the fourth of the six rota days that party has entered walked on none of since the twenty-fourth day of February past, and that that is the twenty-seventh of the charges that party has entered walked on none of since the third day of August past.*

*And that no day of work was booked on the said Monday and no day of work was booked on the said Tuesday and the board was not written on either of them; and that the reason against the first of those lines is that a Monday is the day the wall comes down and the cistern is charged; and that the reason against the second of them is that the holder of that writ is not in that harbor; and that there is no figure against either of them.*

*And that the said Tuesday was a wage Tuesday and a wage of four marks, being six hundred and forty pence, was not paid on it and no envelope was made; and that the column stands at twenty-six lines and one hundred and four marks with the fifth of March the oldest and did not move; and that the count of envelopes on that bench in that chest stands at twenty and did not move; and that the number of weeks fallen due this year with no envelope standing against them is twenty, being the fifteenth day of August past, the fifth of September past, the sixth of October past, the third of November past, the seventeenth of November past, the first of December past, the eighth, the fifteenth, the twenty-second and the twenty-ninth of December past, the fifth, the twelfth, the nineteenth and the twenty-sixth of January instant, the second, the ninth, the sixteenth and the twenty-third of February instant, the second and the ninth of March instant, and that all twenty are entered in that book in the same breath and not one after the other.*

*And that at about a quarter to nine on the said Monday morning the said woman went to a counter in a room off that fish market and asked a copyist of fifty-eight of that counter, and of no office above it, and not of that party, one question, being what the thing they are about to make should be called.*

*And that the said copyist said that she has kept that counter for thirty-one years and has put a name on about nine things in that time, and that every one of them had a name somebody had already given it, and that a name is the last thing a thing gets and the first thing it can be asked for, and that she does not know a single thing in that country that is called anything by anybody that cannot be asked about by somebody with a form; and that a name is a thing an office can be asked for, and that there is no office to ask, and that that is the whole reason and there is not a second reason behind it; and that if the said party names it then in about four years some clerk in some city will write the name on the back of a sheet, and then there will be a file, and then there will be a question about whether it still stands, and then somebody will want to know who holds up its end, and that that party has spent five volumes teaching that coast that a thing with a name on it can be asked about, and that so it does not get one.*

*And that the said copyist said that she answered because the said party asked her the other question first, and that she would have told them to go if they had asked the name first, and that she wanted that in that yard's book and not in hers.*

*And that the said copyist said, and this is entered in that first column because it is a thing a man on a mole can go and ask her about on any day of a week, that four people on a mole in the month of August wrote down in their own words that they will not say what a bell-archive is to a man with a seal, and signed it, and that that is the only reason anything that yard has done in five volumes has a floor under it, and that it is not that yard's; and that a wall with no door in it is the only kind of wall that four people of that coast can stand behind, because there is no office on that coast that can put a door in it; and that the four of them gave it for nothing in the month of August and gave it without knowing what it was worth, and that that is the only thing in that business that nobody in it had to be paid for.*

*And that the said copyist said that a page in a drawer in five towns is a page nobody can check, and that a record a man can check is worth what the man who checks it is worth and a record nobody can check is worth what the person who keeps it is worth, and that there is no figure for either and there never has been, and that she has met about nine people in thirty-one years she would trust with a page and about four hundred she would not, and that the nine are not better than the four hundred; and that she is not going to tell that party that it is a contradiction and is not going to tell it that it is a good trade, and that it is worth what the four of them are worth, and that that is the price and it is not a figure and cannot be made one.*

*And that the said copyist said that she has a copy in a back room that she made nine years ago for a man who did not come back, and that she has never looked at it and does not know what is on it, and that she told two strangers about it on the thirteenth of February past and would not have told one, and that she is not going to be asked about it again and is not going to ask about that party's four envelopes either; and that that yard did not ask her about it and is not going to.*

*And that at about half past seven on the said Monday night, in that shed, five people wrote five things on one sheet of paper, and that one of the five was not in that room and the other four were, and that the holder of that writ's hand is not on the said sheet and his name is not on it and he is up that coast road and is not going to be asked to sign it when he comes back.*

*And that the said five lines are five and are not numbered and have no heading, and the said sheet has nothing at the top of it, and the said five lines were written in five hands and each hand is in a town, and that the said woman wrote the first, and that a man of about thirty-four who sells lime and chalk wrote the second in that town on the Wednesday before he went down that coast and is in the country and not on that coast and cannot be in that room; and that a man of about sixty of that town who keeps a day-book of his own that his father began wrote the third; and that a woman of about thirty-one of that town who has kept a page of her own since she was nineteen wrote the fourth and was not asked a question about it; and that the said copyist wrote the fifth at about half past seven, and that she came up that hill for nothing and was not paid and did not ask.*

*And that the said five lines were read back from the top of that bench by the said woman, read back after her by the man of about sixty, read back twice more by the man of about sixty, and then read back by the said woman to herself, and that no name was put on it, and that the day, the five towns and the five hands were written under the five of them in the said woman's hand, and that the reason that there is no name on the top of it is in the second column and is not going to be improved on.*

*Second column, not checkable from a mole, entered on Tuesday the ninth day of March instant at about half past nine in the evening in this shed, and no name is against it: that the reason that thing has no name on it is that a name is a thing an office can be asked for, and that there is no office to ask, and that the answer to that is a counter in a room off that fish market and not an office and not a person with a title, and that in about four years a man will read those five lines and say that that is not an agreement but five people being kind to each other, and that he will be wrong, and that he will not be able to be shown.*

*And that a page in a drawer in five towns is a page no man in that harbor can look at, and that that yard has spent five volumes insisting that a page a man cannot check is worth less than a page a man can look at, and that that yard knows it, and has entered it, and is not going to get out of it with a better sentence, and that the price of the whole of this is paid here and not in a speech.*

*And that the four lines above the said named line are the four of that printed sheet and are still four of them, and are still nameless, and are not struck out, and are in about sixty boats on that coast; and that a request to strike a line out of a column is refused in the same words it is refused for a line in any column, and that a line does not come out of a column because it has been printed and cannot be got back.*

**Nothing came into this yard on the said Monday and nothing came into it on the said Tuesday, and nothing went out of it on either of the two days, and sixty-four marks, three shillings and ten pence is in the bag, which is ten thousand two hundred and eighty-six pence, and the figure the bag is short of the wage of four marks is nine thousand six hundred and forty-six pence. The whole of what came into that yard in the twenty days ended on the said Tuesday is five days at the face of the second seam, being two thousand four hundred pence, and the whole of what went out of that yard in the twenty days ended on the said Tuesday is fourpence at the post on the sixth of March, and the whole of what has gone out of that yard since the sixth of December past is two shillings and seven pence in five transactions at three counters. Twenty-six lines and one hundred and four marks in the wage column, the fifth of March the oldest, twenty envelopes on that bench, and twenty weeks fallen due with no envelope standing against them, being the fifteenth day of August past, the fifth of September past, the sixth of October past, the third of November past, the seventeenth of November past, the first of December past, the eighth, the fifteenth, the twenty-second and the twenty-ninth of December past, the fifth, the twelfth, the nineteenth and the twenty-sixth of January instant, the second, the ninth, the sixteenth and the twenty-third of February instant, the second and the ninth of March instant, and all twenty are in that book in the same breath and not one after the other. The chandler's note stands at thirty-one and is not re-written and its three counts of weeks stand at twenty-two and thirteen and eleven. The rest of that standing position, and every figure in it, is on the page above, and none of it has moved.**
