# Chapter 218

*A Man With a Firebox and a Key, and a No That Was Read as Something Else*

Saturday the ninth and Sunday the tenth of January, at the head of this dock, and in the shed.

**No day of work was booked on the said Saturday and none on the said Sunday, and the board was not written on either of them, and this party entered that with the reason against the line, which is that a day on which nothing happens still happened, and that the said Saturday is a day on which this yard was asked a question in the open in about eleven people's hearing and gave an answer in the open, and that a question and an answer in the open are two things that happened and neither of them is work.**

---

**The man had come up this hill on the Friday before last, at about nine in the morning, with a cart, and had put the cart down at the top of the mole and had not come up, and had sent a boy up with a card.**

The card said that a dry room with a firebox and a lock was kept above a chandler's on the north side of this town, and that a shelf was kept in it, and that the shelf was free.

He is sixty-eight. He has kept that room since he was thirty-one. He has kept other people's things in it — a chest of a widow's, a bundle of a man's instruments, a box of a family that went to the next town and did not want to pay the carriage — and he has never charged anybody for the use of the shelf and he does not know that that is unusual. He said his name and he gave his trade, which is a trade, and there is no office in it.

"**I am not asking you for anything,**" he said, at the head of this dock, on the Saturday morning, with about eleven people standing there and the market gone in. "**I am sixty-eight and I have got a dry floor and a firebox and a key, and I have thought about this for three weeks, and I will say why I have thought about it for three weeks: because there is a thing in this town that a man in a city would give a great deal to have a copy of, and because you have not got a place to put it, and because I have got a place and I am going out of it in the spring.**

"**I am not going to be responsible for a piece of paper in the spring. That is the whole of it. I am sixty-eight and I have a room and I have nobody coming after me and the room goes to a man who buys chandlers, and he will not know what your paper is and he will put it in a sack.**

"**I am not asking you to sign anything. I am not asking you to tell me what is on it. I am not asking you to be examined about it, and I do not know what that means and I do not want to know. I am asking you for one shelf and a key, and I will give you a second key, and you will not need it.**"

Nobody in the street said anything for about four seconds.

Then Calder Marr said: "**No.**"

"**I have asked it fair and I will not argue with you about it. I would have said it was a foolish thing to offer if I were not sixty-eight.**"

"**You offered it fair and I am going to refuse it in the open, and I am going to give you the reason in the open, and there is going to be about eleven people in this street who hear the reason and that is the price of saying it and I am paying it.**"

"**Then say it.**"

"**A shelf in your room is not a place to keep a record. It is a favor, and a favor is a thing with a man at the end of it. And the whole of what this yard has said for four volumes is that a thing with a man at the end of it is a thing that can be got at by anybody who can be pleasant to that man, and there is a man in a city who is extremely pleasant.**"

"**That is fair,**" said the man of sixty-eight. "**I would not have said it so tidy. I would have said: I have a dry room.**

"**You have a dry room and the reason is the same and I am not going to argue with a man of sixty-eight about which word is the better one.**

"**And I am going to say one more thing in this street because if I do not say it now it will be said about me in a month, and it is not a secret and it is not a threat: this yard has not got anywhere to put it. Not a room of yours, not a drawer, not a shelf in a shed. There is no such place in this harbor and this yard has not got one and is not going to make one in a fortnight.**"

"**Then I will not ask again,**" said the man of sixty-eight, "**and I will tell you what I will say when somebody asks me, because I would rather you heard it from me than heard it wrong. I will say: I asked, and they said no, and that is what happened. I am not going to say you are keeping it. I did not see you keep it.**"

He put his hat on and went down the mole and somebody carried his cart for him and he did not say thank you in a way anybody repeated afterwards.

---

**And two men of the first lay were standing at the head of that dock when the man of sixty-eight was saying that, and neither of them had been asked anything by anybody, and neither of them has been asked anything by anybody since, and this yard has not got either of their names and is not going up that hill for them.**

One of them said, to the other, and loud enough for a man standing four feet away to hear, which is the ordinary volume of a street in this harbor at ten in the morning:

"**Turned down a dry room. In this town. In this weather.**"

"**They will have somewhere.**

"**They have got somewhere. A man does not turn down a man's key unless he has a better one.**"

Nessa Pike said, to the holder, quietly, and not to the street:

"**Do not say anything to them.**

"**I am not going to.**

"**You are not going to, and I am not going to, and we have not got their names and we are not going up that hill, and that is now three days out of five weeks on which two men have said a thing about this yard at the head of that dock and this party has gone up on none of them.**"

"**The third of those days was not out of nothing,**" said Calder Marr. "**A man asked a question in front of eleven people and we said no in front of eleven people, and they were standing there. You cannot have a refusal in a street in front of witnesses and then not have a witness.**

"**I know. Write it exactly like that and do not write it better than that.**"

---

**And at about half past eight on the Sunday night, in the shed, with the lamp lit, and the two of them, and nothing read back, because there is nothing in that shed to read back that has not been in it for a month.**

Nessa Pike put her hand flat on the bench.

"**Say it to me,**" she said, "**the thing you cannot say in a street, and say it once, and then it goes in the book and it does not come out of the book again this winter.**

"**I cannot say it in a street because there is no sentence of it that is true and safe. If I say we have nowhere to put it, I am telling eleven people that this yard means to keep it. If I say we have somewhere to put it, that is a lie and you would know it was a lie before I had finished the sentence. If I say we will give it up, that is a lie of the other kind and it is the one that hurts. Those are the three things a man can say in a street and every one of them is wrong.**

"**And none of that is the difficulty. The difficulty is the other one.**

"**Both of those are true and neither of them cancels the other,**" said Nessa Pike. "**It is true that this yard is not holding anybody's record and it is true that this yard is not going to hold anybody's record, and it is also true that a yard which cannot say which of the two it is, in about eleven people's hearing, on a Saturday, is a yard a man in a city can write a line about. And the line he writes will be the second of those and it will be right and it will be wrong at the same time and there is nothing anybody in this street can do about it.**

"**And the reason I cannot correct it in a street is not the four people of this coast. I want that said, because that is what you are going to write in the book and it is not what I am thinking about. If I say why I cannot say it, I put four people in a street. So I am not going to say why, and I am not going to correct it, and the reason I am not going to is that I do not have a reason that does not cost somebody, and I have looked for one for two days.**

"**What is the reason, then.**

"**That the only way to be read correctly is to have somewhere to put it that is not a hold. And we have not got one. And we are not going to have one in a fortnight. And a man of sixty-eight with a firebox was the best that anybody in this harbor could offer us and he was sixty-eight and he is going out of it in the spring, and if I had said that in the street, the whole of this town would have worked out in about a week that this yard is looking for a place to put a record, and a yard that is looking for a place to put a record is a yard that means to have it.**"

Nobody said anything for a while. The pump was under the sheet and a half with no signature and no date on it, and it has been there since the ninth of December past.

"**So the answer is no, and the answer stays no,**" she said, "**and the reason the answer stays no is not a reason, it is a hole, and a hole in a street fills itself.**"

*Second column, not checkable from a mole, entered on Sunday the tenth day of January instant at about half past nine in the evening in this shed, and no name is against it: that the holder said in that shed, once, the three sentences a man can say in a street about a record and why every one of them is wrong, and that if he says this yard has nowhere to put it then he is telling about eleven people that this yard means to keep it, and that if he says this yard has somewhere to put it then that is a lie, and that if he says this yard will give it up then that is a lie of the other kind and is the one that hurts; and that the difficulty is not that one and is another, and that it is that a yard which cannot say which of the two things is true, in about eleven people's hearing, on a Saturday, is a yard a man in a city can write a line about, and that the line will be the second of the two and will be right and wrong at the same time and that nothing anybody in that street can do will alter it; and that the reason the holder did not correct it in the street is not the limit of four people of this coast, and that the said woman said that out loud in that shed and said that if the reason were that, four people would be in a street; and that the reason is that the holder does not have a reason that does not cost somebody, and that she looked for one for two days and did not find one, and that the reason against that line is not checkable from a mole and is not going to be given to anybody including the four people of this coast, who are not going to be asked; and that the four lines above the named line of the twentieth day of July past are still four and are still nameless and are not struck out, and that a request to strike a line out of a column is refused in the same words it is refused for a line in any column.*

*And that this party entered on the said day, in the first column and not the second, that the only way to be read correctly is to have somewhere to put it that is not a hold, and that this yard has not got one; and that the best that any person in this harbor could offer this party in the three weeks ended on the said Saturday was a shelf in a dry room with a firebox and a lock above a chandler's on the north side of this town, and that the man who offered it is sixty-eight and has kept that room since he was thirty-one and has never charged anybody for the use of the shelf, and that the room goes to a man who buys chandlers in the spring; and that this party did not accept that shelf, and that the reason it did not accept it is in the first column, and that the reason it cannot say, in a street, that it has not got a place, is in this column and is not checkable and is not going to be given; and that the reason this entry is in the first column is that any man in this town can come up this hill on any day of a week and ask the two of them whether they have got a place to put a page in, and be told they have not, and can go down to a chandler's on the north side and ask a man of sixty-eight whether he has offered it and be told that he has.*

**No money came into this yard on the ninth of January and none on the tenth, and nothing went out of it on either day, and thirteen marks, six shillings and four pence is in the bag, which is two thousand one hundred and fifty-six pence. A shelf in a dry room with a firebox and a lock, kept above a chandler's on the north side of this town, was offered to this yard in the open at the head of this dock on the ninth of January instant by a man of sixty-eight who keeps that room and has kept it since he was thirty-one, and the shelf was not taken, and the man has not been asked again and is not going to be, and the room goes to a man who buys chandlers in the spring and will not know what the paper is. The chandler's note is at thirty-one and has not been touched, and the three counts of weeks it carries stand at twenty-two and thirteen and eleven, and this yard said none of that out loud in any of these twenty days. That is the eighth month this shed has stood without anything in a tin, and the last thing that went into one went in on the twenty-eighth day of April. Three marks for the eighth of August past, owed to a man of Ashtide, standing. His name is not on this page and it is not going to be, and no one has been sent. There is seven pence owing to this yard's own shipwright, for a letter's postage, and it is not paid. Eight marks and a day of a boat to this harbor's own bellwright since the twenty-third of May past, still unargued and still unpaid, and he has not been asked anything and the chest has not been opened. This yard owes eight shillings and tenpence to a woman of this harbor, and her name is on the figure, and the figure has not been touched since the day she put it there. A boy of twenty-two is owed forty marks and every one of them is there; the three nots of the eighth of June past stand; the third column has his hand at the head of it and nobody's after it. Forty marks first after food to a person of no office, standing since the fifteenth of October past, with nothing paid and nothing offered; she told this yard not to ask her again this winter and this yard has not asked and did not ask her in any of these twenty days. The wage column is where it was in August at twenty-six lines and one hundred and four marks with the fifth of March the oldest, there are twenty envelopes on that bench, and eleven weeks stand fallen due with no envelope against any of them, and a twelfth falls due on Tuesday the twelfth of January instant. The five bells are at about five and a half fathoms inside the four hundred yards off the outer marks, on nobody's schedule, and the four people they belong to hold a limit, and the holder has not asked to have it lifted. Those two objects of nobody's are about two hundred and eleven days up the crown of that mole, about nine feet apart, under two tarpaulins, one with a corner off it since the seventeenth of June past and not lashed back, and neither has moved.**
