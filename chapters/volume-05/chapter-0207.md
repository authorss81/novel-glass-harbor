# Chapter 207

*A Counter off the Fish Market, and Two Prices That Are Not the Same Price*

Friday the eighteenth and Saturday the nineteenth of December, at a counter in a room off the fish market, and in the shed.

**And the far bench was occupied on the Friday from about ten until about four, and was not asked for anything and asked nothing, and Calder Marr did not go over to him, and he was not at that bench on the Thursday at any hour.**

**And on the Saturday there was no day of work booked and the board was not written, and this party entered that with the reason against the line, which is that a day on which nothing happens still happened, and which is a day on which this yard bought one sheet of paper at a penny and got a figure that is in its own hand, and that is two things and only one of them is a cost.**

---

**And at about ten on the Friday morning this party went down the hill and into a room off the fish market, and it is a room with one counter in it and a bench along the wall, and the counter is open to anybody from eight until the market is off the boards, and where a sheet is a penny and forty sheets a shilling.**

The holder took the copy of the record out of the inside pocket of a coat and put it on the counter flat, and did not read any of it out, and neither did the ship's hand, and nobody in that room heard one line of it.

"**We are not here to have that copied,**" he said, "**and I want that said first and I want it said to the woman at the counter and not said to the room.**"

Pernel Saye, fifty-eight, who keeps that counter, looked at the sheet for about half a minute without touching it, and then she looked at him.

"**Nobody has ever said that to me at that counter,**" she said, "**in thirty-one years. They come in and they want a copy and they want it done while they wait. So whatever it is you have come in for, sit down, because you are not the first man to arrive in December with a figure and no errand and I am not going to be rude about it.**

"**Now. What is it you want priced.**"

"**Two things,**" said Calder Marr. "**I want to know what it would cost to have a page that anybody in nine places could check. And I want to know what it would cost to have a page that nobody could. And I have been told by nobody that those are the same price and I do not believe they are.**"

The copyist pulled the stool out from under the counter and sat on it, which she did not do for the last three people who came in that week.

"**Say nine places,**" she said. "**Say what a place is. Is a place a town or is a place a drawer.**"

"**A town.**"

"**Right. Then I can price it and you will not like either figure.**

"**The first one. A page that anybody in nine places could check. Now — a copy is a copy. If I make you nine of them they are nine of the same words, and a man in the ninth town can hold his up against a man in the first and every one of the nine is right and not one of the nine checks anything, and I have been doing that for thirty-one years and I know what I am telling you.

"**What a man in nine places can check is nine people who each went and got it. Not nine copies. Nine journeys, nine counters, nine days apart, and nine men who can each stand in their own town and say *I went and I got it and I paid a penny for it and here is the day I paid it on*. That is not a copying trade. That is a fetching trade. And there is not one trade on this coast that does it, and the four people on this coast who could do it are alive in this town with no boat and about nine marks between them and I have watched them carry water.**

"**So: the paper in the first version is nine pence. Forty of those is two shillings and a threepence, and I will do it in three days and I will not charge you for my three days because nobody charges for three days.

"**And the price of the other one is nine men's time. And a man's time is not a penny. A man's time in a working port in December is a day, and a day at a good trade is about three marks on a board with the eighth and the share off the top, and that is the honest unit and it is the only unit I have. So the price of a page that anybody in nine places could check is nine men each losing a day, and one of those days is the whole of what a yard of two and a half people brings in on a day at the second seam.

"**And I will say that again because you did not move: the price of a checkable page is the whole of a day's work at a seam.**"

She turned round and put a hand flat on the counter.

"**The second one. A page that nobody can check. That is a sheet, and a shed, and no money at all, and it is the cheapest instrument in this country and I have never in thirty-one years seen a cheaper one. And it is the one that cannot be shown to anybody, and a thing that cannot be shown to anybody is a thing that has not been made, and the two of those are the same sentence and people who sell the second one usually do not know they are selling it.**

"**And that is your two prices, and you were right, and they are not the same price, and one of them is a day at a seam and the other one is a sheet, and I could not tell you in a room which one is better because I have never been asked and because it is not my trade to say.**"

"**I have one more question and it is not about paper.**"

"**Go on.**"

"**What does it cost to write a reason against a line, and then to have the reason stop at the edge of the page.**"

The copyist did not answer that for about four seconds.

"**Now that is a trade question,**" she said, "**and nobody has ever asked it of me at that counter either, and I am going to answer it and I am going to answer it exactly.

"**A paragraph is a paragraph. I will write you a paragraph on a sheet for a penny, the same as anything else, and I will write a good one, and the good ones are worth more than the bad ones and there is a difference of about a penny in it and no more. That is what a trade is: the thing costs the same whether you are careful or not, and the careful ones are not expensive.

"**And here is the whole of what is wrong with you, and I have had it out with about four people in this room in thirty-one years and I am not going to have it out with you twice. The moment a reason goes on a page it is a paragraph, and a paragraph is a trade, and a trade travels. You can keep a reason in a shed for nine years and it will not go anywhere. You cannot keep it in a paragraph. It goes off the counter at a penny and it is in another town in a fortnight and it has lost the only thing that made it true, which is that a particular pair of hands wrote it about a particular afternoon.**"

Nobody said anything about that.

"**And one more thing, because it is in my trade and not in yours, and I am going to say it about sheets in general and not about yours, and you may do what you like with it.**" She put two fingers on the copy of the record without touching it. "**A sheet with a man's name at the foot of it is a sheet I keep a second copy of for myself, in a book behind me, because if a man comes back in a year and says that is my hand, I want to be able to say that it is. A sheet with an office's name at the foot of it and no man's name on it anywhere is the easiest thing in the world for me to sell, because there is nobody on it for me to ask where it came from. Those go out of a shop and into somebody's buyer's book and nobody ever knows where they have been, and the people who buy them are not crooks, they are men doing a day's trade.**"

"**Write that down,**" said Calder Marr.

"**I have not said anything you cannot get off a counter,**" said Pernel Saye, "**and I have not said a word about that sheet and I would like it noticed that I have not said a word about it.**"

He bought one sheet of her paper at a penny, and gave the penny into her own hand, and it was for this yard's own use and not for a copy of anything, and the reason is entered in the first column and the reason is that this yard cannot put a price in a record out of a person's mouth.

---

*First column, entered on Saturday the nineteenth day of December instant at about half past nine in the evening in this shed: that on Friday the eighteenth instant at about ten in the morning this party went down the hill into a room off the fish market to a counter that is open to anybody from eight until the market is off the boards, where a sheet is a penny and forty sheets a shilling, and that a copyist of this harbor, being Pernel Saye, fifty-eight, sat down on a stool at that counter which she did not do for the last three people who came in that week, and that this party put the copy of the record flat on that counter and read none of it out and the ship's hand read none of it out and nobody in that room heard one line of it; and that the holder said first, and said it to the said woman and not to the room, that this party was not there to have the said sheet copied, and that the said woman said that nobody has ever said that to her at that counter in thirty-one years.*

*And that this party put two questions to that trade and got two answers in that trade's own words. That the first was what it would cost to have a page that anybody in nine places could check, and that the said woman said that a copy is a copy, and that if nine copies are made they are nine of the same words and not one of the nine checks anything; and that what a man in nine places can check is nine people who each went and got it, and nine journeys and nine counters and nine days apart, and nine men who can each stand in their own town and say that they went and got it and what day and at what price; and that that is not a copying trade and is a fetching trade, and that there is not one trade on this coast that does it; and that the paper in that version is nine pence and that forty of those is two shillings and a threepence, and that the said woman said she would do it in three days and would not charge for her three days because nobody charges for three days; and that the price of the other version is nine men's time, and that a man's time in a working port in December is a day, and that a day at a good trade is about three marks on a board with the eighth and the share off the top, and that the said woman gave that as the honest unit and the only unit she has; and that the price of a page that anybody in nine places could check is nine men each losing a day, and that one of those days is the whole of what a yard of two and a half people brings in on a day at the second seam.*

*And that the second answer was that a page that nobody can check is a sheet and a shed and no money at all, and that it is the cheapest instrument in that country and that the said woman has never in thirty-one years seen a cheaper one, and that it is the one that cannot be shown to anybody, and that a thing that cannot be shown to anybody is a thing that has not been made, and that the said woman said that those are the same sentence and that people who sell the second one usually do not know they are selling it; and that the said woman said she could not tell the holder in a room which of the two prices is the better one because she has never been asked and because it is not her trade to say.*

*And that the holder put a third question at that counter, which was not about paper, which was what it costs to write a reason against a line and then to have the reason stop at the edge of the page; and that the said woman said that a paragraph is a paragraph and that she will write a paragraph on a sheet for a penny the same as anything else, and that the good ones are worth about a penny more than the bad ones and no more, and that that is what a trade is; and that she said that the moment a reason goes on a page it is a paragraph and a paragraph is a trade and a trade travels, and that a reason can be kept in a shed for nine years and will not go anywhere, and cannot be kept in a paragraph, and that it goes off a counter at a penny and is in another town in a fortnight and has lost the only thing that made it true, which is that a particular pair of hands wrote it about a particular afternoon.*

*And that the said woman then said one more thing, and said it about sheets in general and not about this party's, and that the holder asked that it be written down; and that what she said was that a sheet with a man's name at the foot of it is a sheet a copyist keeps a second copy of for herself in a book behind the counter, because if a man comes back in a year and says that is my hand she wants to be able to say that it is; and that a sheet with an office's name at the foot of it and no man's name on it anywhere is the easiest thing in the world for a copyist to sell, because there is nobody on it for her to ask where it came from; and that those go out of a shop and into somebody's buyer's book and nobody ever knows where they have been, and that the people who buy them are not crooks and are men doing a day's trade; and that the said woman said that she had said nothing about the sheet on the counter and would like it noticed that she had said nothing about it.*

*And that the holder bought one sheet of the paper of that counter at a penny at about half past ten in the morning of the said Friday and put the penny into the said woman's own hand, and that the said sheet was for this yard's own use and not for a copy of anything, and that the reason against the line is that this party cannot put a price in a record out of a person's mouth; and that the bag held seven marks, six shillings and five pence on the Friday morning, which is one thousand one hundred and ninety-seven pence, and that the penny went out of it, and that the figure in the bag after it is one thousand one hundred and ninety-six pence, which is seven marks, six shillings and four pence.*

*And that no day of work was booked on the said Friday or on the said Saturday and that the board was not written on either day, and that the reason against the line is that a day on which nothing happens still happened and that the said Saturday is a day on which this party bought one sheet of paper at a penny and got a figure that is in this yard's own hand, and that those are two things and only one of them is a cost; and that the far bench was occupied on the said Friday from about ten until about four and was not asked for anything and asked nothing, and the holder did not go over to him, and he was not at that bench on the Thursday at any hour.*

*Second column, not checkable from a mole, entered on Saturday the nineteenth day of December instant at about half past nine in the evening in this shed, and no name is against it: that a copyist of fifty-eight of this harbor said at her own counter that a paragraph is a trade and that a trade travels, and that a reason cannot be kept in a paragraph; and that the reason this line is in this column and not the first is that a man on a mole can check what a woman said at a counter on a Friday morning and can check what she charged and cannot check what is going to be read off that counter in a town four days up this coast in a fortnight, and that the whole of what this yard is about to decide is going to be decided by things that happen at other counters and not in this shed; and that this party did not tell the said woman what a second column is, and did not ask her, and gave her no reason to guess, and that the only person in this harbor who could have told her is a woman of no office four days up this coast who is owed forty marks and who has told this yard not to ask her again this winter.*

**A penny went out of this yard on the eighteenth of December and nothing came into it on either of these two days, and nothing went out on the nineteenth, and seven marks, six shillings and four pence is in the bag, which is one thousand one hundred and ninety-six pence. Thirty-one on the chandler's note, not re-written, and the three counts of weeks stand at twenty-two and thirteen and eleven. Five marks of compressor's gland on that page, unpaid and useless, and the thing after the gland is the hose and this harbor has no hose. Nothing in a tin since the twenty-eighth of April. Three marks owing to a man of Ashtide for the eighth of August, unmoved, and he is not named in that book again. Seven pence owing to this yard's own shipwright and it is not paid. Eight marks and a day of a boat owing to this harbor's own bellwright since the twenty-third of May, unargued, and he was not asked anything in these two days, no digit of anything he said on the second of July is printed, the chest was not opened and was not brought down the hill. Eight shillings and tenpence owing to a woman of this harbor with her name on the figure. Forty marks standing whole to a boy of twenty-two, and the three nots stand, and the head of the third column keeps his hand, and that column has not been filled since the first of August and nobody has been appointed to it. Forty marks to a person of no office, first after food, unpaid since the fifteenth of October, and not one mark of it paid and not one shilling of it offered, and she has told this yard not to ask her again this winter and this yard has not asked. Twenty-six lines and one hundred and four marks in the wage column, the fifth of March the oldest, twenty envelopes on a bench, eight weeks fallen due with no envelope standing against them. The two objects of nobody's are about a hundred and eighty-nine days up the crown of that mole, about nine feet apart, under two tarpaulins, one with a corner off it since the seventeenth of June and not lashed back, and neither of them has moved. The plate at the fifth seam is in eleven feet, seen and not measured and not touched, and the note against it stands open, and no better letter was written about it in these two days. The notice of the thirty-first of October has its four clauses and has not been lifted, narrowed, re-served or altered, and no person, no boat and no lead of this yard went inside its first clause on either of these two days. The mole-head light is not on, because a light costs oil. The north berth is out. The tide gang is four men short. The fire party's hose is dead until the spring. The hard at Ashtide is unanswered. One herring berth of two is out. Nineteen winters are in a list being made up in a city by a woman at a table, and this yard's four returns are in her hands, and nobody in Morrow Quay will ever be able to check what she writes with them. The *Sea Wren* is at the pile and this yard has not thanked him. The delayed note stands at eleven logged occasions and two of them with no call in them, and nothing was entered in these two days and nothing was measured, and the delay is a hand's width of a second and has not moved, and nobody in this harbor has ever said why. The bell is off the south end in eight and a half feet and is not going out inside four years. The writ runs to midnight on the first of March next, four names and no fifth, and the second season is a named absence. The custody room was not asked for, nothing came out of it, no digit of any of the three numbers is in this book, and the question that no paper in this harbor or in that office's copying room answers was not put to anybody in these two days. The case of five bells is at about five and a half fathoms inside the four hundred yards off the outer marks and is on nobody's schedule, and the four of the Sill are alive with no boat and no wages and about nine marks between them, and the limit they wrote themselves stands, and the holder did not ask in these two days to have it lifted. The coast road is in type in a city beside a pilot's figure and neither is withdrawn, and this volume does not decide which of them is right, and the Kettle was not looked at. And a page that anybody in nine places could check costs nine men a day each and one of those days is the whole of what this yard brings in on a day at the second seam, and a page that nobody could check costs a sheet and a shed and no money at all, and both of those figures are in this yard's own hand and came out of a woman's mouth at a counter on the eighteenth instant, and no price in either of them was paid to anybody.**
