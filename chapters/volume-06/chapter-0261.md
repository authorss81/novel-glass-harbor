# Chapter 261

*The Rate, Said Out Loud at the Head of That Mole, and the Cut on a Bench Above the Water*

Monday the fifth of April, at the head of that mole, in the shed, and on the bench outside the shed.

**At eight in the morning the wall behind the reeve's bench came down and it came down bare, and that was the forty-eighth Monday since the first of May past, and the counter did not restart, and the clerk kept his own page and did not mention it, and no sitting has been held in that room on that day or on any other day of this book.**

**And the cistern behind the engine house was charged at no figure at about ten on the said Monday morning by four men this yard does not know, and this party walked none of it, and that is the thirty-fifth of the charges that party has entered walked on none of since the third day of August past, and the walk is nil and it stays nil.**

**And no day of work was booked in this yard on the said Monday, and the reason against the line of that day is that there is no paper in the drawer to book a day against, and there has not been one since the fourth day of March past.**

---

He came up the mole steps at about eight with the same sheet of thirty-eight lines in his coat, folded at the thirty-eighth line, and he did not take it out.

**"I did not sleep," said Garrow Fisk. "I want that in whatever you write down, because a man who did not sleep and came up a hill at eight has a figure in his head, and the figure is the one I am going to say, and I would like it written that I said it tired."**

"Write it," said Nessa Pike, and wrote it.

"**Nine marks a day.**" He said it on the mole itself, standing on the second step from the top with the market not yet up and about nine people on the crown of it who had come for the tide and were not going anywhere. "A compressor, a housing, the pipe, the gauges, and one man off a Crown house book who knows how to run them and who is not me. Nine marks a day, and it is the same nine marks a Crown hull pays, and a Crown hull has it in a harbour's book and takes it out without looking at it, and you are going to look at it every single day of it, and I am not going to pretend to you that the figure is small. I said on Friday in that shed that I would not, and a man who says it once and then says it smaller is a man you cannot take a pot down on."

**Nobody on that mole said that it was small.** A woman with a basket of fish on her arm said, "That is a lot for three days," and put the basket down and picked it up again, and that was the whole of what about nine people said.

"My own time is two marks and eightpence a day, and I named that to you on Friday before anybody asked me for it, and it does not change. That is the figure the book has me at and I have never once been argued off it." He turned round and looked at the water off the south end. "And the hand on the hose is yours to find, and I do not care what you pay him, and if you want my advice he is worth about a shilling and fourpence a day and not a penny more, because that is what a hand is worth, and a hand that costs more than that has been somewhere else and will go somewhere else in the middle of a tide."

"So the set is twenty-seven marks for three days," said Calder Marr, "and you are six marks and two shillings for three days, and a hand is three marks and six shillings for three days, and that is the whole of what three days of this costs and it is thirty-six marks and eight shillings and there is nothing else in it."

**"And that is what you have to find," said Fisk, "before you say yes to anything. I have watched two men in my trade agree to a figure in a doorway and then find it with a spade, and the spade is always the same spade. I am telling you the figures, and I am not going to be one of the two men who waits to be asked what he wants."**

Nessa Pike read the three figures back to him and corrected one of them, which was that the hand is a shilling and fourpence a day and not a shilling and sixpence, and he took the correction and said she was right, and she read it back a second time.

---

Then they went into the shed, and he asked what the descent was, and was told.

"A free descent in open water," said Calder Marr. "Off the south end and a mile and a half out, in the cut. About forty fathoms. Nothing to go into. No hull, no bell, no plate, no door, nothing but the bottom and a line and a pot."

**"Then I will go down that," said Garrow Fisk, "and I want you to hear why in the same breath, because you have heard the rest of it and it is not caution. It is a man I worked beside for nine years on the western shelf, and he is alive and does not go down at all now, and what happened to him did not happen to a hull. It happened to a line."**

He put his hand flat on the bench, the way a man does when he is about to say the thing he has been carrying up a hill.

"**Everything I sell you is on the end of a line.** A compressor is on the end of a pipe, and a pipe is on the end of a man. If there is nothing hung on that bottom, then there is nothing on your line but the man and the pot, and the line is a rope, and a rope is a convenience. If there is something hung on that bottom, then your line is a thing a swell on the surface can put round a corner and take hold of, and everything on the surface of this earth is then holding one end of it and pulling, and there is a man at the end of it in the dark, and the man at the end of it does not get a vote." He took his hand off the bench. "**So nothing goes under that has not been cut. I do not mean I want the water to be tidy. I mean I want your line to be short enough that it cannot be used against the man on it.**"

"That is not a rule in a book," said Calder Marr.

"It is not a rule in a book, and there is no book on this coast with it in, and if there were I should want to know who wrote it and what it cost him."

---

**"Then cut it," said Nessa Pike.**

He turned round. She had a shackle in her hand, one off her own bench. It was not new and it was not the make that was on the mark off the face of the second seam, and it was about the size of a man's fist.

"You are not going down a wire," she said. "A wire is a wire, and a wire holds a man to a place. You are going down a **shot line** — a bight of it, rove through that shackle, with the two ends up on the crown and a man on each end holding it. And when the pot is on the bottom and the gear is off him, the bight is **cut** on the surface, and what is left in the water is a man and a pot and forty fathoms of slack and nothing anybody is holding. That is what he means. That is the whole of what he means, and he has said it in about two hundred words, and I have made it a shackle and a bight and three turns of wire."

Garrow Fisk looked at the shackle for a while.

"**Then the turns are on the wrong side.**"

"They are on the water side on purpose," said Nessa Pike, "and I will tell you why, and I would rather you heard it from me than worked it out on a beach somewhere and wrote to a man about it. On the landward side is where this yard turns. It has always been on the landward side. And there is a shackle on the face of that second seam with three turns on the **water** side of it, and a flat on the crown of the bow that this yard does not fit, and somebody put that on in a hurry in the dark. If I turn your shackle the way this yard turns, then the only shackle on this coast with three turns on the water side is one this yard made, and a man on a mole with a spanner in his hand would be two and two, and this yard does not want that man to be able to be two and two. **I am going to make yours the other way up, and it is going to look wrong to about four people in this harbor, and it is going to look right to about nobody, and that is the price of the thing.**"

"Write that in the leaf," said Calder Marr.

"**It is in the leaf**," said Nessa Pike. "In the fourth column, under the head you wrote with a blunt nib on the Saturday. It says: *what it is not — that three turns of wire on the water side of a shackle of this yard's own making is not a hand, and a man on a mole with a spanner in his hand is not seeing a name.* And if that is the wrong column then the column is wrong and not the line, and you ruled the column and not me."

---

**And then the man of the *Anna Ray* came up the mole, and he had been standing at the top of it for a quarter of an hour and nobody had gone for him.**

He was fifty-one and there was rain in his shoulders and he had a net's worth of mud on his boots that had not come off in a week, and he said his name before anybody asked, which in that harbor is a thing a man does when he is about to say a figure and does not want to be asked twice.

"Elias Voke, of the *Anna Ray*, second lay. And I have been up here a quarter of an hour because there are three of you and one of you is a fireman with a price on him, and I did not want to be standing in the middle of that. So say it again for me, and I will tell you afterwards whether I have come for it."

Fisk said the three figures again, slowly, and Voke listened with his mouth a little open and did not interrupt, which in that harbor is respect.

"**Then here is what I have come for.** My ground is shut. I lost my warp and my net and eleven cork floats on the night of the twenty-ninth of March past, in the cut off the south end, in about forty fathoms, and they are not lost. **They are hung.** They went down over something and they are on the bottom holding on, and that is worse, because a lost net comes up by itself in a week in a bad tide, and a hung net stays where it is until somebody cuts it off. Until somebody cuts it off there is a mile and a half of that ground that no boat in this harbor can drag a net through, and it is not my mile and a half. It is six boats' mile and a half, and I have the first refusal on it, and I cannot use it."

"How long," said Calder Marr.

"Until the herring are off that ground, which is the end of next month, and then it is somebody else's problem and there is no money in it for anybody." Voke took his hands out of his pockets and looked at them. "And I have been on that water nineteen years, and I have never once had to be told what was down there, and I am not asking you what is down there. I am asking you to find out what my net is hung on and cut it off. **And I will pay eleven marks.**"

He said the figure before anybody had said one word about a price, and he said it the way Fisk had said his, out loud, on the mole, with the market coming up behind him and about eleven people within hearing.

"That is eleven marks, and it is not the cost," said Voke. "I know it is not the cost. I heard the twenty-seven. **I have got nineteen winters in a list that a woman at a table in a city is making up, and a list is not a wage, and there is not one penny of that list come back to this quay, and a man cannot pay out of a list.** I am not going to be argued up. If you want more than eleven marks you will not get it from me this season, and you can put that in your book with the date on it, and I will not come back and say I said twelve."

Nessa Pike wrote it and read it back and he nodded at it.

"And the arithmetic," said Calder Marr, "is that eleven marks is not a third of the cost, and the difference is this yard's, and the yard is going to take it, and here is the why, and it goes in the first column with the date on it."

"Go on."

"**Because a man asked for his own net off his own ground, and it is his, and because a party that cannot be hired has exactly one thing left that it can sell, which is a day of itself, and it has not sold one in twenty-three days.** And I am going to write beside it, so that a man in four years cannot read this page and think we were paid for it: *this was not hired work and it is not salvage and it is not a claim, and there is no paper of any kind behind it, and a man with a net in a mile and a half of water is not a hirer relying on our paper, and we are not a Crown hull, and we did not pretend to be one this morning.*"

---

*First column, entered on Monday the fifth day of April next at about half past nine in the evening in this shed: that the wall behind that harbor authority's reeve's bench came down bare at eight in the morning on the said Monday, being the forty-eighth Monday since the first of May past, and that the counter did not restart and the clerk kept his own page and did not mention it.*

*And that the cistern was charged at no figure at about ten on the said Monday by four men this yard does not know, and that party walked none of it, being the thirty-fifth of the charges that party has entered walked on none of since the third day of August past.*

*And that a man of about thirty-four, who gave his name as Garrow Fisk and who is of a pressure house at Ternwatch, came up that mole at about eight in the morning on the said Monday and said that he had not slept, and asked for that to be written down; and that the said man then named three figures out loud at the head of that mole in about nine people's hearing, being that the compressor, the housing, the pipe, the gauges and one man off a Crown house book who knows how to run them is nine marks a day, and that the figure is the figure a Crown hull pays, and that the said man said out loud that he would not pretend it was small; and that the price of the said man's own time is two marks and eightpence a day, named on the Friday before and not changed; and that a hand on the hose is a shilling and fourpence a day, and is this yard's own figure to find and not the said man's.*

*And that the whole of the three figures was read back to the said man and that the ship's hand corrected one of them and read the three back a second time, and that the said man said he would go down a free descent in open water off the south end in about forty fathoms with nothing to enter, and that the reason against his condition is that everything he sells is on the end of a line, and that when there is nothing hung on the bottom the line is a rope, and when there is something hung on it the line is a thing a swell can take hold of, and that what he wants is for the line to be short enough that it cannot be used against the man on it.*

*And that the ship's hand said the cut would be a bight of shot line rove through a shackle of that yard's own iron, with the two ends on the crown, cut on the surface when the pot was on the bottom and the gear was off, and that the three turns on that shackle would be on the water side, against that yard's own practice, and that the reason against that is that a shackle on the face of the second seam carries three turns on the water side of it, and that if that yard's cut turned the way that yard turns then the only shackle on that coast with three turns on the water side would be one that yard had made; and that the said line was entered in the fourth column of the leaf ruled on the twenty-seventh of March past.*

*And that a man of fifty-one, who gave his name as Elias Voke and who is of the herring boat of the second lay called the Anna Ray, came up that mole at about ten in the morning and said that his warp, his net and eleven cork floats went down on the night of the twenty-ninth of March past in the cut off the south end in about forty fathoms, and are hung and not lost, and that until they are cut off there is a mile and a half of ground that no boat in that harbor can drag a net through, and that the ground is his by first refusal and he cannot use it, and that the herring are off that ground at the end of the next month.*

*And that the said man offered eleven marks, and named the figure before any price had been spoken of by anybody, and that he said he knew it was not the cost and was not going to be argued up, and that he has nineteen winters in a list being made up in a city, and that a list is not a wage; and that the ship's hand read that figure back to him and he nodded at it.*

*Second column, not checkable from a mole, entered on the said Monday at about half past nine in the evening in this shed, and no name is against it: that the reason that yard agreed to lose money on this is not a reason a man on a mole can check, and that it is entered here and not in the first column, and that it is that a party with no paper behind it has one saleable thing left and has not sold it in twenty-three days, and that a day of itself is a thing a man on a mole can check and the twenty-three days is a thing a man on a mole can check, and that the reason underneath both of them is not.*

*And that the number of hands available to that party on that day was one and a half, and that a man cannot count a half, and that the half is a woman who writes with her left hand at about twice the time it takes her, and that no figure is entered for the half because there is no figure for a half.*

---

**Nothing came into this yard on the said Monday and nothing went out of it, and sixty-five marks and threepence is in the bag, which is ten thousand four hundred and three pence, and that figure is where it was on Sunday. The whole of what came into that yard in the twenty-one days ended on the said Sunday is nothing, and the reason is the form of the fourth of March past and not a mood, and the figure of Elias Voke was named in that shed on the said Monday and did not come in until the morning after. The wage column stands at twenty-six lines and one hundred and four marks and twenty-three weeks have fallen due with no envelope standing against them, and the said Monday was not a wage Tuesday and the column did not move, and the next is tomorrow.**
