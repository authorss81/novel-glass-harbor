# Chapter 252

*Six Marks a Day and a Man of About Forty, and the Terms of a Boat That Is Not the Yard's*

Thursday the eighteenth and Friday the nineteenth of March, on the mole's steps and in a shed on that mole.

**The cistern behind the engine house was charged at no figure at about ten on the said Thursday morning by four men this yard does not know and this party walked none of it, and that is the thirtieth of the charges this party has entered walked on none of since the third day of August past, and the walk is nil and it stays nil, and no day of work was booked in this yard on that Thursday, and the reason against that line is that four men this yard does not know have been at that cistern since ten and a party of one does not work a day it has spent at a cistern.**

**And the far bench was not occupied on the said Thursday at any hour, and was occupied on the said Friday from about ten until about four, and the holder of that writ is up that coast road and did not go over to him on either of the two days, and a man of sixty-one was not asked for anything on either of them and asked nothing on either of them, and the only question asked in that shed on the said Friday was asked by a man of sixty-one of this harbor and not by anybody in it.**

**And no day of work was booked in this yard on the said Friday either, and the reason against that line is the same reason and not a second one, and a boat of about forty tons came alongside that mole at about eight in the morning on the said Friday and stayed three hours and went away again without its owner in it.**

---

Tobias Wren came up the mole steps at about ten past eight on the Friday and stopped at the top of them the way a man stops who has been up steps for forty years and intends to keep the fact available, and he looked at the shed, and at the boat alongside, and at the two objects of nobody's under their tarpaulins on the crown, and he did not look at them for longer than a man looks at two things that are somebody else's trouble and his.

He is sixty-one. He is half deaf in the left ear and uses a whistle to catch people at a distance, and he came down the mole that morning with a third of a mile of hard walking in him and did not use the whistle on anybody, which is how Nessa knew it was him, because a man with two ears uses his voice.

"You had a mark fouled," he said.

"You heard about a mark fouled."

"I heard about a mark fouled on Saturday at the fish market, from a man who heard it from the man of the second lay, who does not repeat things, so that is a good chain." He put his hand on the bollard. "I brought the boat up to see it. I did not come up to be asked."

Nessa put the slate down on the bench, which is what she did with a slate when she was going to write, and then she did not write, and left it there.

"You have not looked at it," she said.

"I have not looked at it. There is a mark fouled on the weather side with about four inches of green on it and there is a boat going out past it on Wednesday whether anybody looks or not, and if the man on the wheel does not know it is there, then it is a thing I have heard about and not a thing I have seen, and a man who has not seen a thing has no business pricing it."

**"Who is paying," he said.**

That was the whole of it. He did not ask it loudly and he did not ask it as a complaint. He asked it the way a man asks a thing he has asked before in other harbours and has always been glad to be asked, because the answer decides whether he is talking to a party or to weather.

"I am," said Nessa. "Out of that drawer."

"How much is in the drawer."

"Seventy—" she said, and stopped, because a man who has been wrong in front of a harbor once does not do it twice in a year. "Sixty-nine marks, two shillings and five pence. A mark is a hundred and sixty pence. It is twenty-three days of a day's work on the second seam, and there is no day of work on the second seam to be had, because there is no paper, and I am telling you that because you will find it out in a fortnight and it is better that a thing is said by the person it concerns than found out by the person it concerns."

Tobias Wren looked at her for a while. Then he sat down on the bench, on the near end, which is the end a man sits on when he is going to be there an hour.

"Then here is what a boat is," he said, "and I am going to say all of it before you ask me for any of it, because the last time I let a yard ask me a boat one question at a time I was in it four days and never knew what I was in.

"A day is six marks. That is my boat and my gear and the wear, and it is the same six marks it was in the spring and it is the same six marks it will be in the autumn, and I do not come down in a bad month and I do not come up in a good one, and if you want me to say that to somebody in a market you may and it will be true, and if you want me to say it to an office you will not find me at an office, because I have not been at an office since the spring.

"The man who comes with me is about forty and he is called Ruad and he has been with me eleven years and he is not mine and he is not yours and he is paid by the day and he is the best hand on this coast after the two of us and he has a family at Lowfen and he will not come for nothing and he will not come for a promise. That is his day and it is separate from mine and you pay it separate.

"And the fuel is fuel and it is what it costs and it is not in the six marks.

"So a day is six marks and a man and the fuel, and a day of a boat with a man in her that I do not go in is a mark and eightpence, and that is the price the man of the second lay is charging you and he is charging you right, and I am not going to beat him and I would think less of you if you asked me to."

Nessa did not say anything for a moment. The wind came under the shed door again and found the gap.

"Six marks is two days of what the second seam pays a man," she said, "and this yard has not had one of those days since the fourteenth of March."

"Six marks is two of your days and I can see that you have done that sum already, and it is not the sum that makes it dear." He turned the slate round and put it face down. "It is a day of a hull and a day of wire and a day of a man's hands that are mine because there is nobody else to do it, and it is dear, and it is dear because I am the only man on this coast with a hull that can stand being put where you are going to put her." He looked at her hands. "That is not a favour and it is not a favour you are being offered. It is a price, and you may not have it, and I am telling you the price so that you cannot say afterwards that nobody told you."

**"And one more thing, and I am putting it in front of you now so it is not a surprise in a month," he said. "I will not be a teacher."**

"Nobody asked you to be."

"I know nobody asked me to be. That is why I am saying it. In thirty-one years, by another man's account, I have never once been asked what was in a thing I went into, and I have never once said, and I am not going to start at sixty-one because a yard on a mole has nothing to do." He got up off the bench, which took him longer than it used to. "I will tell you what I saw the last time, and I saw it thirty-one years ago by another man's book, and I will tell you what I have seen since, and you may write both of them down or you may not, and if you write them down you write that they are a man of sixty-one telling you about a thing he was not on."

"What did you see the last time."

"Took a wreck apart with a wire saw and a bad wheel and my own hands and came up with nothing in the frame that I could carry, and left a thing on the bottom that would have fitted in my coat, and went home." He was already at the steps. "The going out is the whole of it. It is not the rest of it and the rest of it is what costs you the money, and the money is not the part I care about, and I have never been able to say that to a man in a shed and I have just said it to a woman in one, so you may tell your holder I said it and he will not know what to do with it."

He went down the steps. Nessa stood at the top of them with the slate still on the bench behind her and did not pick it up for about a minute.

Then she wrote the day down. Six marks. A man of about forty, called Ruad, paid by the day. The fuel. And under it, in the second column, in her own left hand: *six marks a day is twice what a day of work on the second seam pays this yard, and this yard has had none of those days since the fourteenth of March, and the season of that mark is nine marks, and nobody in this country is going to be asked for nine marks.*

---

*First column, entered on Friday the nineteenth day of March next at about two in the afternoon in this shed: that a boat of about forty tons belonging to a man of sixty-one of this harbor came alongside that mole at about ten past eight on the said morning and lay there three hours and went away again, and that the man of sixty-one came up those steps and said three things and asked one question, and that the question was who is paying.*

*And that the said man named his terms before any of them was asked for, being six marks a day for the boat and the gear and the wear and the same six marks in any month of any year, and a hand of about forty called Ruad who has been with him eleven years and who is paid by the day separate from the boat and who has a family at Lowfen and will not come for nothing and will not come for a promise, and the fuel at what fuel costs and not in the six marks; and that a day of a boat with a man in her that the said man does not go in is one mark and eightpence, being the price the man of the second lay is charging, and that the said man said that the man of the second lay is charging right and that he would not beat him and would think less of anybody who asked him to.*

*And that the said man said that he would not be a teacher, and that in thirty-one years by another man's account he had never been asked what was in a thing he went into and had never said, and that what he saw the last time was a wreck taken apart with a wire saw and a bad wheel and his own hands and nothing in the frame he could carry and a thing left on the bottom that would have fitted in his coat, and that he went home, and that the going out is the whole of it and the rest of it is what costs the money and the money is not the part he cares about.*

*And that no day of work was booked in that yard on either of the said two days, and that the reason against the line of the said Thursday is that four men this yard does not know were at that cistern from about ten, and that the reason against the line of the said Friday is the same reason and not a second one.*

*Second column, not checkable from a mole, entered on the said Friday at about two in the afternoon in this shed, and no name is against it: that six marks a day is twice what a day of work on the face of the second seam pays this yard, being four hundred and eighty pence against nine hundred and sixty pence, and that this yard has nothing coming in and the reason is the form of the fourth of that month and is not a mood, and that the season of the mark set on the twelfth is nine marks and is not going to be paid and nobody is going to be asked for it.*

*And that a boat of six marks a day is not dear because of the fuel and is not dear because of the man and is dear because there is one hull on this coast that can stand being put where this yard would have to put her, and that a party with no paper and no instrument is asking a man with both to come and work on a mole, and that the said man said no to the part of it that was asked for before it was asked for, and that the yard entered it and did not improve on it.*

**Nothing came into this yard on either of the said two days and nothing went out of it on either of them, and sixty-nine marks, two shillings and five pence is in the bag, which is eleven thousand and sixty-nine pence, and the figure the bag is short of the wage of four marks is ten thousand four hundred and twenty-nine pence. The whole of what has gone out of that yard since the sixth of December past is four marks, four shillings and eight pence in seven transactions at five counters, and no figure has entered that bag in two days and no figure has left it, and the six marks a day of the said man's boat has not been paid and is not going to be paid out of that book this month, and the price of it is not in that book at all because the price of it has not been agreed. Nothing in a tin in that shed since the twenty-eighth of April. Three marks owing to a man of Ashtide, unmoved. Seven pence to that yard's own shipwright, unpaid. Eight shillings and tenpence to a woman of that harbor with her name on the figure. Eight marks and a day of a boat owing to that harbor's own bellwright since the twenty-third of May past, unargued, and he was not asked anything in these two days and no digit of anything he said on the second of July past is on a page of that book. Forty marks first after food to a person of no office, unpaid since the fifteenth of October past, and that yard has not asked her for anything since the twenty-sixth of December past and did not ask her in these two days. Forty marks stand whole to a boy of twenty-two, the three nots stand, the third column is not filled and nobody is appointed to it, and the boy's hand is at the head of it. The wage column stands at twenty-six lines and one hundred and four marks and twenty-one weeks have fallen due with no envelope standing against them, and the said Tuesday and the said Friday were not both wage Tuesdays and only the said Tuesday was one, and the column did not move. Thirty-one on the chandler's note, not re-written, its three counts unmoved at twenty-two and thirteen and eleven, and it was said out loud for the fourth time in seven months on the fourth of that month and will not be said a fifth time. The notice of the thirty-first of October stands with its four clauses, unlifted, and no person, no boat and no lead of that yard has been inside its first clause on either of these two days, and no return of any kind was made out of either of them. The plate at the fifth seam is in eleven feet in that mole's own foundation stone and is not reached, not measured, not touched and not asked about, and the note against it stands open, and the only mechanism anybody has named for reaching it is a change of politics in an office and not a better letter out of a shed. The case of five bells is at about five and a half fathoms inside the four hundred yards off the outer marks, on nobody's schedule, was not raised and was not asked about in these two days, and the limit four people of that coast wrote and signed stands and the holder did not ask to have it lifted. The delayed note stands at eleven logged occasions and two of them with no call in them, and nothing was entered on either of these two days and nothing was measured. The two objects of nobody's are about two hundred and eighty-one days up the crown of that mole, about nine feet apart, under two tarpaulins, and neither has moved, and that party did not go near them on either of these two days, and the man of sixty-one who owns a boat looked at them for a moment on the said Friday and went up the steps. The bench under that pump is bare for a forty-eighth night, counting from the Sunday of the thirty-first of January instant.**
