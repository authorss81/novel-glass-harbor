# Chapter 254

*The Forty-Sixth Time the Wall Came Down, and the Shackle That Was Bright*

Monday the twenty-second and Tuesday the twenty-third of March, at the reeve's bench on the west basin, in the shed, and in a boat of about forty tons at the face of the second seam.

**At eight in the morning on the said Monday the wall behind the reeve's bench came down and it came down bare, and that was the forty-sixth Monday since the first of May past, and the counter did not restart, and the clerk kept his own page and did not mention it, and no sitting was held in that room on that day or on any other day of this book.**

**And the cistern behind the engine house was charged at no figure at about ten on the said Monday morning by four men this yard does not know and this party walked none of it, and that is the thirty-first of the charges this party has entered walked on none of since the third day of August past, and the walk is nil and it stays nil.**

**And the far bench was not occupied on the said Monday at any hour and was not occupied on the said Tuesday at any hour, and the holder of that writ did not go over to him on either of the two days, and a man of sixty-one was not asked for anything on either of them and asked nothing on either of them.**

**And the said Tuesday was a wage Tuesday, and the number of weeks fallen due with no envelope standing against them is TWENTY-TWO and is the twenty-second of that same list, and the wage column did not move and the envelope count did not move, and the reason is the reason entered on the twenty-ninth of December past and is not improved on. And a boat of about forty tons went out on the said Tuesday at eleven with the man of the second lay and the ship's hand in her, and the holder of that writ went as far as the face of the second seam and did not go in the water at any time of the day, and the reason against that line is entered in the first column and is not improved on.**

---

The clerk of the harbor authority was wiping the frame of the board with his sleeve when Nessa came in, which he always was at eight on a Monday, and which nobody in that room had ever once mentioned to him.

"It's the sixth time this year I've seen it bare," Nessa said, which was a lie, and she said it to see what he would do with it.

The clerk did not do anything with it. He took his sleeve off the frame, and looked at the bare slate, and said, "It is wiped every Monday at eight whether there is a sitting or not. That is the standing instruction. I have never had cause to ask for a different one."

"I did not think you had."

"No, ma'am." He put the cloth in his pocket. "There's no sitting this morning. There's no sitting on any morning this week. There's been no sitting since the twenty-ninth of November, and I have a page here with the dates on it, and nobody has asked me for the page, and I would not give it to anybody who had not asked."

"That is the correct answer to that question and nobody has asked you the question."

"Aye," said the clerk, and went back to his counter, and did not mention it again.

---

On the Tuesday the shipwright was in the water-free part of it, and Calder Marr was on the mole with a coil of a penny's worth of line over his shoulder, and he was not enjoying himself, and it was in his face.

"Nessa. Give me the slate."

"You cannot hold the slate and read a mark in four fathoms and I cannot hold the slate and steer and do both, and he cannot either, because he is on the wheel in a following sea, and that is three jobs and we are three people and one of us is a woman with a right hand that is four stitches."

"I can hold a slate."

"You can hold a slate. You are not going in the water."

"I am not going in the water."

"You are not going in the water," she agreed, "and I would like it written down that you said it before I said it, because in about a fortnight you are going to want to have said the other thing."

**"Then write it down," he said, and she did, and the line in the first column reads that the holder of that writ offered to take the slate and was refused by the ship's hand on the ground that a private hire of a boat is not a place to put a man in the water, and that the holder said so first, and that the reason against the line is that a mark is a mark and not a wreck and that there is nothing under it anybody is looking for.**

He read that back to her afterwards, at the oars, before the boat was off the stones, and he read it without changing a word, which is the practice and which he has done four times.

The tide was a hand's breadth off the top and running north-east across the face, and the mark was where it had been set on the twelfth, and it was fouled on the weather side with about four inches of green.

**The shackle had three bright places on it and not two.**

"Write three," Nessa said. "I wrote two on Wednesday and there are three."

"Write three and put Wednesday against it, and put the day against that, and don't rub the two out."

"I was not going to rub the two out. I have never rubbed a figure out of that slate in my life and neither have you." She wrote it. "Three bright places. That's the pin, that's the bow, and that's a third one on the underside of the shackle where a shackle does not get handled unless somebody has had it off and put it back on the wrong way up."

**"Off and on again," said the man of the second lay, over his shoulder. "That's two hands and a spanner and a morning, and the morning's the tell. A man's got to have time. Nobody lifts a shackle in a minute and puts it down in a minute and goes away, and if he did that on the Wednesday then he was out here on the Wednesday on somebody else's boat and not on mine, because I was on the north end and I know it."**

"Then he was out here on the Wednesday," said Nessa.

The third bright place was on the underside. And there was a thing on the frame that neither of them had looked at on the Wednesday because they had been looking at the shackle: a wire, and a place where a wire had been cut and mended, and the mend was three turns and a seizing wire and the seizing wire was new and the three turns were on the other side of the frame from the way this yard turns them.

"That is not our turn," Nessa said.

"That is not our turn," the man agreed. "That is a hand that goes the other way round, and it is the turn they do at the water side of a frame and not the landward, because you cannot get a spanner on the landward side in this mud, and everybody who works on the outside of a frame in this harbor turns that way, and the people who work at the water side of a frame — " he stopped, because there is a limit about four days down this coast and both of them knew it, and neither of them said the rest of it.

"Write what you saw," Nessa said, quietly, "and do not write what you were going to say."

He wrote what he saw. Three bright places, two of them the pin and the bow and the third on the underside where a shackle does not get handled unless it has been off and back on the wrong way up; a wire cut and mended with three turns and a seizing wire; and the three turns on the water side of the frame.

She read the slate back to herself before she turned it round, and the man of the second lay read it back over her shoulder, which he has done twice now and which she has entered both times because a second reader is a second reader and is not a smaller thing.

They came in at half past two and nothing else happened on the Tuesday, and the water was flat as a plate by then and the mole was full of people doing nothing, and the twenty-second week of that year went by without an envelope being made for it.

---

*First column, entered on Tuesday the twenty-third day of March next at about half past six in the evening in this shed: that at eight in the morning on the said Monday the wall behind that harbor authority's reeve's bench came down and came down bare, and that that was the forty-sixth Monday since the first of May past, and that the counter did not restart, and that the clerk kept his own page and did not mention it, and that no sitting was held in that room on that day or on any other day of this book.*

*And that on the said Monday the cistern was charged at no figure by four men this yard does not know and that party walked none of it, and that the walk is nil and stays nil, and that that is the thirty-first of the charges that party has entered walked on none of since the third of August past.*

*And that on the said Tuesday, being a wage Tuesday, the twenty-second week of the list of weeks fallen due with no envelope standing against them fell due, and that the wage column did not move and the twenty envelopes did not move, and that the reason is the reason of the twenty-ninth of December past and is not improved on.*

*And that on the said Tuesday a boat of about forty tons was hired at one mark and eightpence for the day with a man in her, and a penny of line was bought at a chandler's counter, and one mark and eightpence and a penny went out of that yard's own book at about half past four in the afternoon, and that the hire was on the man of the second lay's own account and for his own purpose and was not salvage and was not a claim and was not work under any instrument.*

*And that the holder of that writ offered to take the slate on the said Tuesday and was refused by the ship's hand, and that the said holder said so first, and that a private hire of a boat is not a place to put a man in the water, and that a mark is a mark and not a wreck and there is nothing under it anybody is looking for, and that the holder did not go in the water at any time of the day and had not gone in any water since the eighth of December past.*

*And that on the said Tuesday the shackle on the mark set on the face of the second seam on the twelfth of March past had three places on it where the iron was bright, being the pin and the bow and a third on the underside of the shackle where a shackle does not get handled unless it has been off the frame and back on it the wrong way up; and that the two places written on Wednesday the seventeenth are not struck out and Wednesday is written against them; and that a shackle off and on again is two hands and a spanner and a morning, and that the man of the second lay said that he was on the north end on the Wednesday and knew it, and that if the shackle was off on the Wednesday then whoever had it off was out on that ground on somebody else's boat and not on his.*

*And that a wire on the frame of the said mark had been cut and mended with three turns and a seizing wire, and that the three turns were on the water side of the frame, and that the turn this yard uses is the landward turn and is not the turn in that mend, and that the two of them are two turns and not one turn done twice.*

*Second column, not checkable from a mole, entered on the said Tuesday at about half past six in the evening in this shed, and no name is against it: that the mark is eleven days old and that there is about four inches of green on the weather side of it, and that four inches in eleven days is not a season's fouling, and that nobody in that boat said a word about what that is, and that the silence is entered here and is not improved on.*

*And that the man of the second lay began a sentence at the water side of a frame and did not finish it, and that the rest of that sentence is about four people alive in a town four days down this coast who own a case of five bells at about five and a half fathoms inside four hundred yards off the outer marks, and that the limit they wrote themselves and signed stands, and that the holder of that writ did not ask them to take it off and was not asked to and is not going to ask them this season or the next, and that no name of any of the four is in that book and none is going to be.*

*And that a mark which has been lifted and set again is a thing two parties have now got a claim to in their own mouths, and that a party that has a claim to a thing and cannot check the thing is a party with a second-column fact, and that this yard has been keeping a second column since the sixth of October past and is not going to stop.*

**One mark, eightpence and a penny went out of this yard on the said Tuesday and nothing came into it on either of the two days, and sixty-eight marks, one shilling and eight pence is in the bag, which is ten thousand nine hundred pence, and the figure the bag is short of the wage of four marks is ten thousand two hundred and sixty pence. The whole of what came into that yard in the twenty-one days ended on the said Tuesday is nothing, and the reason is the form of the fourth of March and not a mood. The whole of what went out of that yard in the eight days ended on the said Tuesday is one mark and eightpence and a penny of line, and the whole of what has gone out of that yard since the sixth of December past is five marks, four shillings and nine pence in eight transactions at five counters. Nothing in a tin since the twenty-eighth of April. Three marks owing to a man of Ashtide, unmoved, and he is not in that book and nobody was sent for him. Seven pence to that yard's own shipwright, unpaid. Eight shillings and tenpence to a woman of that harbor with her name on the figure. Eight marks and a day of a boat owing to that harbor's own bellwright since the twenty-third of May past, unargued, and he was not asked anything in these two days and the chest was not opened. Forty marks first after food to a person of no office, unpaid since the fifteenth of October past, and that yard has not asked her for anything since the twenty-sixth of December past and did not ask her in these two days. Forty marks stand whole to a boy of twenty-two, the three nots of the eighth of June past stand, the third column is not filled, nobody is appointed to it, and the boy's hand is at the head of it. The wage column stands at twenty-six lines and one hundred and four marks and twenty-two weeks have fallen due with no envelope standing against them. The chandler's note stands at thirty-one, not re-written, its three counts of weeks unmoved at twenty-two and thirteen and eleven, and it will not be said a fifth time. The notice of the thirty-first of October has its four clauses and has not been lifted, narrowed, re-served or altered, and no person, no boat and no lead of that yard has been inside its first clause on either of these two days, and no return of any kind was made out of either of them. The plate at the fifth seam is in eleven feet in that mole's own foundation stone and is not reached, not measured, not touched and not asked about, and the note against it stands open, and the only mechanism anybody has named for reaching it is a change of politics in an office and not a better letter out of a shed. The case of five bells is at about five and a half fathoms inside the four hundred yards off the outer marks, on nobody's schedule, was not raised and was not asked about in these two days, and the limit four people of that coast wrote and signed is quoted in that yard's own first column and stands. The two objects of nobody's are about two hundred and eighty-five days up the crown of that mole, about nine feet apart, under two tarpaulins, one with a corner off it since the seventeenth of June past and not lashed back, and neither has moved, and that party did not go near them on either of these two days. The bench under that pump is bare for a fifty-second night, counting from the Sunday of the thirty-first of January instant, and it is the only bare thing in that shed.**
