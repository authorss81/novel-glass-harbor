# Chapter 277

*The Outer Marks, a Bar That Is Only a Change in the Water, and Two Marks Two Shillings and Eightpence of Nothing*

Wednesday the twenty-first of April, a mile and a quarter off the head of that mole, and in the shed on that mole.

**The wall did not come down on the said Wednesday and the cistern was not charged on it, and the said Wednesday was not a wage Tuesday. Nothing came into that yard on the said Wednesday and three hundred and fifty-two pence went out of it.**

**And the far bench was not occupied on the said Wednesday at any hour, and the holder of that writ did not go over to him at any hour of it, and a man of sixty-one was not asked for anything and asked nothing.**

---

The price was named on the Tuesday evening and not on the Wednesday morning, and that is the whole of the difference between a hire and an act of will.

**The boat of the man of the second lay for a day, at one mark and eightpence, which is the figure a boat with a man in her is worth in this harbor and has been worth since the twelfth of March past. And a man of twenty-six hired by the day at a mark and two shillings, which is the figure this yard pays a hand by the day and has paid since the seventh of April last, and his name is Fen Barrow and he was on the hose and the pot line on the eighth, ninth and tenth and is off a Crown house book in Ternwatch and is not on a Crown house book from this shore.**

Fen Barrow said the figure back before he would take the oar.

"And nobody in the water," he said.

"**Nobody in the water and nobody on a lead and no pot and no gear and no compressor and nothing of that yard's in the sea at all. It is a boat and two men and a line and a compass, and the job is to go and stand beside two baulks of timber in the water a mile and a quarter off the head of that mole and say whether they are where a book says they are.**"

"Then I will have the tiller," said Fen Barrow.

---

They read the bar out loud at the boat before a single man put a foot in her, which is what this yard has done since the seventh of April past and is not going to stop doing, and it took under a minute and nobody improved on it.

**The outer marks are a mile and a quarter off the head of that mole. The bar is four hundred yards off the outer marks. The bar begins about a mile and a half out on the north-west line the outer marks are on, and the cut of the eighth, ninth and tenth of April past was off the south end and round the corner from all of it. The bar has not been narrowed by anything. This party is not going to ask to have it narrowed, did not ask for it in advance, and is not asking for it in this boat.**

"And that said, we are going one mile and a quarter," said the man of the second lay at the oar, "and the outer marks are a quarter of a mile inside it."

"**A quarter of a mile is a long way. I have been out there four times in ten days and the nearest I have been to that bar is a mile.**"

---

The outer marks are two baulks of green timber, a fathom and a half long and about fourteen inches square, lying on the bottom in about six feet at low water and showing at the top of a tide, with a ring of stone round them and a can of sheet iron on a stake driven into the middle one.

They lay alongside at about a quarter past ten on the flood and the man of the second lay took a bearing off the crown of the mole with a compass of his own, and then took it again from a different station, and then Fen Barrow read the marks off the chart in the boat's own locker and read them out.

"The chart in that locker is the *Anna Ray's* and it was drawn by a man who has been dead about thirty years," he said, "**and it puts the outer marks a mile and a quarter off the head of that mole on the north-west line, and we are about two points off that and about a cable's length to the southward of it, and I have got two of them and I would not sweat over the third.**"

"So they are where the book says," said Calder Marr.

"They are where the book says, and they are a good deal older than that book, and nobody has moved them, and that is the whole of what we came for."

---

And then the man of the second lay stood up in the bow of that boat and looked forward, north-west, past the marks, for about a minute and a half, and then he put his hand out flat on the gunwale at the spot where the change was.

"**There,**" he said. "**Do not look at the marks. Look past them. About two hundred yards beyond, where the water changes and stops being one thing.**"

It is there and anybody can see it on any tide of a rising sea and it does not need a boat to be in. It is a line of about a mile and a half long running north-north-west, and inside it the water is a shade darker and rougher and stands up in a low sea like a break, and outside it the water is flat and pale and does not care what the weather is doing.

"That is the bar," said the man of the second lay. "**That is what four hundred yards off the outer marks means. It is not a fence and there is no post in it and no man put it there. Somebody dropped two sets of marks on the bottom of a bay in the last hundred and fifty years and the tide works in and out of the space between them and it has made a step in the water and the step is what everybody in this coast means when they say the bar.**"

"How long does it stand there," said Calder Marr, "for a man in a boat with a compass and a chart in a locker?"

"**All day. Both sides of a spring tide. And you do not want to be on the inside of it in a bad blow off a SW, and I have told three men that in nineteen years and I have been right every time and the two of them remember it and the third one does not, and none of the three were ever inside it.**"

"Nobody in this boat is inside it and nobody in this boat is going inside it and the four of us are on the outer side of a line of white water and you can all see it from where you are sitting."

---

They were back on that mole at about half past two and they had it, and what they had was this:

**The outer marks are where a dead man's chart says they are. The bar is where a book says it is. Nobody has moved either of them and nobody has ever been inside the bar on this yard's account and this yard has now spent two marks, two shillings and eightpence to find out that nothing on that ground has changed since a man who has been dead about thirty years drew it.**

"Well," said the man of the second lay at the steps, and he was not thanking anybody and was not being thanked. "**That is two days of your time and mine and a man of twenty-six's, and you have bought the fact that there is nothing there.**"

"We have bought a line of white water a mile and a half long that a man in a boat can see and a man on a mole cannot, and that is in the first column, and it is checkable, and you could have told me the whole of it for nothing on the seventh of April past in a shed."

"**I could have, and I did not, and the reason I did not is that you would have written it down as something I said and there are three men in this harbor who have been telling me for nineteen years that the old ground is no good, and one of them is right and I do not know which and I have not got a marker to be right with.** Now you have got a marker. It cost you two marks two shillings and eightpence and it is the only instrument in this harbor that says the same thing twice."

---

And in the fourth column of the leaf ruled on the twenty-seventh of March past, that evening, in the ship's left hand, the ninth line:

*What it is not: — that a line of white water a mile and a half long is not a fence, and is not a bar of four hundred yards, and is not in anybody's book as anything but a distance from two baulks of timber; and that going and standing beside two baulks of timber is not a claim and is not a descent and is not a work of this yard under any paper, and that a day's boat and a day's hand is a day's boat and a day's hand and nothing else.*

---

*First column, entered on Wednesday the twenty-first day of April next at about half past nine in the evening in this shed: that the outer marks, being two baulks of green timber a fathom and a half long and about fourteen inches square with a ring of stone about them and a can of sheet iron on a stake in the middle one, lie a mile and a quarter off the head of that mole on the north-west line, in about six feet at low water, and that the said marks were laid alongside at about a quarter past ten in the morning on the flood and that two bearings were taken of the crown of that mole from that boat and a third from a different station, and that the figure is a mile and a quarter and about two points and a cable's length southward of the figure on a chart of about thirty years standing in the locker of the herring boat of the second lay, and that the said figures are within a cable's length of one another.*

*And that the bar of four hundred yards off the said outer marks is a line of about a mile and a half running north-north-west about two hundred yards beyond them, on which the water changes and stands up in a low sea, and that the said line was seen at about eleven in the morning of the said day from a boat about a mile and a quarter off the head of that mole, and that no person and no boat and no lead of that yard was inside the said bar on the said day or on any day, and that the said bar was not narrowed by anything and this party did not ask to have it narrowed and did not ask for it in advance and did not ask for it in the boat.*

*And that the price of the said day was named on the evening of the Tuesday before and not on the morning of the said day, being one mark and eightpence for the boat of the man of the second lay for a day and a mark and two shillings for a man of twenty-six hired by the day, and that three hundred and fifty-two pence went out of that yard's own book on the said evening, and that nothing came into it, and that nobody was in the water and nobody was on a lead and no pot and no gear and no compressor of that yard was in the sea at any hour of the said day.*

*And that one line was entered in the fourth column of the said leaf, being the ninth line in that column, being the line of the twenty-first day of April past.*

*Second column, not checkable from a mole, entered on the said Wednesday at about half past nine in the evening in this shed, and no name is against it: that a man of fifty-one of the second lay said out loud at the steps of that mole that this yard has bought the fact that there is nothing there, and that the said sentence is entered because a man said it on a step, and that what the man of about fifty-one said about three men in that harbor telling him for nineteen years that the old ground is no good is not in the first column, and that the reason against that line is that a man's memory of what he has been told is not a thing a man on a mole can check.*

*And that nothing was done on that ground after the said day, and that no figure was put on any wall, and that no letter was written, and that the reason against the line of all three of those is the reason of the twelfth day of April past and is not improved on.*

---

**Three hundred and fifty-two pence went out of this yard on the said Wednesday and nothing came into it, and nineteen marks, four shillings and ninepence is in the bag, which is three thousand and ninety-seven pence. The whole of the money that has come into that yard since the sixth of December past is one thousand seven hundred and sixty pence, and the whole of what went out of it since the sixth of December past is sixty-five marks, one shilling and eightpence in twenty-one transactions at seven counters, of which two are a penny at a box on the corner of that mole.**
