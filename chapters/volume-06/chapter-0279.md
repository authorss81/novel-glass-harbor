# Chapter 279

*A Letter of One Page Up the Hill, Three Dates Kept Apart, and Three Marks That Are Not Going to Be Paid*

Friday the twenty-third of April, in the shed on the mole.

**The wall did not come down on the said Friday and the cistern was not charged on it, and the said Friday was not a wage Tuesday, and nothing came into that yard on it and nothing went out of it, and a letter of one page came up that hill in the ordinary post and it cost that yard nothing.**

**And the far bench was not occupied on the said Friday at any hour, and the holder of that writ did not go over to him at any hour of it, and a man of sixty-one was not asked for anything and asked nothing.**

---

It came up in the middle of the morning and the envelope had been opened and closed once, and the sheet inside it was folded twice and the fold was soft, which means it had been in a pocket.

The hand is a young hand and it is a good hand. At the foot of it, in the same hand, are two names: **Wend Brace, thirty, and Ilse Brace, nineteen**, and the address is the address this yard wrote to on the third of April past.

He read it out. She had not asked him not to and she had not asked him to.

"**It says the paper came on the Tuesday,**" he said. "**The twentieth. It was not there in the morning and it was there in the afternoon and it was on the step against the door. That is the first date.**"

"Go on."

"**The second date: he came in from the water at about half past five on the Wednesday, the twenty-first, and it was still on the step where it had been since the Tuesday, and it had got wet and it had dried, and he stood in his own doorway and looked at it and did not go in and did not pick it up for about ten minutes. She says that is the part she is sorry about and she is not going to soften it. He was out of the day's work by that, being a hand hired by the day, and the day is three marks, and the boat's man is not in this letter and does not know that there is a letter.**"

"And the third date."

"**The third date: on the Thursday, the twenty-second, she read it out to him, and he had it read twice, and he cannot read, and she says that in this letter in those words, and she says it is not a thing to be sorry about and it is a thing that has to be said.**"

Nobody said anything for a bit.

"**And then the last of it,**" he said. "**She says: he has it on the table and he does not know what to do with it and there is nobody to ask. She says: I am nineteen and I read out a page in our kitchen in the autumn to about nine people and I have not read one out since and I am not going to. She says: if anybody is coming, tell them, because I will not have him find out from a stranger that they were already on the road. And she says: the three marks are gone and they are not coming out of this week either, and if the yard that sent it has three marks then that is a thing about the yard.**"

---

Nessa Pike had it before he had finished the last of it and she had the book open before he had finished the first of it, and she wrote the three dates in as three dates.

"**Say the reason for putting them in as three,**" she said.

"Because that is what a man can check. He cannot check it. He can check that this yard wrote three dates and did not make them one, and that is the whole difference between a first column and a second one, and if I put one date on that sheet then a man in four years reads one date and thinks it was all on one day, and it was not, and the difference between those two is three days and a man's afternoon and a man's day."

"And the three marks is a figure a man can check."

"**No,**" said Nessa Pike. "**Three marks is a figure the boat's man pays and not us, and we do not know the boat's man, and if I put that figure in the first column then in four years it is a figure in our book about a man we have never spoken to, and the day after that somebody comes to this shed with a paper and says the yard has had a man's wage off him. It goes in the second.**"

---

And Calder Marr read it twice and then did the thing that is the whole of the twenty-third of April.

"Send somebody," said the woman of no office. "**Send somebody down. Two days there and two days back and a bed, and a man in a house with a paper on his table and nobody to ask is a man who is going to do something stupid with it before the season turns.**"

"No."

"Calder."

"**No, and here is the reason and it is the reason of the second of April last and it is about a trade and it is not improved on. Every day I have been in this harbor I have said that a man asked on a mole whether he cuts answers it with a price, and this yard cannot pay a price, and it is not going to ask a man of sixty-one what he will take. That was about a trade. This is worse than a trade, because I have got three marks in front of me on a piece of paper and I could pay them, and paying them is the thing I am not going to do, and the reason is not that we have not got it.**"

"**Then say the reason, because I have watched you not say it twice this week.**"

"Because the day this yard pays that man three marks, he is a man this yard has paid, and a man this yard has paid is a man this yard can be asked about, and in about four years somebody is going to say to an office that this yard has an arrangement with a house two days down that coast. **We put a page in that man's daughter's mouth in the autumn without asking him. We have no arrangement with that house. We have got a girl of nineteen who read our thirty-eighth line out in a kitchen to about nine people and a form on her father's step, and that is two things and both of them are ours and neither of them is money.**"

"And the three marks."

"**The three marks are his and they are gone and I am not going to send them to him in a letter, because a letter with three marks in it is a letter that says the yard knows what his day is worth, and we do not know what his day is worth, he told us in a letter he has never seen us.**"

---

The woman of no office was in that shed and she had said nothing for about twenty minutes, and she said one thing at about half past one and it is in the first column in her words because she said it in a shed with the door open.

"**I carried that sheet eleven weeks,**" she said, "**and there is one thing about those three marks that neither of you has got to and I am going to say it and then I am going to go and shut the door. A man who cannot read who has had a piece of paper put on his step does not lose a day. He loses the four hundred years of being a person who is spoken to. He has been spoken to for four hundred years by people handing him things and saying do this and saying don't, and every one of those was a person in a room who could be argued with. And that is what a house is. And a house is the only kind of thing in this country you cannot refuse anybody anything about, and I have been trying to tell that to somebody all week and there has not been anybody in this country to tell.**"

"Nobody in this country to tell," said Calder Marr. "**Write that in the second column. Not in the first, because it is not a thing that was done, it is a thing that is the shape of this coast.**"

---

*First column, entered on Friday the twenty-third day of April next at about half past nine in the evening in this shed: that a letter of one page came up that hill in the ordinary post on the said morning in an envelope that had been opened and closed once, in a young hand, and that it was signed at the foot in the same hand with two names, being Wend Brace, thirty, and Ilse Brace, nineteen, and that the address on the said letter is the address that yard wrote to on the third day of April past, and that the said letter cost that yard nothing.*

*And that the said letter says three things and asks for nothing, and that the said three things are entered with their dates kept apart and were not made into one, being: that a sheet was on a step in that town on Tuesday the twentieth day of April past in the afternoon and was not there in the morning; that a man of about thirty came in from the water at about half past five on Wednesday the twenty-first and found it where it had lain since the Tuesday, wet and dried, and stood in his own doorway and did not pick it up for about ten minutes, and was by that out of a day's work, being a hand hired by the day at three marks, and that the boat's man is not in the said letter and does not know there is a letter; and that on Thursday the twenty-second the said letter was read out to him by his daughter and was read twice, and that he cannot read, and that the said letter says so in those words.*

*And that the said letter says that a daughter of nineteen read out a page in that kitchen in the autumn to about nine people and has not read one out since and is not going to, and that if anybody is coming the said letter is to be told, and that the three marks are gone and are not coming out of this week either, and that the said three marks are entered in the second column and not in the first because a man on a mole cannot check them and because the boat's man is not that yard's and is not named.*

*And that a woman of no office said out loud in that shed at about half past one in the afternoon, with the door open and about four people in the street, that a man who cannot read who has had a piece of paper put on his step loses four hundred years of being a person who is spoken to, and that the said sentence was entered in the first column in her words and that the door was shut after it.*

*And that nothing came into that yard on the said day and nothing went out of it, and that no person of that yard went to that town and none was sent, and that no letter was written to that town, and that no money was sent to that town.*

*Second column, not checkable from a mole, entered on the said Friday at about half past nine in the evening in this shed, and no name is against it: that three marks is a figure the boat's man pays and not that yard, that the boat's man is not named and is not of that yard and was not asked about, and that the said figure is not in the first column because the day after that somebody is going to say to an office that this yard has taken a man's wage off him.*

*And that no person was sent to that town and no money was sent to it, and that the reason against the line of that is that a man asked on a mole whether he cuts answers it with a price, and that this yard cannot pay a price and cannot be the reason it is asked, and that the said reason is the reason of the second of April last and is not improved on and is not about a family.*

*And that the said refusal is entered here and not in the first column, because refusing to pay is not a thing a man did and because the whole of what a first column is for is that a man can go and look at it.*

*And that a page of that yard's own printed sheet was read out in a kitchen in that town in the autumn to about nine people without anybody asking the man of the household, and that a form of an office that yard does not belong to was on a step in that town on the twentieth day of April past, and that the two of those are the whole of what has happened at that address in six months, and that the said two are entered as two and are not made into one, and that the said sentence is the reason against the line of sending anybody and it is not improved on and is not going to be.*

*And that the four lines of the sixth day of March past say that the keeper is not to be told where the page is, and that this yard put its own thirty-eighth line into a kitchen in that town in the autumn, and that a kitchen is a place people are told things in, and that the reason against the line of the six lines of the sixth of March is that they are about a record and about a drawer and about four people who knew what a record was, and that a kitchen in a house in a town is not a record and is not a drawer, and that this yard did not know that on the sixth of March and knows it now and it is entered here and not in the first column because knowing it is not a thing a man did.*

---

**Nothing came into this yard on the said Friday and nothing went out of it, and nineteen marks, four shillings and ninepence is in the bag, which is three thousand and ninety-seven pence, and that is the same figure it was on Thursday night. The whole of the money that has come into that yard since the sixth of December past is one thousand seven hundred and sixty pence, and there is nothing else. The whole of what went out of that yard since the sixth of December past is sixty-five marks, one shilling and eightpence in twenty-one transactions at seven counters. The wage column stands at twenty-six lines and one hundred and four marks and twenty-seven weeks have fallen due with no envelope standing against them, and the next wage Tuesday is the twenty-seventh of April next.**
