# Chapter 272

*A Person of No Office of This Harbor, Thirty, and a Line of the Fourth of April That Is Not Struck Out*

Friday the sixteenth of April, in the shed on the mole, and on the mole steps.

**The wall did not come down on the said Friday and the cistern was not charged on it, and the said Friday was not a wage Tuesday, and nothing came into that yard on it and nothing went out of it, and the figure in the bag is the figure it was on Thursday night, and that is the first night running in which nothing has moved in it since the penny went out on the Thursday.**

**And the far bench was occupied on the said Friday from about ten until about two and was not occupied at any other hour of it, and the holder of that writ did not go over to him at any hour of the said Friday, and a man of sixty-one was not asked for anything and asked nothing.**

---

She came up the hill on the Friday morning in a cart with a man who wanted to come to this harbor, and she paid him her own half, and she said so at the door before anybody asked her, and that is entered.

She is thirty. She has been of this harbor since the fourth of January past, and she came down the coast in the autumn, and she has been of no office since the fourth of January past, and before that she was in an office for nine years, and there is a woman in that office still and the woman and she have not spoken since the fourth of January.

Nessa Pike put the two of them straight and it took about a minute and it was not done gently.

"**You are of this harbor.** You have been of this harbor since the fourth of January. You came down the coast in the autumn and you were in three rooms over a shop at the back of the market and you are in them now. And on the fourth of April past I wrote a line in the second column of that book which said your letter came from a person of no office four days up that coast, and that line is wrong."

"That is not your line to be sorry about," said the woman, "and it is not mine to be sorry about either, because I was not in the room when you wrote it."

"**I know whose line it is, and it is in my book, and my book is what people find out about me in about four years and not before.** Read it."

She read it. It is eight words and a date and it does not take long.

"Right," she said. "**I am of this harbor. Put that in the first column in full, with the date, and do not strike the fourth of April out. A yard which strikes out its own line because it was wrong is a yard which has decided that a wrong line is not a record. It is a record. A man in four years has got to be able to see that this yard wrote it, and that it was wrong, and that this yard knew it was wrong on the sixteenth of April and put the other one beside it.**"

"That is the third column's business," said Nessa Pike, "and the third column is not to be filled, and nobody is appointed to it, and I am not touching it. I will put your name and your age and your town in the first column and the fourth of April's line in the second with this written against it, and I will not put your name in the third column and neither will you."

---

And then she put it on the bench.

At about eleven she had it inside her coat against her arm, folded in four, and she took it out and squared it on the bench with both hands the way a person does with a plate.

It is a single sheet and it is printed, and the print is old enough that the ink has gone brown at the edges of the letters without anybody having rubbed it, and at the head of it, in the type, it says:

**PARTICULARS OF MATERIAL HELD AS UNSETTLED**

and under the head there are five ruled lines and the first of them has been filled in and the other four are blank, and the first line has a name on it and a place under the name, and the place is a house in a town two days down that coast.

At the foot of it, under a rule, there is a line that says **SERVED** and there is nothing after the word, and below that there is a seal line, and **there is a name on the seal line, and the name is Halven Rook.**

"He is a name on a sheet," said the woman. "**I have put that name in this yard's book and he is a name on a sheet and he is not a man in this shed and he is not a man on this coast, and a name on a piece of paper is a thing a man can go and look at, and I have looked at it.**"

"Who filled in the first line," said Calder Marr.

"**I am not going to tell you that, and you did not ask me for it, and I want it noticed that you did not.** It is a young man in that office and he is not a bad man and he is frightened of a thing he is not allowed to name, and if I tell you his name then in about two years you will have a person in a book and in about four years a man in a room, and he did nothing but fill in five lines on a sheet."

"All right," he said.

"**Do not say all right like that.** I have refused you something and you have taken it and I want it entered as refused and not as not-asked, because they are different and you will not remember the difference."

---

Nessa Pike wrote it in the second column, in her own left hand, while the woman watched, and the line is nineteen words long and it says that the woman of no office refused to name the person who filled in the first line of the said sheet, and that nobody asked her twice, and that the reason against the line of the asking is a young man in an office who is frightened of a thing he is not allowed to name.

"Why is it not a bad thing to have a name in a book," said the woman. "You have a book with two columns and a fourth one with seven lines in it that nobody in this harbor can check, and you have put a man's name into the first column of that one, and a man of twenty-two's hand is at the head of the third. I have read your name on the bottom of a thing you did not sign. **You are frightened of names and you are frightened of pages and you have never once in your life been frightened of a woman with a form in her coat, and I would like somebody to tell me what to do about that.**"

"I cannot tell you what to do about that," said Calder Marr, "and I have known for about nine days and I have not got past it, and the reason is on the second of April and it is the same reason in your own words and I have not improved on it either."

She looked at the sheet on the bench for a while.

"How long have you had that," he said.

"Eleven weeks," said Mara Venn. "**Since the fourth of January. And I have not been able to put it in a drawer and I have not been able to burn it and I have not been able to take it to a post office and I have not been able to hand it to a yard. That is four things and I have tried three of them.**"

---

*First column, entered on Friday the sixteenth day of April next at about half past nine in the evening in this shed: that a woman of no office, thirty, of this harbor, came up that hill on the said morning in a cart with a man who wanted to come to that harbor, and that she paid her own half of that fare and said so at that door before anybody asked her; and that she has been of this harbor since the fourth of January past and came down that coast in the autumn; and that the second column of the said book's entry of the fourth day of April past says that a letter came from a person of no office four days up that coast, and that line is wrong, and that it was not struck out, and that the said line is entered in the second column with the said Friday's date written against it and the reason against the line.*

*And that a single printed sheet was brought up that hill on the said morning and put on the bench in that shed at about eleven in the morning and squared on the said bench with both hands, being a form headed in print as particulars of material held as unsettled, with five ruled lines under the head, of which the first is filled in with a name and a place and the place is a house in a town two days down that coast, and with a line at the foot of the said sheet which says served and has nothing after the word, and with a seal line under that on which there is a name, and the name is Halven Rook, and the said name was entered in that first column because a name on a piece of paper is a thing a man can go and look at.*

*And that the said woman refused to name the person who filled in the first line of the said sheet, and that nobody asked her twice, and that the said refusal is entered in the second column and not in the first, and that the reason against the line of the asking is entered with it.*

*And that the said woman said out loud in that shed that she has had the said sheet eleven weeks and since the fourth of January past, and that she has not been able to put it in a drawer and has not been able to burn it and has not been able to take it to a post office and has not been able to hand it to a yard, and that those are four things and that she has tried three of them, and that the said four things were entered in the first column in her words.*

*And that the far bench was occupied on the said Friday from about ten until about two and was not approached, and that a man of sixty-one was not asked for anything and asked nothing.*

*Second column, not checkable from a mole, entered on the said Friday at about half past nine in the evening in this shed, and no name is against it: that a woman of no office refused to name a young man in an office who filled in five lines on a sheet, and that the said young man is not a bad man and is frightened of a thing he is not allowed to name, and that the reason against the line of the asking is that a name in a book is a name in a room in about four years.*

*And that the sheet the said woman carried is one that has not been served, and that nothing on it is a thing a man in this harbor can go and look at, and that the reason against that line is that the sheet is in a town two days down that coast in a coat against a woman's arm and that this party is not going after it, and that the not-going is entered here and not in the first column.*

*And that the said woman said in that shed that this yard is frightened of names and frightened of pages and has not been frightened of a woman with a form in her coat, and that she asked to be told what to do about that, and that nobody in that shed could tell her, and that the not-telling is entered here.*

---

**Nothing came into this yard on the said Friday and nothing went out of it, and twenty-four marks, seven shillings and sixpence is in the bag, which is three thousand nine hundred and thirty pence, and that is the same figure it was on Thursday night. The whole of the money that has come into that yard since the sixth of December past is one thousand seven hundred and sixty pence, being the eleven marks of the sixth of April last, and there is nothing else. The whole of what went out of that yard since the sixth of December past is fifty-nine marks, twelve shillings and threepence in eighteen transactions at seven counters.**
