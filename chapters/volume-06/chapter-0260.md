# Chapter 260

*Two Letters Crossing, and a Party That Has Become One Address More*

Saturday the third and Sunday the fourth of April, in the shed on the mole, and at the post on the corner.

**And the far bench was occupied on the said Sunday from about ten until about four and was not occupied on the said Saturday at any hour, and the holder of that writ did not go over to him on either of the two days, and a man of sixty-one was not asked for anything on either of them and asked nothing on either of them, and no fragment and no report talk and no second story.**

**And no day of work was booked in this yard on either of the two days, and the cistern was not charged on either of them, and the wall behind the reeve's bench did not come down on either of them, and the last wipe was the forty-seventh on the twenty-ninth of March and the next is the forty-eighth on the fifth of April, and the last wage Tuesday was the thirtieth of March and the number of weeks fallen due with no envelope standing against them is TWENTY-THREE and did not move on either of the two days.**

**And one penny of postage went out of this yard at a box on the corner of that mole on the said Saturday at about half past three in the afternoon, and a letter came up that hill in the ordinary post on the said Friday from a person of no office and was read on the said Saturday, and nothing was asked of anybody and nothing was answered and the sheet it went with is not in that post.**

---

The man was about thirty and he could not read, and he is a man of this yard in about forty people's hearing and is not one, and he has a daughter of nineteen who goes to the kitchen where the sheet was read.

Nessa Pike wrote the letter on the Saturday morning at the bench, in her own left hand, and she wrote it slowly and she did not read it to anybody while she wrote it, and she read it back twice to herself before she folded it, which is the practice of that shed and which is the only thing this book has ever offered in place of confidence.

It said three things and it asked for nothing.

**The first:** that on the twelfth of March this yard set a mark on the face of the second seam, and that it was looked at on the seventeenth of that month, and on the twenty-third, and on the twenty-sixth; and that on the seventeenth it was found fouled on the weather side with about four inches of green and the shackle with two places on it about the size of a thumbnail where the iron was bright; and that on the twenty-third it was found fouled to the same depth, and the shackle with three such places, the third of them on the underside of it, which is a place a shackle does not get bright unless it has been off the frame and back on it the wrong way up, and a wire on the frame that had been cut and mended; and that on the twenty-sixth it was found fouled to about a foot, and a line upon it that was not this yard's and a flat of about a quarter of an inch across the crown of the bow of the shackle, which is not a shackle this yard fits; and that the dates are the dates and a person can come and look at the mark any day of a week at the face of that seam and can see for himself that there is a shackle on it and a wire on it and a line upon it.

**The second:** that the thirty-eighth line of that yard's printed sheet says where every other copy of that sheet is, and that the copy of that sheet which went into a kitchen in that town two days down this coast was read out once to about nine people and has not been read out since and is not going to be read out again, and that this yard has not sent anybody to that kitchen and is not going to.

**And the third:** that whoever put that sheet in that kitchen did a thing this yard did not ask for and cannot undo, and that this yard wrote to him about the mark and not about the sheet, and that if he wants the sheet taken down off the wall it is not on a wall in that town, it is in a kitchen, and nobody in that kitchen is a keeper of it and neither is this yard.

**"You are not signing it," said Calder Marr.**

"No."

"Put my name at the bottom. He will not be able to read a word of it and there is not one person in that town who could tell whose hand it is, and a letter with a name on it is a letter that can be produced in a room by a clerk who says *that is the man*."

"That is exactly what a name on it is for, and it is a good reason." She put the nib down. "And here is the other one, and it is the real one, and I have been sitting on it since Friday. A name on that letter is this yard's name on a piece of paper about a mark that two parties have a claim to, going to a town two days down this coast, and four days down that coast there are four people who keep a page in a drawer and have written a limit themselves about what is asked of them. If that letter has a name on it, then in about two years a man in an office can say: this yard wrote to that town. And then somebody can say: and they are the yard with the mark. And then in about four years there is an office with a file, and the file has got a town in it, and the town has got us in it."

"So the letter goes with no name on it."

"The letter goes with no name on it, and it will be read by whoever it is handed to, and if it is thrown in a fire then it is in a fire, and either of those is a thing a person can do with a letter and this yard cannot prevent and would not want to." She folded it. "And I am going to write one line at the bottom that is not my hand and not yours either, and it is the name of the man of the second lay, because he has been on that water nineteen years and if that letter is ever read by anybody who knows anything then it will be read by somebody who knows him, and he does not mind his name being on it because he has not got a name anybody would want."

**It went to the box on the corner at about half past three on the Saturday afternoon and it cost a penny, and that penny is the whole of what that yard has spent in one day on anybody's account, and the last figure that went out of that yard before it was two marks and eightpence on the Friday before, and it is the first thing that yard has put in the ordinary post since the four pages went out on the sixth of March.**

---

The other letter came up the hill on the Friday in the ordinary post, and it was one page, and it was in a hand that any person in that harbor would know and no person in that harbor had seen for five months.

It said one fact and it made no request.

It said that an office she had worked in for nine years has a form, and that the form is for material the office calls unsettled, and that a person who keeps unsettled material in a house is a keeper of it under that form, and that whether that means a person's house or a person's drawer or a person's cupboard the form does not say, and that she has not seen that form served on anybody and cannot say what it does when it is, and that she is a person of no office and has been since the fourth of January past, and that if this harbor wants her she will come, and that she would come if a person asked her and would not come if a yard asked her, and that she is not able to say what the difference is between those two things and that she has thought about it for three days and has not got round to it.

**Nothing was asked of her. Nobody asked her. That is entered in the first column and it is entered with the reason against the line, and the reason is that a yard which asks a person of no office for a thing is a yard which has made itself an address, and this yard made itself an address once this week and is not going to do it twice in a week.**

"You are not writing back," said Calder Marr.

"No."

"Not even to say the post is slow."

"**No, and I want that in the second column and not the first,**" said Nessa, "because a thing that is in the first column is a thing that was done, and not writing back is not a thing that was done. It is a thing that did not happen, and I have never in my life entered a thing that did not happen in the first column, and I am not going to start on the day a woman of no office writes a better sentence than this book has managed in five volumes."

---

The far bench was occupied on the Sunday from about ten until about four, and the holder of that writ was in the shed from about nine and did not go over to him, and neither did the ship's hand, and neither did the boy, who came down at about eleven on his own account because he had not been on the stone since the fifteenth of May and there was nothing on the stone to be on.

**And the leaf with four columns had one entry in it, in the first column, in the ship's left hand, and the second column was empty, and the third was empty, and the fourth was empty under its ragged head, and the reason it was empty was entered on the same line as the head and read: *because nobody has done a thing yet and refused it, and there is one entry in this book that I have not refused and I am not going to pretend to.***

The mark was fouled a foot and a half on the weather side by the Sunday afternoon, on the word of nobody, because nobody went and looked at it, and there was a line on it that was not theirs, and there were twenty-three days on it, and the fouling of a thing left alone on this coast in water at this temperature comes up to a season in about nine weeks.

"That is the fourteenth of May," said Calder Marr, at about four o'clock, with the light going. "Not the ninth of May, not the first of June. The fourteenth of May. Nine weeks from the day we set it is the day our own fouling would be a season's fouling if we could have paid for a man to go and look at it, and we cannot, and it will be a foot and a half and it will not be a season's."

"Write that down."

"Write what down?"

"The fourteenth of May. That is a date a man can go and look at a mark and see whether it is true, and that is a better thing than anything anybody in this shed has said this month, and it does not need a witness and it does not need an office and it does not need us."

She wrote it in the fourth column, which had been empty since Saturday morning, under a head written with a blunt nib, and it is the only thing in that column in the whole of this batch.

*What it is not: — the fourteenth of May is not a date on which the fouling will be a season's. It is a date on which anybody who goes and looks can see that it is not, and that is all it is.*

---

*First column, entered on Sunday the fourth day of April next at about half past nine in the evening in this shed: that on the said Saturday a letter of one page went into the ordinary post at a box on the corner of that mole at about half past three in the afternoon and cost a penny, addressed to a man of about thirty who cannot read in a town two days down that coast, and that it said three things and asked for nothing, being what was found on that mark on the seventeenth, the twenty-third and the twenty-sixth of March and the three dates; that the copy of the printed sheet of thirty-eight lines which went into a kitchen in that town was read out once to about nine people and has not been read out since and is not going to be; and that whoever put that sheet in that kitchen did a thing this yard did not ask for and cannot undo.*

*And that the holder of that writ did not sign the said letter, and that the reason against that line is that a name on a letter is a letter a clerk can produce in a room and say of, and that the shipwright gave that reason and gave a second one, being that a name on a piece of paper about a mark that two parties have a claim to, going out of this yard to a town two days down that coast and near a town four days down it where four people keep a page in a drawer, is this yard's name in an office's file in about two years, and that the said letter is signed at the foot in the hand of the man of the second lay and not in the hand of either of the other two, and that he does not mind his name being on it.*

*And that on the said Friday a letter of one page came up that hill in the ordinary post from a person of no office who has been of no office since the fourth of January past, and that it was read on the said Saturday, and that it said that an office she worked in for nine years has a form for material that office calls unsettled, and that a person who keeps unsettled material in a house is a keeper of it under that form, and that whether that means a house or a drawer or a cupboard the form does not say, and that she has not seen that form served on anybody and cannot say what it does when it is, and that she will come to that harbor if a person asks her and will not come if a yard asks her, and that she is not able to say what the difference is between those two things and has thought about it for three days and has not got round to it.*

*And that nothing was asked of her, and that nobody asked her, and that nothing was written back, and that the reason against the line of the not-writing-back is that a yard which asks a person of no office for a thing is a yard which has made itself an address, and that this yard made itself an address once on the said Saturday and is not going to do it twice in a week.*

*And that the far bench was occupied on the said Sunday from about ten until about four and was not occupied on the said Saturday, and that the holder of that writ did not go over to him on either of the two days, and that a man of sixty-one was not asked for anything on either of them and asked nothing on either of them, and that there was no fragment and no report talk and no second story.*

*And that the fourth leaf ruled on the twenty-seventh of March had, at the end of the said Sunday, one entry in its first column, being the mark set on the twelfth of March, fouled on the weather side, shackle off and on again, wire cut and mended on the water side, a line that is not this yard's and a flat on the crown of the bow, and that its second column and its third column were empty, and that its fourth column had one line in it, and that the said line is the fourteenth of May.*

*Second column, not checkable from a mole, entered on the said Sunday at about half past nine in the evening in this shed, and no name is against it: that not writing back to a person of no office is not a thing that was done and is not entered in the first column, because the first column is a record of what a man did and not a record of what a man did not do, and that the ship's hand said that out loud and said that she has never entered a thing that did not happen in the first column in her life and is not going to start on the day a woman of no office writes a better sentence than this book has managed in five volumes.*

*And that a letter is a channel, and that a channel is a door, and that this yard has refused for five volumes to be an address, and that on the said Saturday it became one address more, and that it did it for a man who cannot read, and that there is no figure for that and there is not going to be one, and that a yard which can be written to is a yard that can be asked about, and that this yard has said out loud in a market in about forty people's hearing that it cannot make any one of four communities trust it and has not tried, and that a letter is not a way of making anybody trust anybody and is the opposite of a way of making anybody trust anybody.*

*And that two letters went in opposite directions in one week, and that one of them was posted on the said Saturday with a penny of postage and no signature but a fisherman's, and the other came up the hill on the said Friday from a person of no office four days up that coast, and that neither has been answered, and that this yard is not going to write to that man again, and that if that woman writes again it will be read on the day it comes and it will be read before anybody is asked anything about it.*

**One penny went out of this yard on the said Saturday and nothing came into it on either of the two days and nothing went out of it on the said Sunday, and sixty-five marks and threepence is in the bag, which is ten thousand four hundred and three pence, and the figure the bag is short of the wage of four marks is nine thousand seven hundred and sixty-three pence. The whole of what came into that yard in the twenty days ended on the said Sunday is NOTHING, and the reason is the form of the fourth of March and not a mood, and that is the first twenty-day run in five volumes in which nothing came into that yard at all. The whole of what went out of that yard in the twenty days ended on the said Sunday is five marks, two shillings and ten pence, and the whole of what has gone out of that yard since the sixth of December past is eight marks, six shillings and two pence in eleven transactions at five counters, of which three are the boat of the man of the second lay at one mark and eightpence a day with a penny of line, and one is two marks and eightpence to a man of about thirty-four of a pressure house at Ternwatch. Nothing in a tin in that shed since the twenty-eighth of April. Three marks owing to a man of Ashtide, unmoved, and he is not in that book and nobody was sent for him and no name of the four people of that coast is in that book. Seven pence owing to that yard's own shipwright, unpaid. Eight shillings and tenpence to a woman of that harbor with her name on the figure. Eight marks and a day of a boat owing to that harbor's own bellwright since the twenty-third of May past, unargued, and he was not asked anything in these twenty days and the chest was not opened. Forty marks first after food to a person of no office, unpaid since the fifteenth of October past, and a letter came up that hill in the ordinary post on the said Friday and nothing was asked of her and nothing was written back. Forty marks stand whole to a boy of twenty-two, the three nots stand, the third column is not filled, nobody has been appointed to it and nobody is going to be, the boy's hand is at the head of it, and the boy was on the mole on the said Sunday at about eleven on his own account and was not on the stone. The wage column stands at twenty-six lines and one hundred and four marks with the fifth of March the oldest, twenty envelopes on that bench, and twenty-three weeks have fallen due with no envelope standing against them, being the whole list of that year, and the next wage Tuesday is the sixth of April. Thirty-one on the chandler's note, not re-written, its three counts of weeks unmoved at twenty-two and thirteen and eleven, and it will not be said a fifth time. The notice of the thirty-first of October has its four clauses and has not been lifted, narrowed, re-served or altered, and no person, no boat and no lead of that yard has been inside its first clause on any of these twenty days, and no return of any kind was made out of any of them, and there are four returns and there is no fifth. The season of the harbor authority ended on the thirty-first of March and no instrument has been issued out of that office since. The plate at the fifth seam is in eleven feet in that mole's own foundation stone and is not reached, not measured, not touched and not asked about on any of these twenty days, and the note against it stands open, and the name of the place at seventy fathoms is not on a page of that book. The case of five bells is at about five and a half fathoms inside the four hundred yards off the outer marks, on nobody's schedule, was not raised and was not asked about on any of these twenty days, and the limit four people of that coast wrote and signed stands and the holder did not ask to have it lifted once in twenty days. The delayed note stands at eleven logged occasions and two of them with no call in them, and nothing was entered on any of these twenty days and nothing was measured, and the delay is a hand's width of a second and has not moved. The two objects of nobody's are about two hundred and ninety-seven days up the crown of that mole, about nine feet apart, under two tarpaulins, and neither has moved, and that party did not go near them on any of these twenty days. The bench under that pump is bare for a sixty-fourth night, counting from the Sunday of the thirty-first of January instant, and it is the only bare thing in that shed. Nothing went up on any wall in that harbor in these twenty days and nothing came off, and the fourth leaf does not go on that wall and never will. A working harbor of that coast is poorer on the fourth day of April next than it was on the twenty-third day of February past, and one herring berth of two is out, the third lay filled on the first of April and is a lay short, the north berth is out, the mole-head light is not on because a light costs oil, the fire party's hose is dead until the spring, the tide gang is four men short, the hard at Ashtide is unanswered, and a reeve's office has shut for the season. And a yard on that mole has been twenty days without a paper of any kind, and has looked at a mark of its own three times and paid a man's day three times for it, and has ruled a leaf with four columns on the back of a sheet of chandler's paper, and has sent one letter to a man who cannot read and received one from a woman of no office, and neither has been answered, and there is nobody in this country to ask.**
