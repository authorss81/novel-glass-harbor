# Chapter 262

*Twenty-Four Weeks on a Tuesday, and the First Figure That Has Come Into That Yard in Twenty-Three Days*

Tuesday the sixth of April, in the shed on the mole, and at the head of that mole.

**The said Tuesday was a wage Tuesday and the wage was not paid, and the twenty-fourth week fell due with no envelope standing against it, and the column stands where it stood on Monday and did not move a line, and the twenty envelopes are on the bench where they have been since the third week of March past.**

**And the wall behind the reeve's bench did not come down on the said Tuesday, because the wall comes down on Mondays, and the cistern was not charged on the said Tuesday, because the cistern is charged on Mondays and Thursdays, and no figure is entered against a day this party did not see.**

**And no day of work was booked in this yard on the said Tuesday, and eleven marks came into it at about ten in the morning from a man of the second lay, and that is the first money that has come into that yard since the fourteenth day of March past.**

---

Elias Voke put it on the bench in coin and did not put it down out of a coat, which told Nessa Pike more about him than the whole of the morning's talking had.

Sixteen hundred and seventy-six pence, being eleven marks, in threes and fives and a great many ones, because a man who has not had a sum of money in his hand for a season does not arrive with it in fives.

She counted it twice, and then she read it back to him as a figure, because that is the practice of that shed, and he counted it back to her to check her counting, and they both stopped at the same figure, and the figure was correct.

"**That is a fool's arithmetic and it is the right arithmetic,**" he said. "I have had men on that water take half of that for a bad night's dragging. I am not arguing about it. I am going to be gone by six so that I can be on my own ground at the top of the tide, and you will not see me again until I have something to tell you or until you have made a mess of it."

"You may see me before either of those," said Calder Marr.

"You may," said Voke, "and I will not hold you to it, and that is the last soft thing I am going to say today."

Then he asked the one question he had come to ask, and he asked it to Calder Marr and not to the ship's hand, which was the right of the two.

"**Who comes up if it goes wrong.**"

"That is not a question about a man," said Calder Marr. "That is a question about a price, and I am going to give you the price and not the name, and I will give you both by Wednesday, and the price will be named before it is agreed, and I am telling you that now so that you cannot say afterwards that it was sprung on you."

"**I have been in a boat with a thing go wrong**," said Elias Voke, "and I want the man to be somebody who was not in the boat. I do not want a man who is good at it. I want a man who is somewhere else and does not have to be fetched, because the fetching is the part that kills people in this trade and everybody in it will tell you so and none of them will say it out loud in a doorway."

"Tomorrow," said Calder Marr.

"Good," said Voke, and went down the mole at ten minutes past ten.

---

Then the twenty-fourth week.

Nessa Pike wrote it in the book under the twenty-third, and the twenty-third stood where it had stood since the thirtieth of March, and the column of figures beside the weeks did not move, and the twenty envelopes on the bench did not move, and nothing was in them.

"You wrote on the thirtieth of March that twenty-three was the whole of that year's list," she said. "And that a man can go and look at the thirtieth of March and see that you wrote it, and it is in your hand and it is not struck out, and it is not going to be struck out."

"Then I will write beside it," said Calder Marr. "**The thirtieth of March said that twenty-three was the whole of that year's list. Twenty-three was the whole of that year's list to the thirtieth day of March. The list has a week in it for this morning.** And that is a sentence about a list and not about a year, and I wrote the wrong one on a Wednesday afternoon with a bad hand and I am not going to rub it out, because a page a man cannot check is worth less than a page a man can look at, and this one can be looked at."

"It is entered in the first column," said Nessa Pike, "with both dates on it, and the twenty-fourth week is in the first column and the figure of the column is not, because there is no figure of the column, and the envelopes are twenty and have been twenty since the third week of March past."

---

And then Garrow Fisk, who had gone down to the packet and come back up with the tide, and had the set engaged for three days, and had it booked for the eighth, the ninth and the tenth, and stood in the shed door with a paper in his hand and did not go further in.

"Nine marks a day for three days," he said, "and the book at Ternwatch has it, and the book does not have your name on it, because a Crown house book is a Crown house book and a man with no instrument is not a party to one, and I have written the name of the yard on the sheet and not in the book, and I am telling you that I wrote it on the sheet, because if I did not tell you and you found it out in a year you would be right to be angry and I would be right to be quiet."

"That is a fair way round," said Calder Marr.

"The man off the book is **Amos Searle, twenty-eight**, and he is a holder and he has been a holder four years, and he is the best hand on that coast and he does not know this harbor and has not been asked to come." Fisk turned the paper over. "And the other thing on the sheet is this. The book has a column for a depth. It has no column for anything else. Not a year, not a name, not a reason. **A Crown house book can tell you what a man went to and it cannot tell you why, and I have thought about that a great deal in the last three days and I have got no better than that.**"

"No," said Calder Marr. "There is no better than that. Write it in the second column."

"It is in the second column," said Fisk, "in a hand that is not yours, and I have not signed it and I am not going to, and if you want it signed you can ask me again on Friday and I will think about it on Saturday."

---

And in the afternoon Nessa Pike brought up the letter of the second of April and put it on the bench face down, which is how it had lain since Saturday, and said that she had been thinking about it for two days and had got to the bottom of it and did not like it.

"It is a form," she said. "That is all it is. An office has a form for material the office calls unsettled, and a person who keeps unsettled material in a house is a keeper of it under that form, and she has never seen it served on anybody and cannot say what it does when it is. That is the whole of it. It is a form and not a favour, and it is the truth she sent, and I do not doubt a word of it."

"No," said Calder Marr. "I do not either."

"Then here is what I have got to the bottom of." She put two fingers on the back of the letter. "**We are an address.** That is what Saturday was. We were not an address for five volumes and on Saturday we became one, in an ordinary post, at a penny, to a man who cannot read, and every address in this country can be given to a man who is going somewhere. And I do not care what the form is. I care that we did that to ourselves, in writing, in a day, for a good reason, and that a good reason is the reason people use when they have not thought about the fourth year."

"And we are not going to write to her," said Calder Marr.

"**We are not going to write to her, and I am not going to, and you are not going to, and neither of us is going to walk up that coast road and stand on a doorstep.** And it goes in the second column, because not writing is not a thing that was done."

"That is twice this week that column has had a refusal in it," said Calder Marr, "and once last week, and I am beginning to think the column is more use than the first one and that is not a thing this yard has ever been able to say out loud."

"You cannot put it in the first column," said Nessa Pike. "**The first column is a record of what a man did. Not writing to a woman of no office is not a thing a man did. It is a thing a man did not do, and I have never entered it in the first column in my life and I am not going to start on the day a form in a city we have never seen in is the reason.**"

---

*First column, entered on Tuesday the sixth day of April next at about half past nine in the evening in this shed: that a man of fifty-one of the second lay, who gave his name as Elias Voke, put one thousand seven hundred and seventy-six pence on that bench at about ten in the morning on the said Tuesday, being eleven marks, in threes and fives and a great many ones, and that it was counted twice by the ship's hand and read back to him as a figure and counted back by him, and that both counts stopped at the same figure and that figure was correct.*

*And that the said man asked one question, being who comes up if it goes wrong, and that the holder of that writ answered that it was not a question about a man and was a question about a price, and that the price would be named before it was agreed and would be given by the Wednesday, and that the said man said that he wanted a man who was not in the boat and did not want a man who was good at it, and that the fetching is the part that kills people in that trade.*

*And that a compressor, a housing, a pipe, gauges, and one man off a Crown house book were engaged for three days on the said Tuesday at nine marks a day, and that the days named were the eighth, the ninth and the tenth of that month, and that the name of the man off that book is Amos Searle, twenty-eight, a holder four years, and that he is the best hand on that coast and does not know that harbor and has not been asked to come; and that the name of that yard is on a sheet at that pressure house and is not in that book, because a Crown house book is not a book a party with no instrument is a party to, and that the said man said out loud that he wrote the name on the sheet and not in the book.*

*And that the said man said that a Crown house book has a column for a depth and no column for a year, a name or a reason, and that a Crown house book can tell a man what a man went to and cannot tell him why, and that the said line was entered in the second column in a hand that is not that of the ship's hand, unsigned.*

*And that the twenty-fourth week fell due on the said Tuesday with no envelope standing against it, and that the column stands at twenty-six lines and one hundred and four marks and did not move, and that there are twenty envelopes on that bench; and that the holder of that writ said out loud that the thirtieth day of March last said that twenty-three was the whole of that year's list, and that twenty-three was the whole of that year's list to the thirtieth day of March, and that the list has a week in it for the said morning, and that the sentence of the thirtieth is not struck out.*

*Second column, not checkable from a mole, entered on the said Tuesday at about half past nine in the evening in this shed, and no name is against it: that not writing to a woman of no office and not going up that coast road to a doorstep is not a thing that was done and is not entered in the first column, and that the reason against the line is that a good reason is the reason a man gives when he has not thought about the fourth year, and that nobody in that shed has said what the form does and that this yard is not going to be the first to find out.*

*And that the said party has become an address in the ordinary post at a penny, and that an address is a thing that can be given to a man who is going somewhere, and that this is entered here and is not a matter of law and is not a matter of the form and is only a matter of what a shed did on a Saturday.*

*And that the said man's own opinion of the column, being that the second column is more use than the first one, is not entered at all, because a man in a shed door on the strength of a bad hand is not a figure.*

---

**One thousand seven hundred and seventy-six pence came into this yard on the said Tuesday and nothing went out of it, and seventy-six marks and threepence is in the bag, which is twelve thousand one hundred and sixty-three pence. The whole of what came into that yard in the twenty-two days ended on the said Tuesday is eleven marks, and the whole of the money that has come into that yard since the sixth of December past is one thousand seven hundred and seventy-six pence, and there is nothing else. The whole of what went out of that yard since the sixth of December past is eight marks, six shillings and twopence in eleven transactions at five counters, and it was spent before that Tuesday. Nothing in a tin in that shed since the twenty-eighth of April past. Three marks owing to a man of Ashtide, unmoved, and nobody was sent. Seven pence to that yard's own shipwright, unpaid. Eight marks and a day of a boat to that harbor's own bellwright since the twenty-third of May past, unargued. Eight shillings and tenpence to a woman of that harbor with her name on the figure. Forty marks first after food to a person of no office, unpaid, and a letter came up that hill on the second of April and nothing was asked of her and nothing was written back. Forty marks stand whole to a boy of twenty-two, the three nots stand, the third column is not filled, nobody is appointed to it, and the boy's hand is at the head of it. The wage column stands at twenty-six lines and one hundred and four marks and twenty-four weeks have fallen due with no envelope standing against them, and the said Tuesday was a wage Tuesday and the column did not move.**
