# Chapter 256

*A Line and a Shackle of a Make Nobody Here Makes, and a Leaf With Four Columns*

Friday the twenty-sixth and Saturday the twenty-seventh of March, in a boat of about forty tons at the face of the second seam, and in the shed on the mole.

**A boat of about forty tons went out on the said Friday at eleven with the man of the second lay and the ship's hand in her, hired at one mark and eightpence for the day, and one mark and eightpence went out of that yard's own book at about half past four in the afternoon, and no day of work was booked in that yard on either of the said two days, and the reason against the line of the said Friday is that a party of two spent the whole of that day in a hired boat at the face of that seam and there is no paper in that drawer for a day to be booked against, and the reason against the line of the said Saturday is the same reason and not a second one.**

**And the far bench was occupied on the said Friday from about ten until about four and was not occupied on the said Saturday at any hour, and the holder of that writ was in a boat off the face of the second seam on the said Friday and did not go over to him on either of the two days, and a man of sixty-one was not asked for anything on either of them and asked nothing on either of them.**

**And the said Saturday was not a wage Tuesday and the number of weeks fallen due with no envelope standing against them is TWENTY-TWO and did not move on either of them, and the reason is the reason of the twenty-ninth of December past, and the last wage Tuesday was the twenty-third of that month.**

---

There was about a foot of green on the weather side, where on the twenty-third there had been about four inches, and a green that comes up eight inches in three days is a green that a man has been putting there.

"Say that again," said the man of the second lay.

"I did not say it. You said it."

"I said what I would have said. There is no green in this water that comes up eight inches in three days, and the only way a mark gets fouled faster than the water does it is a man with a bag of something from another harbour and a bad conscience, and I have never known a bad conscience to travel without a bucket."

**And there was a line on the mark that was not theirs.**

Their own line was a hand's width of three-strand laid stuff, tarred in the year before last, and this was four-strand and it was not tarred and it was new and it was bent in a figure of eight and tied with a sheet bend, and a sheet bend is what a man ties when he is in a hurry and cannot find a fisher's bend and does not know one.

"And that's not ours," Nessa said.

"That's not ours."

"That's nobody's."

"That," said the man of the second lay, "is somebody who was in a hurry on a dark night with a bucket and a sheet bend and a shackle, and I have met him. Not this week. Ever."

And the shackle was not their shackle either. Their shackle was oval, forge-finished, the shank of it drawn down in a fire and closed on a mandril, and it was a Kestrel-side make and every chandler on this coast had them in a crate under the counter. This one was oval too, and it had a **flat on the crown of the bow**, a quarter of an inch across, where somebody had hit it with something to break a paint scale, and the metal was bright under the paint and had not had time to go dull in salt water, and the sea does not leave a quarter-inch flat on a shackle on its own.

"Holbech's," Nessa said. "Or Holbech's father's before him. You swage them down to nothing and finish the bow on the anvil to true it, and it comes out with a flat, and the flat is the whole of the reason a man who has fitted a hundred of them knows it in the dark."

"Ternwatch," said the man of the second lay. "There is one smith on this coast who finishes them like that and he is sixty-eight and he has a shop off the yard at Ternwatch and he does it for the yards there and for nobody on this side, and I have had a shackle of his in my hand four times in nineteen years and three of them were off the Sill." He took the slate. "Write the flat. Write the date on the flat. That is a thing a man can go and look at in a shop in Ternwatch tomorrow and a thing he cannot go and look at anywhere else, and I am telling you that because it is the only useful thing I have got and I have not got a name and I am not going to one."

They came in at half past two with the slate and the flat and the figure of eight and the foot of somebody else's green, and Calder Marr was on the mole with his arms folded and had clearly been like that for some time.

---

He wanted the complaint. That was the first thing and he did not hide it, which is the one thing about him that has never needed improving.

"A name in a book in a town four days down this coast," he said. "Four people. If somebody has been on our mark, the only people on this coast who could have been on our mark are those four. Nobody is obliged to answer anything. Ask them, and if they say no, we are wrong and we write down that we were wrong. That is all it is."

"No," said Nessa.

"They wrote a limit about the bells and not about—"

"**It is a complaint with the words taken off it,**" said Nessa, "and you said the sentence in a market on this mole in March and I wrote it in this yard's second column and you did not improve on it then and I am not letting you improve on it now. We cannot make any one of those four communities trust us. You said that at the head of that dock in about forty people's hearing and you said we had not tried and were not going to. If we go up that coast with a question about a mark, then the thing that is remembered about us on that coast is a yard that came and asked, and the mark does not matter."

**He did not answer her for a long time. He is a man who has spent five volumes being the person in the room who knows when to stop talking, and on that Saturday on a mole he stopped talking, and then he said:**

"Read it back."

"Which of it."

"The part about the page. Read it back the way you read a position back."

She read it back, and he made her read it again, and then he said, "That is the same thing I said in a market and it is better in your mouth than it was in mine, and I would like it entered that I lost that argument to my own sentence, because in about a month I am going to want to have won it."

**"And there are two other things and I am going to say the price of both of them out loud before either of them is agreed, because that is the practice and because a price named after the yes is not a price.**" He was counting on his fingers, which he does. "One: a cut. A wire through it, in the dark, by a man who is not me, on a night with no moon, and then the thing is on nobody's bottom and both of us have nothing. That wants a boat and a man and an instrument, and we have none of the three, and the last figure anybody in this harbor put on a piece of work like that is in a book in a room in the west of this city that we are not allowed to ask about and are not going to ask about. And it wants somebody who will go out at night for a boat and a man and a piece of paper, and there is nobody on this coast like that, and if there were he would want paying and we have no money.

"Two: a mark that can be seen from further off. Bigger, higher, a frame on top of it, so that a man on the wheel at a quarter of a mile does not have to come round the point to see it. That is four marks of iron at a chandler's and an hour of somebody's time and about half a day of a boat, and it is the only one of the three we could pay for, and we cannot pay for it, and it is also the one of the three I do not want, because a bigger mark says louder, and there are two parties on that ground now, and the louder one is the one that gets cut in the dark by a man with no lights."

"So," said Nessa. "That is one we cannot do, one we cannot pay for, and one we cannot do."

"Yes."

"Write it down and stop there, then, and go in."

---

She ruled the leaf on the Saturday afternoon, and she did it on the back of a sheet of chandler's paper because the good paper costs eightpence and the good paper was not in that drawer either.

Four columns, in her own left hand, with a rule between each of them and a head over each of them.

The first head was **what was there**. The second was **who saw it**. The third was **what was done**. And the fourth was four words long and she had to get a new nib for it, and the nib was blunt, and the fourth head is a little ragged and it is the only ragged thing in that book.

**What it is not.**

"There," she said. "That is the whole of it and you can see why it is the one I could not do with the other three. Three columns tell a man what happened. Three columns are a story and a story can be argued with. That one cannot be argued with because it is not a claim, it is a refusal, and the only person who can fill it in is whoever comes next, and he will fill it in with what he did not do and he will not have to agree with me about it and I will not have to agree with him."

**"Where does it go?"** said Calder Marr.

"That is the fourth question of this week and it has got a worse answer than the other three." She put the nib down. "Not on the wall. The wall has our fourth return under a sheet and that return is the only paper on that wall that any office in any city is obliged to look at, and if I nail a leaf with four columns on that wall then I have made the wall a place where a yard puts things, and a place where a yard puts things is a place a yard can be asked about, and I have spent five volumes refusing to be an address."

"So it is in a book in a shed."

"It is on a leaf in a book in a shed, and there is no second copy, and nobody on this coast can check it, and in about four years there will be a page in a drawer in a town that I cannot look in and I will not know whether it is in that drawer or on a fire, and I have entered that, and I am not going to get out of it with a better sentence." She put her hand flat on the leaf. "This is the second one. The five drawers are the first one and they were made on the sixth of March and they cost us a post and a page. This one is cheaper and it is worse, and the reason it is worse is that anybody can come in here with a reason and read it and nobody can ask anybody to produce it."

She wrote the first entry in the first column, in her own hand, and it read: *the mark set by this yard on the face of the second seam on the twelfth of March; fouled on the weather side; shackle off and on again; wire cut and mended on the water side; a line that is not ours; a flat on the crown of the bow.*

She left the fourth column empty under its ragged head.

---

*First column, entered on Saturday the twenty-seventh day of March next at about half past eight in the evening in this shed: that on the said Friday there was about a foot of green on the weather side of that mark, where on the twenty-third of March there had been about four inches, and that a green that comes up eight inches in three days is a green that a man has been putting there, and that the said man of the second lay said he had never known a bad conscience to travel without a bucket.*

*And that on the said Friday there was a line on that mark that is not the line of this yard, being four-strand and untarred and new and bent in a figure of eight and tied with a sheet bend, and that a sheet bend is what a man ties in a hurry when he cannot find a fisher's bend and does not know one, and that the three of those are entered as three findings and not as one.*

*And that on the said Friday the shackle on that mark was not the shackle this yard fits, being oval but with a flat of about a quarter of an inch across the crown of the bow, the metal bright under the paint and not yet dulled, and that a shackle finished that way is swaged down in a fire and trued on an anvil and comes out with a flat on the crown, and that the said ship's hand named the make and the said man of the second lay said that there is one smith on this coast who finishes them that way and that he is sixty-eight and has a shop off the yard at Ternwatch and does it for the yards there and for nobody on this side of the water, and that the said man has had a shackle of that make in his hand four times in nineteen years and three of them were off the Sill, and that no name is entered in that book and none is going to be and the said man said he was not going to one.*

*And that three things were said out loud on the mole on the said Friday with their prices before any of them was agreed, being a cut, which wants a boat and a man and an instrument and this yard has none of the three and the last figure anybody in this harbor put on such a piece of work is in a book in a room in the west of this city that this yard is not allowed to ask about and is not going to ask about, and somebody who would go out at night for a boat and a man and a piece of paper, of whom there is nobody on this coast; a bigger mark, at four marks of iron and an hour and about half a day of a boat, which is the only one of the three this yard could pay for and cannot pay for and does not want, because a mark with two parties on it that says louder is the mark that gets a wire through in the dark; and a complaint, which is a name in a book in a town four days down this coast and is the four people of that coast, and which was refused by the ship's hand and not put on the table and is not going to be put on the table.*

*And that on the said Friday the holder of that writ lost the argument on the complaint to his own sentence of the eleventh of March, and asked for that sentence to be read back twice, and asked that it be entered that he lost it to his own sentence and not to a better one, and that it was entered.*

*And that on the said Saturday a leaf was ruled in that yard's own book on the back of a sheet of chandler's paper, being four columns, headed what was there, who saw it, what was done, and what it is not; that the fourth head was written with a blunt nib and is the only ragged thing in that book; and that the said leaf is not dated before the said Saturday and no entry in it is of any date before the said Saturday.*

*Second column, not checkable from a mole, entered on the said Saturday at about half past eight in the evening in this shed, and no name is against it: that a page no man can check is worth less than a page a man can look at, and that this yard has said that in five volumes and in a market in about forty people's hearing, and that this leaf is a page no man can check, and that it is the second such thing this yard has built, and that the first cost a post and a page on the sixth of March, and that the said woman said that this one is cheaper and is worse, and that the reason it is worse is that anybody can come in that shed with a reason and read it and nobody can ask anybody to produce it.*

*And that the fourth column is empty under its head and is going to be filled in by whoever comes next and not by the woman who ruled it, and that a column of refusals is the only method this yard has that is not a confession and not a return and not a claim; and that the said leaf does not go on any wall in that harbor and never will, because a wall where a yard puts things is a wall a yard can be asked about.*

**One mark and eightpence went out of this yard on the said Friday and nothing came into it on either of the two days and nothing went out of it on the said Saturday, and sixty-seven marks and one shilling is in the bag, which is ten thousand seven hundred and thirty-two pence, and the figure the bag is short of the wage of four marks is ten thousand and ninety-two pence. The whole of what came into that yard in the twelve days ended on the said Saturday is nothing, and the reason is the form of the fourth of March and not a mood. The whole of what went out of that yard in the five days ended on the said Saturday is one mark and eightpence, and the whole of what has gone out of that yard since the sixth of December past is six marks, five shillings and five pence in nine transactions at five counters, of which three are the boat of the man of the second lay at one mark and eightpence a day. Nothing in a tin since the twenty-eighth of April. Three marks owing to a man of Ashtide, unmoved, and he is not in that book and nobody was sent for him and no name of the four people of that coast is in that book. Seven pence to that yard's own shipwright, unpaid. Eight shillings and tenpence to a woman of that harbor with her name on the figure. Eight marks and a day of a boat owing to that harbor's own bellwright since the twenty-third of May past, unargued. Forty marks first after food to a person of no office, unpaid since the fifteenth of October past, and that yard has not asked her for anything since the twenty-sixth of December past. Forty marks stand whole to a boy of twenty-two, the three nots stand, the third column is not filled, nobody is appointed to it, and the boy's hand is at the head of it. The wage column stands at twenty-six lines and one hundred and four marks and twenty-two weeks have fallen due with no envelope standing against them, and neither of these two days was a wage Tuesday. Thirty-one on the chandler's note, not re-written, its three counts of weeks unmoved at twenty-two and thirteen and eleven, and it will not be said a fifth time. The notice of the thirty-first of October has its four clauses and has not been lifted, narrowed, re-served or altered, and no person, no boat and no lead of that yard has been inside its first clause on either of these two days, and no return of any kind was made out of either of them. The plate at the fifth seam is in eleven feet in that mole's own foundation stone and is not reached, not measured, not touched and not asked about, and the note against it stands open. The case of five bells is at about five and a half fathoms inside the four hundred yards off the outer marks, on nobody's schedule, was not raised and was not asked about in these two days, and the limit four people of that coast wrote and signed stands. The delayed note stands at eleven logged occasions and two of them with no call in them, and nothing was entered and nothing was measured. The two objects of nobody's are about two hundred and eighty-nine days up the crown of that mole, about nine feet apart, under two tarpaulins, and neither has moved. The bench under that pump is bare for a fifty-sixth night, counting from the Sunday of the thirty-first of January instant, and it is the only bare thing in that shed.**
