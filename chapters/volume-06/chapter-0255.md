# Chapter 255

*What Fouling Says, and the Four People Who Are Not Going to Be Asked*

Wednesday the twenty-fourth and Thursday the twenty-fifth of March, on the mole's steps, in the shed, and at the head of that dock.

**The cistern behind the engine house was charged at no figure at about ten on the said Thursday morning by four men this yard does not know and this party walked none of it, and that is the thirty-second of the charges this party has entered walked on none of since the third day of August past, and the walk is nil and it stays nil, and no day of work was booked in this yard on either of the said two days, and the reason against the line of the said Thursday is that four men this yard does not know have been at that cistern since ten.**

**And the far bench was not occupied on the said Wednesday at any hour and was not occupied on the said Thursday at any hour, and the holder of that writ did not go over to him on either of the two days, and a man of sixty-one was not asked for anything on either of them and asked nothing on either of them.**

**And nothing came into this yard on either of the two days and nothing went out of it on either of them, and the said Tuesday was the last wage Tuesday and the number of weeks fallen due with no envelope standing against them is TWENTY-TWO and did not move on either of them, and the reason is the reason of the twenty-ninth of December past.**

---

The man of the second lay had been nineteen years in the water and had fouled, or watched other men foul, more marks than he could be asked to count, and when Nessa put the slate on the bench on the Wednesday morning he looked at it for a long time without touching it.

"That is a good slate," he said at last. "That is a proper slate. Whoever keeps that slate is a fool and whoever wrote on it is a person who can be trusted with a lead line."

"That is not what I want from it."

"No," he said. "No, it is not, and I know what you want, and I am going to tell you the four things I know and then you are going to let me go to the north end."

"Go."

"Growth takes a season. On this coast, in water at this temperature, in a fouled iron thing on the bottom, green comes up in about nine weeks in a hard year and eleven in a soft one, and it comes up on the weather side first and last, because that is the side the water works on. That is one.

"A shackle that is bright has been handled. Bright is not fouling, bright is *absence* of fouling, and a thing only gets absent where a skin has been in the last fortnight. That is two.

"A mended cut is a cut somebody made on purpose and mended on purpose, and a spanner on the underside of a shackle is somebody who took the shackle off the frame, and the pin is somebody who put it back on. And the third bright place is the whole of it, because nobody lifts a shackle and puts it down without turning it in their hand. That is three.

"Four," he said, "is that none of those three things says a word about who did it, and that anybody who tells you which of them did it is selling you something, and I have been on the water nineteen years and I have never once known a fouled mark tell me a name."

**"Say the other half of that out loud at the head of the dock," Nessa said, "and I will write it down and you will not be selling anything."**

He looked at her.

"What other half."

"That all four of those are true, and that the fouling says a hand was there and the fouling says nothing about the hand, and that those two are both true and that neither of them is a reason to go up the coast and knock on a door." She squared the slate on the bench. "You have been saying that for nineteen years to men who pay you to say it. You said it to me in a boat on Wednesday and you did not get paid for it and you did not know you were saying it. I want it said where nine people can hear it, because in about a month somebody on that dock is going to be told which of four people did it, and I want the sentence to be in the air before that."

He thought about it for a while.

"Nine people," he said. "And about a quarter past ten, because that is when the market is thickest and the fish are in and nobody is on the north end yet."

---

There were eleven of them and not nine, and about half of them were not listening, and Elias Voke of the *Anna Ray* was one of the two who were, and he said so afterward, in the shed, to nobody.

So the man of the second lay said it at the head of that dock on the Thursday morning at about a quarter past ten, with a slate in his own hand, and he said it the way he said everything, which was as though he were reading out the price of rope, and about half of them did not hear him at all.

"Fouling takes a season," he said. "That is nine weeks and eleven weeks and I have been wrong about it twice in nineteen years and I was wrong in the year of the big blow. Bright on a shackle is a hand inside a fortnight. A mended cut is somebody with a spanner and a morning. And none of those three knows a name, and I have never known a fouled mark tell me a name, and if a man on this dock tells you which of four people set that shackle, he is selling."

Somebody at the back said something about the four of them at the water thereabouts, and the man of the second lay did not turn round.

**"I have not said a word about the four of them and I am not going to, and I want that in whatever book you keep, because there is a line in a book in a town four days down this coast that four people wrote themselves and signed, and it stands, and nobody in this yard is going to ask them to take it off, and if I have said one word to-day that touches it then I will write it out of your slate myself with my own thumb.**

"**And I will tell you the rest of it, because you are going to be asked. There is a mark on the face of the second seam with two men in it now. You set it. Somebody else has been on it. Both of those are true and neither of them cancels the other. A mark with two men in it is not a thing anybody can raise. It is a thing that gets a wire through in the dark by a man with no lights who does not want it found, and then both of them have nothing, and that is what happened to a mark off the Sill in my first year and I do not know the names of the two and I am not going to be asked.**

"**And I am not going to be the one to go and look. I have looked at two of them this week and I have said what I know and what I do not know, and that is the end of what nineteen years is worth, and anybody who tells you a man of fifty can do more than that is telling you about himself.**"

Nessa wrote it down, all of it, including the part about the first year and the mark off the Sill, and she wrote that down because a man who has said a thing like that in front of eleven people should not have to say it twice, and then she read it back to him in the shed at about noon, and he said it was right, and then he went to the north end.

In the shed at about half past one Calder Marr read what she had written and put it down on the bench with his thumb on the fourth line.

"**He is wrong about one of them and he is right about three,**" he said.

"Which one."

"He is wrong that nobody is going to be asked. Somebody is going to be asked. Not those four, not this month. Somebody with a boat and a reason and no scruples is going to be asked in about six weeks and the four of them are going to hear about it from a market, the same way we heard about the fouling on Saturday." He took his thumb off the page. "So we get six weeks and a page of what a man of fifty knows, and I would take six weeks over a name, and I would not tell him that in a shed with a wall this thin."

---

*First column, entered on Thursday the twenty-fifth day of March next at about half past nine in the evening in this shed: that a man of the second lay with nineteen years in the water read the slate of the twenty-third and said four things out loud, being that growth takes a season, being nine weeks in a hard year and eleven in a soft one, on the weather side first and last, and that he has been wrong about it twice in nineteen years and was wrong in the year of the big blow; that bright on a shackle is the absence of fouling and a skin has been in it inside a fortnight; that a mended cut is a cut made on purpose and mended on purpose and a spanner under a shackle is somebody who took it off the frame; and that none of those three things says a word about who did it, and that anybody who says which of four people did it is selling.*

*And that the said four things were said out loud at the head of that dock on the said Thursday at about a quarter past ten, with the slate in the said man's own hand, in eleven people's hearing of whom about half were not listening, and that the said man said at the time that he had not said a word about the four people four days down that coast and was not going to, and asked that that be written down, and that it was written down.*

*And that the said man said that a mark with two parties in it is a thing that gets a wire through in the dark by a man with no lights who does not want it found, and that he has seen two of them, and that he does not know the names and is not going to be asked, and that the two of them were off the Sill and were in his first year and he does not know the names of the two.*

*And that both of those are true and neither of them cancels the other, and that the said sentence was said on a mole's steps by a fisher and not improved on by anybody and is not going to be said again this month.*

*And that on the said Thursday the cistern was charged at no figure by four men this yard does not know and that party walked none of it, and that the walk is nil and stays nil, and that that is the thirty-second of the charges that party has entered walked on none of since the third of August past, and that no day of work was booked in that yard on either of the said two days.*

*Second column, not checkable from a mole, entered on the said Thursday at about half past nine in the evening in this shed, and no name is against it: that a mark with two parties in it cannot be raised and cannot be cut and cannot be complained about, and that the complaint is the only instrument anybody has got and that the complaint requires a name and a name is not in that book and is not going to be put in it, and that the four lines four people of that coast wrote themselves and signed stand, and that the holder of that writ did not ask them to take it off and was not asked to and is not going to ask them this season or the next.*

*And that the holder of that writ said out loud in that shed that the man of the second lay is wrong about one of his four things, being that nobody is going to be asked, and that somebody with a boat and a reason and no scruples is going to be asked in about six weeks and the four of them are going to hear about it from a market, and that the said holder said that he would take six weeks and a page of what a man of fifty knows over a name, and that he would not say so in a shed with a wall that thin.*

*And that the six weeks is not a plan and is not in the first column and is not going to be improved on, and that it is in this column because a man said it to one person in a shed and it is not checkable from a mole.*

**Nothing came into this yard on either of the said two days and nothing went out of it on either of them, and sixty-eight marks, one shilling and eight pence is in the bag, which is ten thousand nine hundred pence, and the figure the bag is short of the wage of four marks is ten thousand two hundred and sixty pence. The whole of what has gone out of that yard since the sixth of December past is five marks, four shillings and nine pence in eight transactions at five counters, and nothing has come into that bag since the fourteenth of March past. Nothing in a tin in that shed since the twenty-eighth of April. Three marks owing to a man of Ashtide, unmoved, and he is not in that book and nobody was sent for him. Seven pence to that yard's own shipwright, unpaid. Eight shillings and tenpence to a woman of that harbor with her name on the figure. Eight marks and a day of a boat owing to that harbor's own bellwright since the twenty-third of May past, unargued, and he was not asked anything in these two days and no digit of anything he said on the second of July past is on a page of that book. Forty marks first after food to a person of no office, unpaid since the fifteenth of October past, and that yard has not asked her for anything since the twenty-sixth of December past. Forty marks stand whole to a boy of twenty-two, the three nots of the eighth of June past stand, the third column is not filled, nobody is appointed to it, and the boy's hand is at the head of it. The wage column stands at twenty-six lines and one hundred and four marks and twenty-two weeks have fallen due with no envelope standing against them, and neither of these two days was a wage Tuesday and the column did not move. The chandler's note stands at thirty-one, not re-written, its three counts of weeks unmoved at twenty-two and thirteen and eleven, and it was said out loud for the fourth time in seven months on the fourth of that month and will not be said a fifth time. The notice of the thirty-first of October has its four clauses and has not been lifted, narrowed, re-served or altered, and no person, no boat and no lead of that yard has been inside its first clause on either of these two days, and no return of any kind was made out of either of them. The plate at the fifth seam is in eleven feet in that mole's own foundation stone and is not reached, not measured, not touched and not asked about, and the note against it stands open, and the only mechanism anybody has named for reaching it is a change of politics in an office and not a better letter out of a shed. The case of five bells is at about five and a half fathoms inside the four hundred yards off the outer marks, on nobody's schedule, was not raised and was not asked about in these two days, and the limit four people of that coast wrote and signed stands and the holder did not ask to have it lifted. The delayed note stands at eleven logged occasions and two of them with no call in them, and nothing was entered on either of these two days and nothing was measured and the delay has not moved. The two objects of nobody's are about two hundred and eighty-seven days up the crown of that mole, about nine feet apart, under two tarpaulins, one with a corner off it since the seventeenth of June past and not lashed back, and neither has moved, and that party did not go near them on either of these two days. The bench under that pump is bare for a fifty-fourth night, counting from the Sunday of the thirty-first of January instant, and it is the only bare thing in that shed. Nothing went up on any wall in that harbor in these two days and nothing came off, and the market wall of that harbor carries that yard's own fourth return under one sheet and is still the only paper on it that any office in any city is obliged to look at, and the two naval notices are not on that wall and were not taken down by that party.**
