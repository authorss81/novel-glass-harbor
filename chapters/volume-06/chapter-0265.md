# Chapter 265

*A Net Does Not Feel Like a Net at Forty Fathoms in the Dark*

Friday the ninth of April, off the south end of that mole and a mile and a half out, and in the shed on the mole.

**The said Friday was not a wage Tuesday and the column did not move, and the wall did not come down and the cistern was not charged, and no figure is entered against a day this party did not see.**

**And a man of twenty-eight of a pressure house at Ternwatch went off that harbor in a boat on the said Friday with a shoulder that will not go above his head and a rib that is not his own business, and he is on that shore a hand's width from a hard that has been unanswered since the ninth of November past, and the whole of what that costs is entered in the first column of this book with the date on it, and one mark and eightpence and fourpence of it went out of this yard on the said Friday at about six in the evening.**

---

They went out on the top of the flood again, and they had the day in front of them, and Calder Marr went down with the two men off the Crown house book's boat and a spanner and a saw and a short bar of iron, and the saw was a hack saw with a broken handle and it was the ship's and she had said so on the Tuesday and it was not a gift.

The first hour was the hardest and it was the hardest in the way that everything on that bottom was hard, which is that the ground was the right ground and the water was the right water and the only difficulty was that there were forty fathoms of it on top of the work.

They got the turns off the bar in about fifty minutes. It is one thing to say that a chain is made to a bar. It is another thing to say that a chain made to a bar by a smith a hundred and forty years ago or more, and rusted into a socket in a stone, with eleven days of a herring net bent round it in two turns and a half, will come off in the dark with a hack saw and a spanner and two men's hands. It came off. Calder Marr took the bar out of the socket with the spanner and put it in his own belt, because a bar of iron two feet long is not a thing to be left on a floor at forty fathoms, and nobody has ever once regretted a man taking a piece of iron up with him.

The chain came free in one piece, about nine feet of it, and it went up the line.

And then the net came up.

---

**Nobody saw it. That is the honest shape of it and it is in the second column and not the first.** A herring net that has been lying on a made floor at forty fathoms for eleven days has not been lying still. It has been moving an inch and then an inch and then a foot in a tide, all of it, all at once, and a net that is holding on by the middle of its belly has been swinging its ends about like a slow arm for eleven days, and when the belly came free the two ends had somewhere to be.

It came up on Amos Searle from below and behind and it took him up about eleven feet and put him into the edge of a course of squared stone, and the course had been laid by somebody who had squared it.

He was in the water for a further two minutes and came up himself. He said one thing, and the thing he said was not about his shoulder.

"**It came up slow,**" he said. "That is the thing. It came up slow, and it was wide, and it came up all at once when the middle let go, and I have pulled nets for four years and a net that has been hung eleven days comes up slow. **If I had known that this morning I would have gone under it and not beside it.** And I knew it this morning and I did not think about it, and that is not anybody's fault, and I am not going to stand in a boat and say that it is."

"Say it anyway," said Fisk, from the crown, over the pipe, "because I am going to write it in my own book tonight and I want it to be your words and not mine."

---

They got him up. It took a quarter of an hour and the pot came up with him because nobody was going down to fetch anything, and the gauge read **eighty-four** on the coming up and the needle was not steady, and Fisk read it as **eighty-two** and the ship's hand wrote eighty-four because the needle came back, and both of them were in the book with the difference against them.

He would not go up the ladder. He sat on the boat's thwart with his good arm over his knee and his head down, and he was sick once over the side and was not ashamed of it and did not apologise for it, and the man of the second lay took his coat off without being asked and put it across the other's knees.

"He cannot lift that arm above his head," said Fisk, and put his own hand on Searle's shoulder and got the whole of it in about a second and a half, in the way of a man who has handled a great many shoulders. "**The shoulder is gone. It is not out. It is gone.** And I have been a holder eleven years and I can tell you what a shoulder is worth, and this arm is worth about four weeks of nothing and after that about half of what it was, and I am telling you that on the crown of a mole in about a hundred people's hearing, and if that is not my business then there is no such thing as a man's business.**"

"Is it a week," said Elias Voke, who had come out in his own boat and had not been asked to and had not brought anybody.

"Five weeks off that book," said Fisk, "and then light work, and the word light is the Crown's word and not mine and the light work is somebody else carrying things he cannot carry, and it goes in the book under a date, and I will read it out when the book comes up the road and not one minute before. **The book went down that road on the Thursday night with the news of the shoulder in it, and that road is a night's road each way, and it comes up again on the Friday night behind a man I did not ask for, and I am not going to say a figure out of a book I have not got in my hand.**"

---

The rest of the day was waste and everybody said so.

Calder Marr went down at about two with a man of twenty-six on the hose and came up at about half past three with nothing, because there was no reason to go down; the warp was off the bar and the net was lying on a floor at forty fathoms and it was not going anywhere before the tide turned, and a thing that is not going anywhere is a thing you can come back to on the morning, and that is a sentence this yard has learned at some cost.

They came in on the last of the flood with about an hour of light, and the mole-head light was out, and the compressor was quiet by six, and Fen Barrow carried the pipe up the steps in eleven lengths and counted them, and the ninth length was short.

"Nine," he said. "There are nine and there should be eleven."

"There are nine because two of them are joined with a union and are one length with a name on it," said Fisk, "and I have been in this trade nineteen years and a man of twenty-six has just told me what is wrong with my hose, and I am going to be glad about it for the rest of the week."

---

Nessa Pike had the ship's ledger out on the bench when the boat came alongside, because she had worked out at about two o'clock, alone, in a shed, with the compressor off and the tide making, what the day was going to cost, and she had done it twice and got the same figure both times, which is the only kind of arithmetic in this book worth anything.

"Twelve marks, two shillings and eightpence again," she said. "Nine for the set. Two and eight for him. A shilling and four for the hand. And it is a day that bought the turns off the bar and a bar of iron and nine feet of chain and nothing else, and the chain is in that shed now and the rest of that day is on the other side of nothing."

"And the surgeon," said Calder Marr.

"**The surgeon is not mine to put in that column and it is not a week's work either.**" She had her hand flat on the page. "A mile and a half by water to a place with a hospital in it and a bed in the hospital, and a man to go with him, because he is not to be left on a boat. And a man at Ashtide who sets a shoulder and a rib, and a week's keep of the bed, and a bed is a thing you pay by the day and I do not know for how many days, and I am not going to write down a week because a week is the week I hope for and this column is for figures and not for hopes."

"Then write a week," said Fisk. "**A shilling a day is the bed at Ashtide and I know it because my sister was in it for five weeks eleven years ago**, and you write a week and you have paid a week and if he is in there on Tuesday you have paid a week, and the second week is not this yard's and it is not this yard's because this yard has not got a second week."

"That," said Nessa Pike, "is the first sentence anybody has said to me this week that I did not have to argue with."

---

*First column, entered on Friday the ninth day of April next at about half past nine in the evening in this shed: that the pot was charged on the said Friday to eighty-four pounds and came up off the floor of the said cut with a man of twenty-eight named Amos Searle in it, and that the gauge was unsteady on the coming up and the man of about thirty-four of a pressure house at Ternwatch read it as eighty-two and the ship's hand wrote eighty-four, and that both figures are entered with the difference against them.*

*And that a net of the herring boat of the second lay came up on the said man from below and behind at about eleven o'clock in the morning and took him up about eleven feet and put him into the edge of a course of squared stone, and that he was in the water about two minutes afterwards and came up himself, and that he would not go up a ladder and was taken up the side of a boat on a warp, and that his left shoulder will not go above his head and his left ribs are broken in two places.*

*And that the said man said out loud, in a boat, off that mole, and asked for it to be written down in his own words, that a net that has been hung eleven days comes up slow, and that it came up slow, and that if he had known that in the morning he would have gone under it and not beside it, and that he knew it in the morning and did not think about it, and that it is not anybody's fault and he is not going to say otherwise in a boat.*

*And that the man of about thirty-four of that pressure house said on the crown of that mole in about a hundred people's hearing that the said shoulder is not out and is gone, and that the arm is worth about four weeks of nothing and after that about half of what it was, and that the said man said the book at Ternwatch carries the said man as unfit for five weeks from the ninth day of April and as fit for light work after, and that he offered to read the entry out of the book on the Saturday morning and that it was read out on the Saturday morning, the said book having gone down that coast road on the Thursday night and come up again on the Friday night behind the coach that brought the second man, and that the said road is a night's road each way and a man was in a coach on it for fourteen hours.*

*And that the warp was taken off the bar in about fifty minutes and the bar of iron was taken out of its socket with a spanner and put into a belt and brought up, being two feet long and square, and that nine feet of chain came up on the said Friday and is in that shed, and that the net was left lying on that floor at forty fathoms and was not cut free on the said day, and that a man of twenty-six and the holder of that writ went down at about two in the afternoon and came up at about half past three with nothing, and the reason against the line of that descent is that a thing which is not going anywhere is a thing that can be come back to on the morning.*

*And that twelve marks, two shillings and eightpence went out of that yard's own book on the said evening on the same three figures as the Thursday, being nine marks for the set, two marks and eightpence for the said man, and a mark and two shillings for the said man of twenty-six.*

*Second column, not checkable from a mole, entered on the said Friday at about half past nine in the evening in this shed, and no name is against it: that nobody saw the net come up, and that the reason against the line of that is that there were five people on the surface of that water and none of them was in it, and that the man it came up on is the only one who can say anything about it and he has said it and it is in the first column in his words, and that what cannot be checked from a mole is what a man of twenty-eight knew at eight o'clock in the morning.*

*And that the last day a working day could be booked in that yard was the fourteenth day of March past, and that this party has done two days of work since and has entered both of them, and that neither of the two was booked against anything, and that the two days cost twenty-four marks, five shillings and fourpence and that the whole of what came into that yard for them was eleven marks from one man, and that the difference is this yard's and is in the first column and is not in the second.*

*And that the said man of about thirty-four said that the bed at Ashtide is a shilling a day and that a week of it was written down because a week is the week that party hopes for, and that the ship's hand entered it, and that the second week is not that party's and not that party's because that party has not got a second week, and that the said man did not say the second week out loud in a book and this column is where it goes.*

---

**Twelve marks, two shillings and eightpence went out of this yard on the said Friday and nothing came into it, and fifty marks, seven shillings and threepence is in the bag, which is eight thousand and eighty-seven pence. The whole of what went out of that yard since the sixth of December past is thirty-three marks, twelve shillings and sixpence in fourteen transactions at six counters. And a man of twenty-eight of a pressure house at Ternwatch is on his way to a hospital at Ashtide with a shoulder that will not go above his head, and a mile and a half of water is between him and it, and the fare and the surgeon's fee and a week of the bed have not gone out of that yard's book yet and will go out of it on the morning.**
