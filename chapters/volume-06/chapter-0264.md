# Chapter 264

*The Forty Fathoms, and the Floor That Is Not the Floor of a Quarry*

Thursday the eighth of April, off the south end of that mole and a mile and a half out, and in the shed on the mole.

**The cistern behind the engine house was charged at no figure at about ten on the said Thursday morning by four men this yard does not know and this party walked none of it, and that is the thirty-sixth of the charges that party has entered walked on none of since the third day of August past, and the walk is nil and it stays nil. And the wall behind the reeve's bench did not come down on the said Thursday, and the said Thursday was not a wage Tuesday.**

**And a day of work was done in this yard on the said Thursday and was not booked, and the reason against the line of that day is that there is no paper in the drawer to book it against, and the day is in the first column with the price named before it was agreed, and there was no first entry and nothing to enter and the two ends of that shot line were cut on the surface at about eleven o'clock in the morning while a man was forty fathoms down and nothing on the surface of this earth was holding anything at all.**

---

They went out on the top of the flood, which was a little after half past eight and would give them three and a half hours on the ground.

The set went out first, on the boat, in the housing and the compressor and the pipe coiled, and the compressor was bolted to the ring of the deck with the crown of the mole behind them and the mole-head light out above it because a light costs oil. Fisk had it up and charged in about twenty minutes and did not hurry and did not talk.

Amos Searle came down the ring in his own gear from the boat's own boat, which was a Crown house boat with a Crown house name painted on the bow, and he was twenty-eight and he was not frightened and he was not pleased and he was exactly what a man is on a working day.

"**Call the gas when you are ready and call it again before you go under**," he said, to Calder Marr, in the water, at the pot. "Twice. Every time. I have been on a job where a man called it once and I have thought about it for eleven years."

"You will hear it twice," said Calder Marr, "and if you hear it once you come and get me, and if that is inconvenient then you do it anyway."

Fen Barrow had the hose and the pot line and stood on the boat's rail with his boots off, because a man who has to get off a rail in a hurry should not be in boots. The man of the second lay had the wheel and did not offer to help and did not need to.

"**Down**," said Fisk.

The pot went. The needle climbed. It took about forty seconds for the needle to come up to eighty-four, and the face said eighty-four, and Fisk read it out loud and Nessa Pike wrote it in the book on the crown of the mole with a hand she had to slow down.

The shot line went over and came down, and the shot line was a bight with a shackle in it, and the two ends of it were on the crown, in two men's hands, in about eleven fathoms of water on a flood, and nobody had a dog in and nobody had a ladder out.

**"Cut,"** said Fisk, and one of the two men on the crown took a knife and put it on the seizing wire, and the wire parted, and the two ends went into the water, and there was nothing on the surface of the earth holding anything at all.

---

It is not a quarry.

That is the thing that came up, and it came up in the first two minutes and then took the rest of the morning to be believed.

The cut is not a quarry because a quarry has a face, and a face is one thing at a time, and a face is cut back and up in steps and every man who has ever worked one knows what a step looks like. This has no step. It has a **floor**, and the floor is laid, and the floor is laid in courses of cut stone, and the courses run in lines as straight as a stretched line for about a hundred and forty feet in one direction and rather less in the other, and the stones are squared and the joints are tight, and the whole of it is a floor at forty fathoms on a coast where everything else is a slope.

He went along it. He had time; there was three and a half hours on the ground and he was a working man and he used about two.

He had a slate on a lanyard, which is what that trade is, and he wrote on it with a pencil stub in the dark, and the pencil had been no good since February and he had said so.

At the end of every course, at the near end, where a man standing in the trench laying that course would have his right hand, there is a **figure cut in the stone**, cut before the stone was set, because the cut is fresh and the arris is not rounded and the cut is a little under an inch deep and about two inches long.

He read them off with his thumb and wrote them on the slate, and then he read the slate back to himself in the dark to see whether he had read them right, which is a thing he has done since he was nineteen and has never once told anybody he does.

**One. Two. Three. Four. And so on, to forty-one, and then nothing, and then the trench turns and there are more courses with figures on them and those go on past a hundred.**

---

And at about the fourth hour of the flood, on the end of the seventh course from the edge, in the top course, there is a **socket**, cut and chiselled clean into the dressed face of one stone, and about four inches by two, and a bar of iron in it, and the bar is square and about two feet long and it is not rusted through, and a chain is made to the bar.

Voke's warp is bent round that bar in two turns and a half and the net is spread out from it across about thirty feet of the floor in a way that a man who has dragged for nineteen years can read at a glance: **a net that went down over the top of something and did not go past it and is holding on by the middle of its belly.**

"That is a good net," said Garrow Fisk, on the crown, over the pipe. "That is a good net and it has found a good place to hang and it is going to stay there until somebody is more careful than it."

---

At about two o'clock the signal came up and the pot came up and the gauge went down, and the second reading that day was **eighty-four**, and Fisk read it and Nessa Pike wrote it and did not look at the page while she wrote it.

And Calder Marr came over the gunwale of the pot in about six inches of water with the slate against his chest under his shirt, and he got up onto the boat's rail, and then he sat down on the rail instead of standing on it, and put his forearms on his knees.

"The first number is eighty-four," he said, "and the second one — " and then he stopped, and looked at the gauge, and then at the book, and the ship's hand turned the book so that he could see it.

"Eighty-four," she said. "You read it. It is the same figure twice."

"I know it is the same figure twice. I read it off the needle and the needle was at eighty-four and the first number was eighty-four and I had it in my hand and I could not get it out of my hand." He stayed on the rail. "I have been down about two hours and I have a figure in my head that is not that one, and it is not another one, and it is not a number. **It is the number of courses, and I have got it, and I cannot say what it is.**"

Nobody asked him what he meant, because it was half past two on a flood with a compressor running and a man of fifty-one in a boat behind them, and because nobody in that harbor has ever asked him a question about a thing he has seen at the bottom of something, in all the years he has been in and out of it.

---

And in the boat, at about four, with the ground starting to make again, Elias Voke stood over the slate on the thwart and read the numbers off it with his own hand, one finger going along, and got to the end of them.

"**Forty-one,**" he said. "And then a hundred and something."

"And then a hundred and something," said Calder Marr, "and I have not written the last of them and I am not going to on the back of a boat."

"Then write it when you get in," said Voke, "and I will be here, because I want to see what I have paid eleven marks for, and I have never in nineteen years wanted to see anything less."

---

*First column, entered on Thursday the eighth day of April next at about half past nine in the evening in this shed: that a compressor, a housing, a pipe and gauges were put on a boat and moored off the south end of that mole and a mile and a half out, on the top of the flood on the said Thursday, and that the compressor was charged and that the pot was charged to eighty-four pounds on a face of a hundred and fifty and that the reading was read out loud and entered, and that a man of twenty-eight named Amos Searle went down on that day on a shot line and that the two ends of that shot line were cut on the surface at about eleven o'clock in the morning while a man was forty fathoms down, and that nothing on the surface was holding anything at the time.*

*And that the holder of that writ went down on the said day at about nine o'clock in the morning on the same shot line, and that there was no first entry and nothing to enter and the notice of the thirty-first of October past does not catch three named people going into the water on a private man's own ground for that man's own purpose, and that the reason against the line of that day is that two men this yard does not know were on that compressor and one man of twenty-six was holding a hose and a pot line on that boat's rail; and that the ground of the said day is on the seaward side of the bar of four hundred yards off the outer marks and not inside it, being off the south end and round the corner from the north-west line the outer marks are on, and that the said bar has not been narrowed by anything, and that this party did not ask to have it narrowed on the seventh and did not ask for it in advance and did not ask for it in the boat, and that no person and no boat and no lead of that yard was inside that bar on the said day.*

*And that at about forty fathoms in the said cut the bottom was found to be a floor of cut stone laid in courses, and not the floor of a quarry, and that the courses run in straight lines for about a hundred and forty feet in one direction and rather less in the other, and that the joints are tight and the stones are squared, and that a figure is cut in the stone at the near end of every course, cut before the stone was set, to a depth of about an inch and a length of about two inches, and that the figures read off with a thumb and written on a slate ran from one to forty-one without a break and then went on past a hundred on courses beyond a turn in the trench.*

*And that in a socket cut and chiselled in the dressed face of one stone in the top course there is a bar of square iron about two feet long, and that a chain is made to that bar, and that the said chain is not of that yard's making and is not of any make in that harbor that anybody present could name, and that the warp of the herring boat of the second lay is bent round that bar in two turns and a half, and that the said net is spread across about thirty feet of that floor in a manner that a man of nineteen years' standing read at a glance as a net that had gone down over the top of a thing and had not gone past it.*

*And that the gauge read eighty-four a second time on the coming up, and that the two readings are the same figure, and that the holder of that writ read the first of them off the needle and could not get the figure out of his hand afterwards, and that nobody asked him what he meant, and that the reason against the line of that not-asking is that nobody in that harbor has ever asked him a question about a thing he has seen at the bottom of something and this was not the day to begin.*

*And that twelve marks, two shillings and eightpence went out of that yard's own book on the said evening, being nine marks for the compressor, the housing, the pipe, the gauges and one man off a Crown house book, two marks and eightpence for the said man of about thirty-four of a pressure house at Ternwatch for his own time, and a mark and two shillings for a man of twenty-six hired by the day for the hose and the pot line.*

*Second column, not checkable from a mole, entered on the said Thursday at about half past nine in the evening in this shed, and no name is against it: that the two ends of a shot line being cut on the surface with a man at forty fathoms is a thing a man on a mole can check if he is on the mole at the time, and that nobody in that harbor except four people were, and that two of those four were hired by the day and one of them is a woman's son and one of them is a man of fifty-one who has never once been asked to hold a line in his life and held it well.*

*And that a figure cut in a stone at the end of a course is a thing a man on a mole can check by going and looking, and that nobody in that harbor has looked, and that this party is not going to ask anybody to look, and that the reason against the line is that the reason a mason cut those figures is a reason about a course of stone and not about anything else, and that this yard has been asked one question since the twenty-eighth of March past and it is not going to go and invent a second one.*

---

**Twelve marks, two shillings and eightpence went out of this yard on the said Thursday and nothing came into it, and sixty-two marks, nine shillings and elevenpence is in the bag, which is ten thousand and thirty-nine pence. The whole of what came into that yard in the twenty-four days ended on the said Thursday is nothing, and the reason is the form of the fourth of March past and not a mood. The whole of what went out of that yard since the sixth of December past is twenty-one marks, nine shillings and tenpence in thirteen transactions at six counters, of which four are the boat of the man of the second lay at one mark and eightpence a day with line at a penny a fathom, and one is two marks and eightpence to a man of about thirty-four of a pressure house at Ternwatch, and one is a day of that set at twelve marks and two shillings.**
