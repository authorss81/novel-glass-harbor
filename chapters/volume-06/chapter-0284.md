# Chapter 284

*The Second Descent Off the South End at About Forty Fathoms, a Second Man Off a Book, and Eighty-Four Pounds on a Face of a Hundred and Fifty*

Wednesday the twenty-eighth of April, a mile and a quarter off the south end of that mole, and in the shed on that mole.

**The wall did not come down on the said Wednesday and the cistern was not charged on it, and the said Wednesday was not a wage Tuesday. Nothing came into that yard on it and two thousand two hundred and eighty pence went out of it, being fourteen marks, three shillings and fourpence, and the whole of it was for one day of a set belonging to a pressure house at Ternwatch.**

**And the far bench was not occupied on the said Wednesday at any hour, and the holder of that writ did not go over to him at any hour of it, and a man of sixty-one was not asked for anything and asked nothing.**

---

They read the six conditions out loud at the boat before a single man put a foot in her, in order, the way they have been read since the seventh of April, and it took under two minutes and nobody improved on it and nobody laughed.

Then the holder of that writ said the thing that has to be said at the boat, which is not a formality and has never been a formality.

"**The outer marks are a mile and a quarter off the head of that mole. The bar is four hundred yards off the outer marks, and the bar begins about a mile and a half out on the north-west line the outer marks are on. We are going off the south end and we are going round the corner from all of it, and we are going about a mile and a quarter on a different line, and nobody and nothing of this yard is inside that bar today, and that bar has not been narrowed by anything, and this party is not going to ask to have it narrowed, did not ask for it in advance, and is not asking for it in this boat.**"

"**Same as the seventh,**" said Fisk, and it was not a question.

"**Same as the seventh. And there is no first entry. And there is no harbor authority's book to go in, because that office's season ended on the thirty-first of March past with nothing issued and the clerk keeps his own page, and this yard's own book is not that book and is not the same thing, and the day was in that book on the Tuesday with the whole price in it before any of this was agreed, which is the only rule there is.**"

The man of fifty-one of the second lay was not in the boat. He had not asked and had not been asked and his ground was not in it.

---

The pot went down at about twenty past nine on the flood and it came up at about ten to twelve, and it was in the water two hours and fifty minutes, and it was a free descent in open water in a trench off the south end with nothing to enter.

**The gauge is a pressure gauge and the figure on it is pounds and not feet. It is a face of a hundred and fifty. It read eighty-four pounds on the eighth, ninth and tenth of April past and Fisk put a pencil mark on the face where the needle stood, so that the reading is on a gauge in a shed at Ternwatch as well as in a book in this shed and a man in that shed can put a finger on it. It read eighty-four pounds again on the said Wednesday, and the needle stood a hair past the mark, and nobody moved the mark, because the mark is the only reason the two books are the same book.**

"The face is a hundred and fifty," said Fisk, "**and eighty-four is not near the end of it, and that is the second time I have said that sentence to a man in a boat.**"

---

They put the pot on the top course, because that is the course with the socket in it, and the socket is empty and has been since the ninth of April, and the cut where the bar came out of it is old and clean.

And when the pot was landed, the lead weight came down across the belly of a herringman's net.

There is two hundred and eight fathoms of that net up out of four hundred and eleven and the belly of it is still on that floor, and it lies across about thirty feet of the made stone, and it had been lying there since the ninth of April with a chain that is now on a nail in a shed wall off it.

**The lead came down on it and about six fathoms of the belly of that net, all of which was lying loose on that stone on the Wednesday morning and not flat anywhere on it, is now flat on a course of cut stone under a weight, and it is not cut and it is not mended and it will not come up whole, and nobody in that boat cut it, and nobody in that boat could have helped it, and the ground is that man's by first refusal and this yard was on it without him and did not ask him and named the price before it agreed and did all of it right and it has still cost him six fathoms of gear.**

"That is on me," said Fisk.

"That is on the book," said Calder Marr, "**and I am going to write it in the first column, because a lead came down on a net at forty fathoms and a man can go and look at the place, and I am not going to write it in the second one and hide it in with the things we did not do.**"

---

The figure at the near end of the top course is cut in the stone about an inch deep and about two inches long, the way a figure is cut at the near end of every course on that floor, and there are forty-one of them running from one to forty-one without a break and then past a hundred on courses beyond the turn in the trench.

The forty-second is the one in the top course, under the pot, and it is not cut like the others.

"It is about a quarter of an inch deep," said Fisk, "**where the other forty-one are an inch, and the strokes are laid on the other way, cross-stroke first, and there is a man in Ternwatch who will tell you that is how a gang lays a figure when the foreman is not standing there, because I have watched both.**"

"**That is what it is.**"

"**That is what it is. Write that down. That is all of it.**"

"**Write what you said and not what it means.**"

Fisk wrote it on the slate with a stick of chalk and put the slate in the boat and did not hand it to him.

---

And here is the whole of what that day cost the holder of that writ, and it cost him the thing he said in the shed on the Tuesday before he went:

At about four o'clock he had the slate in his left hand and the pencil in his right and he could not do either, because the pencil went on the line and came off it again, twice, and the third time he put the slate down on the thwart and gave it across, and Fisk took it.

"**Take it,**" he said. "**I can hold a compass and I cannot hold that, and I told you I would not find out on the bottom which of the two I lose, and I have found out on the top of it instead, and it took me two hours and a half to lose it.**"

"And the compass?"

"**The compass is a needle and a card and a bit of looking and I can hold it. The slate is a join. I have known that since the twelfth of April and I have had it in the second column every day since, and this is the first day it has cost me anything anybody else can see.**"

Nobody wrote anything down about that in the boat, and it is in the second column of that yard's own book at about half past nine that night with a reason against the line and no name in it, and the reason is the reason that has been against that line since the twelfth of April past and is not improved on.

---

*First column, entered on Wednesday the twenty-eighth day of April next at about half past nine in the evening in this shed: that the six conditions of a first entry were read aloud at the boat at about nine in the morning of the said day in order and there was no first entry, and that the outer marks are a mile and a quarter off the head of that mole and the bar is four hundred yards off the outer marks and the said bar begins about a mile and a half out on the north-west line the outer marks are on, and that the said day was worked off the south end of that mole and round the corner from all of it and no person and no boat and no lead of that yard was inside the said bar on the said day and the said bar was not narrowed by anything and was not asked to be.*

*And that the pot was put on the bottom at about twenty past nine in the morning on the flood off the south end of that mole and came up at about ten to twelve, being two hours and fifty minutes in the water, and that the gauge is a pressure gauge on a face of a hundred and fifty and read eighty-four pounds on the said day as it read on the eighth, ninth and tenth of April past, and that a pencil mark stands on that face where the needle stood on the tenth of April past and was not moved, and that the depth of the floor is about forty fathoms off the line and no figure of depth is entered except in fathoms.*

*And that the pot was landed on the top course of the made floor at the said depth, in which course a socket is cut and chiselled in the dressed face of one stone and the said socket is empty and the bar of square iron that came out of it on the ninth of April past is in a drawer in that shed, and that when the said pot was landed the lead weight came down across the belly of a herringman's net, and that about six fathoms of the said net is now flat on that course under a weight and is not cut and is not mended and will not come up whole, and that nobody in that boat cut it and the ground is that man's by first refusal and he was not asked and was not sent for and was not in that boat.*

*And that a figure is cut at the near end of every course on that floor and that the figure in the said top course is the forty-second and is cut about a quarter of an inch deep where the other forty-one are cut about an inch deep, and that the strokes of the said figure are laid on cross-stroke first, and that the said figure was read and written down by the ship's hand's man and not by the holder of that writ, and that the holder of that writ said out loud in that boat that the said figure was to be written down as what it is and not as what it means.*

*And that two thousand two hundred and eighty pence went out of that yard's own book on the said day for that one day, being nine marks, two marks and eightpence, a mark and two shillings and two marks and eightpence, and that no day of work was booked on the said day and there is no paper in that drawer to book it against.*

*Second column, not checkable from a mole, entered on the said Wednesday at about half past nine in the evening in this shed, and no name is against it: that the holder of that writ could not hold a slate at about four in the afternoon of the said day and gave it across the thwart, and that the reason against the line of his holding of it is the reason that has been against that line since the twelfth day of April past and has no name in it, and that the said day carries no figure for that and there is no figure for a day, and that no person of that yard was in the water on the said day and none was on a lead.*

*And that the said figure in the top course differs from the other forty-one and that a difference in a made thing says the thing was made in two hands at two times and says nothing whatever about which of them is right, or why, or by whom, and that the said holder of that writ said out loud in that boat that the said difference was to be written down as what it is and not as what it means, and that the reason against the line of the saying is that a figure a man cannot check from a mole is a figure and not a finding.*

*And that the concealment is owed to this yard and was not told on the said day, and that no office was in that boat and no office answered anything on the said day, and that no clerk in any copying room explained what the said sheet is, and that Tobias Wren was not sent for and nothing was asked of him on the said day.*

---

**Two thousand two hundred and eighty pence went out of this yard on the said Wednesday and nothing came into it, and five marks, one shilling and fourpence is in the bag, which is eight hundred and sixteen pence. The whole of the money that has come into that yard since the sixth of December past is one thousand seven hundred and sixty pence, and the whole of what went out of it since the sixth of December past is seventy-nine marks, five shillings and one penny in twenty-three transactions at eight counters. The figure the bag is short of the wage of four marks does not exist and did not exist on the said day and will not come back while that bag is above six hundred and forty pence, and nobody in that shed said one word about it at any hour of it.**
