# Chapter 251

*The Drawer With Six Papers In It and a Place for One That Is Not There*

Tuesday the sixteenth and Wednesday the seventeenth of March, in the shed on the mole, and in a boat of about forty tons at the face of the second seam.

**No day of work was booked in this yard on the said Tuesday, and that is the first time since the sixth of October past that a working day could not be booked at all, and the reason against that line is not a mood and is the fourth page of a form that came up this mole in a hand on the fourth of this month.**

**And the far bench was not occupied on the said Tuesday at any hour and was not occupied on the said Wednesday at any hour, and the holder of that writ is up that coast road and did not go over to him on either of the two days, and a man of sixty-one was not asked for anything on either of them and asked nothing on either of them.**

**And the last wage Tuesday before the said Tuesday was the ninth of March past, and the number of weeks fallen due with no envelope standing against them is TWENTY-ONE and is the twenty-first of that same list, and the reason is the reason entered on the twenty-ninth of December past in the holder's own left hand and it is not improved on. And on the said Wednesday the cistern was not charged, it being neither a Monday nor a Thursday, and the wall behind the reeve's bench did not come down, it being a Tuesday and a Wednesday, and no sitting was held in that room on either of them.**

---

She counted the drawer twice, which is a thing she does, and both times it came out the same.

Six pieces of paper in it. The four, and the Crown form that went in on the fourth of March in place of a charter, and the page about that harbor that went in on the sixth. The charter's place was where the charter had been, between the acknowledgment of the twenty-ninth of November and the printer's letter, and the place was empty and clean and the wood was a slightly different colour where a thing had been lying on it for four months.

Nessa Pike read the form of the fourth of March from the top to the bottom twice more, in the standing light of a shed door at about half past eight in the morning with the wind coming off the water and finding the gap under it, and she did not read it aloud, because there was nobody in the shed, and a document read aloud to an empty shed is a thing a person does to feel better and she was not going to do it.

It was careful. That was the thing she kept coming back to. It was not a writ and it was not a licence and it was not a finding. Nothing in it suspended anything. Nothing in it fined anybody. It said, in the way these things say things, that a party which does not produce a thing on a day is not fined, because a court does not fine a poor man, and the matter simply goes on. It said the harbor authority is not the office that can give that instrument back and never was. And then, in a paragraph near the foot with a seal line at the top of it and a name on the seal line that she did not read twice because she has read that name four times in a year and knows what it costs to read it, it said that nothing in it required a party to produce any book of its own in any column, and that nothing in it required anybody to say who altered anything or at whose instance or with what intent, and that a party producing the instrument might enter in writing once any particular it wished recorded.

**The whole form asked this yard for nothing.** That was the shape of it. She sat with her back against the bench and looked at it for a while.

Then she got up and did the arithmetic she had been avoiding since the Saturday, which is that the whole of what this yard is is a piece of paper a stranger can rely on. Not the gear. The *Kittiwake* is on the hard with her bilge blocked in and no cradle under her and she has not moved since the autumn. The gear is hired at a mark a tide. The hands are two and one of them has four stitches in her right hand. The thing a hirer at the face of the second seam pays for is not a diver and it is not a pump; it is the ability to send a boat to ground that is inside a harbor line, and the ability to send a boat to ground inside a harbor line is a piece of paper, and the piece of paper is in a drawer, and the drawer is in a shed, and the shed is on a mole, and the mole is in a harbor whose authority is not the office that can give the paper back.

**There was no paper.** So there was nothing to sell. So the second seam, which is four fathoms and six joints and three hundred and fifty-one feet of the best work on this coast, would lie at the same number all week as it had lain on Friday, and a man with a compressor and a bad back who wanted the money would go to a yard in the north basin that had a paper.

She wrote it in the first column at about a quarter to nine, in her own left hand, because her right hand is four stitches and a splint and the last one comes out on the twenty-eighth at the earliest and she does not write with a hand that is not finished.

**And she did not say it out loud to anybody, because there was nobody there, and because a thing said out loud in a shed with nobody in it is a thing said out loud to the person who said it.**

---

The man of the second lay came down the mole at about half past nine on the Wednesday with his fingers in his beard, which is what he does when he has already decided something and is annoyed about the deciding.

"That mark of yours," he said. "Fouled. Weather side. I want it looked at."

"It is fouled. It has been fouled since the Sunday and you told me so."

"I told you it was fouled. I did not tell you how it was fouled." He took his fingers out of his beard and put them in his coat. "There's a boat going out at eleven either way, because I have to be at the north end at two and there's nothing to sit on and the herring's about. Your mark is fouled and if I take a man of forty tons out past that face with a mark fouled on the weather side, and a boat comes round the point the wrong way, then I have learned something about that mark on the worst possible morning of my life, and I have learned it off your ground."

"It was a mark and eightpence last autumn for the boat and you," Nessa said. "Is it a mark and eightpence now."

"It was a mark and eightpence last autumn for the boat and me and it is a mark and eightpence now for the boat and me, and I am not going up by a penny in a year in which I have not had a penny, and you can write that down as a fact about me and not as a favour." He shifted the oar. "And I want the boy and I cannot have the boy. You will take the slate left-handed and you will tell me if I go over the falls, and if you tell me I go over the falls I will say you were wrong, and you can put that in your book."

"I'll put it in the book," she said. "Left-handed and I'll say it once, and if you go over the falls I will say it twice, and if you go over the falls twice that is a different book."

He looked at her a second longer than he needed to, which from him is a laugh.

The boat went out at eleven. The tide was two hours off the top and about a hand's breadth of a half-ebb running north-east across the face, which is the worst of it and the only hour there is. The mark was where it had been set on the twelfth, four days back and not a week; she was wrong about the week and did not correct it out loud, because being wrong about a date in front of a man who is about to work is worse than being wrong quietly.

The mark was fouled on the weather side with about four inches of green.

Nessa wrote the figure down on the slate and read it back to herself before she turned the slate round, because the rule about reading a position back is a rule in this yard's own book and it is in that book because somebody got it wrong once and it cost a boy his hand.

Then she looked at the shackle.

The shackle was the ordinary shackle, an oval iron thing with a pin through it, and it was green and it was weeded on the weather side like everything else, and on the lee side, where the weather had not been working on it, there were two places about the size of a thumbnail where the iron was bright.

Bright meant handled. Fouling took a season; she knew that and he knew that and both of them had been told it by people who had been doing it forty years. **Two bright places the size of a thumbnail on the pin and on the bow of the shackle meant a thumb and a forefinger had been on that iron within a fortnight, and a hand takes time.**

"Mark it," she said. "The shackle, both places. Don't lift it."

"Wasn't going to."

They came back in at half past two.

In the shed Nessa wrote the fouling down and the two bright places down and the fact that the mark was four days old and the fouling was about four inches and neither of those two things is a season, and she wrote *both of those are true and this is what was found* and stopped, because the fourth column of that leaf did not exist yet and there was nowhere to put what it was not.

She put a mark against the line instead, which is what a person does when they know they will come back to it, and she wrote the date beside it, and she did not write a conclusion, because a conclusion is a thing you write at the top of a page and this page was not that.

Then she went down the mole and posted the hire money to the man of the second lay at the little box on the corner, one mark and eightpence, and came back up the hill, and the whole of what came into that yard in two days was nothing, and the whole of what went out of it was one mark and eightpence, and there were no two people in that yard on either of the two days who had not been in it on the day before.

---

*First column, entered on Wednesday the seventeenth day of March next at about half past six in the evening in this shed: that no day of work was booked in this yard on the said Tuesday, and that the reason against that line is the fourth page of a form that came up that mole in a hand on the fourth of that month, and that a hirer at the face of the second seam pays for a piece of paper and the piece of paper is in a drawer in that shed and the drawer is empty of it.*

*And that on the said Wednesday a boat of about forty tons went out at eleven with the man of the second lay and the ship's hand in her, hired at two marks for the boat and the man's day and one mark and eightpence paid out of that yard's own book at about half past four in the afternoon at a box on the corner of that mole, and that the hire was on a private person's account and for a private person's purpose and was not salvage and was not a claim and was not work under any instrument, and that the two of them were off the water at half past two and were never in it at any time of the day.*

*And that the mark of that yard's own, set on the face of the second seam on the twelfth of March past, is fouled on the weather side with about four inches of green, and that the shackle has two places on it about the size of a thumbnail where the iron is bright, one at the pin and one at the bow, and that the two of them were written on a slate and read back before the slate was turned round, and that the fouling and the bright places are two separate findings and are entered separately and were not entered on one line.*

*And that the fouling is not a season's fouling and the mark is four days old, and that nobody in that shed said a word about what that is, and that that silence is entered here and not improved on.*

*And that the said Tuesday was a wage Tuesday and that the twenty-first week of the list of weeks fallen due with no envelope standing against them fell due on it, and that the wage column stands at twenty-six lines and one hundred and four marks with the fifth of March the oldest and twenty envelopes on that bench, and that the column did not move on the said Tuesday and the envelope count did not move with it.*

*Second column, not checkable from a mole, entered on the said Wednesday at about half past six in the evening in this shed, and no name is against it: that the mark has been handled since it was set, and that the handling is a fortnight old or less, and that the only people on this coast who could set a mark and get a boat to it and mend a shackle in a morning are four people alive in a town four days down this coast who own a case of five bells at about five and a half fathoms inside four hundred yards off the outer marks, and that the limit they wrote themselves and signed stands, and that this yard is not going to ask them to take it off, and that the man of the second lay did not suggest it and was not asked to.*

*And that a mark on the water is a wall that moves, and that a thing on the water has to be looked at, and that the looking at is the first thing about that trade and is not the trade, and that the season of that mark is nine marks and this yard is not going to pay nine marks and is not going to ask anybody for nine marks, and that in four years nobody in that town will know whether this yard is right about the two bright places or not.*

**One mark and eightpence went out of this yard on the said Wednesday and nothing came into it on either of the two days, and sixty-nine marks, two shillings and five pence is in the bag, which is eleven thousand and sixty-nine pence, and the figure the bag is short of the wage of four marks is ten thousand four hundred and twenty-nine pence. The whole of what came into that yard in the two days ended on the said Wednesday is nothing, and the reason is the form of the fourth of that month and not a mood. The whole of what went out of that yard is one mark and eightpence at a box on the corner of that mole on the said Wednesday, and the whole of what has gone out of that yard since the sixth of December past is four marks, four shillings and eight pence in seven transactions at five counters, being a penny on the eighteenth of December past, a penny on the twenty-eighth of January instant, a penny on the thirteenth of February instant, two shillings to a bonesetter on the twentieth of February, fourpence at the post on the sixth of March, three marks and nine pence to the man of the second lay on the twelfth of March, and one mark and eightpence to the same man on the seventeenth of March instant. Nothing in a tin in that shed since the twenty-eighth of April. Three marks owing to a man of Ashtide for the eighth of August past, unmoved, and he is not in that book and nobody was sent for him. Seven pence owing to that yard's own shipwright, unpaid. Eight shillings and tenpence to a woman of that harbor with her name on the figure. Eight marks and a day of a boat owing to that harbor's own bellwright since the twenty-third of May past, unargued, and he was not asked anything in these two days and the chest was not opened. Forty marks first after food to a person of no office, unpaid since the fifteenth of October past, and that yard has not asked her once since and is not going to ask her this week. Forty marks stand whole to a boy of twenty-two, the three nots of the eighth of June past stand, the head of the third column keeps that boy's hand with a name set beside it that is neither that boy's nor the holder's, and that column has not been filled since the first of August past. The wage column stands at twenty-six lines and one hundred and four marks and twenty-one weeks have fallen due with no envelope standing against them, being the fifteenth day of August past, the fifth of September past, the sixth of October past, the third of November past, the seventeenth of November past, the first of December past, the eighth, the fifteenth, the twenty-second and the twenty-ninth of December past, the fifth, the twelfth, the nineteenth and the twenty-sixth of January instant, the second, the ninth, the sixteenth and the twenty-third of February instant, the second and the ninth of March instant, and the sixteenth of March instant, and all twenty-one are in that book in the same breath and not one after the other. Thirty-one on the chandler's note, not re-written, its three counts of weeks unmoved at twenty-two and thirteen and eleven, and it was said out loud for the fourth time in seven months on the fourth of that month and that yard said in the same breath that it will not say it a fifth time and that stands unreversed. The notice of the thirty-first of October has its four clauses and has not been lifted, narrowed, re-served or altered, and no person, no boat and no lead of that yard has been inside its first clause on either of these two days. The plate at the fifth seam is in eleven feet in that mole's own foundation stone and is not reached, not measured, not touched and not asked about, and the note against it stands open. The case of five bells is at about five and a half fathoms inside the four hundred yards off the outer marks, on nobody's schedule, was not raised and was not asked about in these two days, and the four lines of a limit four people of that coast wrote and signed are quoted in that yard's own first column and stand. The two objects of nobody's are about two hundred and seventy-nine days up the crown of that mole, about nine feet apart, under two tarpaulins, one with a corner off it since the seventeenth of June past and not lashed back, and neither has moved, and that party did not go near them on either of these two days. The bench under that pump is bare for a forty-sixth night, counting from the Sunday of the thirty-first of January instant, and it is the only bare thing in that shed.**
