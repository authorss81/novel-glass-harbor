# Chapter 253

*Nineteen Days, and a Bench at the Far End of the Shed He Did Not Go To*

Saturday the twentieth and Sunday the twenty-first of March, in the shed on the mole, and on the top of that mole at about four in the afternoon.

**No day of work was booked in this yard on the said Saturday and no day of work was booked in this yard on the said Sunday, and that is two working days in a shed with two people in it, and the reason against both of those lines is the same reason, being the fourth page of a form that came up that mole in a hand on the fourth of that month, and it is not improved on.**

**And the far bench was not occupied on the said Saturday at any hour, and was occupied on the said Sunday from about ten until about four, and the holder of that writ came up that hill at about half past two on the said Sunday and did not go over to him, and a man of sixty-one was not asked for anything on either of the two days and asked nothing on either of them, and no fragment and no report talk and no second story, and the reason against that line is the reason that has been against it every day since the sixth of October past and is not going to be improved on in this volume either.**

**And the cistern was not charged on either of the said two days, it being a Saturday and a Sunday, and the wall behind the reeve's bench did not come down, and the last wage Tuesday before the said Sunday was the sixteenth of March and the twenty-first week fell due on it and the column did not move and the reason is the reason of the twenty-ninth of December past.**

---

He came up the coast road in somebody else's cart and got off at the bottom of the mole steps at about half past two, which was late for him, and stood at the bottom of them for a while with his hand on the rail.

He was thinner. That is the whole of what a person can tell about nineteen days at a glance and it is not nothing. His forearm was bad again in a way that had a shape to it, the way a thing has a shape when it has been held in one position for four hundred miles of cart track.

"You look like a man who has been in a chair," Nessa said, coming out of the shed door.

"I have been in a chair."

She did not ask him what he had brought, because he would put it on the bench if it was going on the bench. He had a flat paper under his arm and he kept his hand on it the whole way up the steps, which told her more than the paper did.

Inside, he read the form of the fourth of March from the top to the bottom in about four minutes, standing, holding it at the bottom edge with two fingers so it did not curl.

Then he said: "There is nothing in that I could not have said out loud on a mole."

"No," said Nessa. "There is nothing in it you could not have said out loud, and the reason I could not sleep on Wednesday night is that a thing which asks you for nothing is a thing that does not need you."

He put the form back in the drawer in the place it had been, which was not the place the charter had been, and he counted the drawer, because she had watched him do it, and he came out with six.

**"Did you eat on the road?" she said.**

"I ate on the road."

"That is not an answer to the question I asked and both of us know it." She put her hand on the table. "Sit down. You are grey. I have boiled water and there is bread and there is an onion, and if you say you are not hungry I will put the onion in front of you and leave it there, and you will eat it out of spite, which is what you did in the autumn."

He sat down. He ate the onion. He did not say thank you for it and neither of them expected him to, and that is in the book and is not an insult.

Then he put the flat paper on the bench and squared it, which is a thing he does with every paper, and it was four lines in a hand that was not his and not hers, and the ink had been run over with a thumb where the cart had been rough.

"That is a copy," he said. "Of four lines out of a book of ground that is about nine miles of water nine miles from the door of the man who keeps it, and it is a copy, and I did not ask for it and he gave it to me and he was in the cart with me for ten days and he did not once ask me a single question about what I was going to do with this coast when I got back to it."

"Which four lines."

"Two of them are where the water is. One of them is a date with a month in it that is not a month anybody here uses. And one of them is a sentence, and the sentence is the reason I am going to hand you this instead of a better thing, and the sentence is this." He read it. "**We will not send a man to a coast where the man who writes things down is the one everybody remembers.**"

Nobody said anything for a moment. The pump dripped. The bench at the far end of the shed had a man on it and had had one since about ten.

"Four people," Nessa said.

"Four people. A woman of about sixty-eight who does not go out. A man who has been deaf in one ear since a warp took him. And two sisters over the water who are not sisters to anybody else I have met." He turned the paper round so that it faced her. "I asked them once, on the Wednesday, and I want that in your book in the order I said it and not improved on. I asked for a witness. Not a page. A witness, somebody alive, who has been on that water. And the man said no and the woman said no and one of the sisters said no, and then one of them read me the sentence, and I wrote it down on this, and then the man of the forty-five sat down opposite me and copied four lines out of his own book with his own hand and gave it to me and would not take anything for it and would not let me pay for the cart back down."

"And you were nineteen days and you came back with four lines and a sheet of paper."

"I came back with four lines and a sheet of paper," he said, "and the four lines are a copy of somebody else's page, and a copy of somebody else's page is not that page, and that is the fifth thing I have learned in nineteen days and it is the only one of the five that I am going to put in the first column."

He wrote it in the first column himself, standing, in his own left hand, in about the space of a paragraph, and it took him a long time, and he read it back to the woman before he read it back to himself, which is the rule in this yard and which he has taught to two other people and was taught himself by a man who is dead.

**At about four o'clock the woman at the far end of the shed stood up and put her hand on the bench on her way past, which is what everybody does to a bench they have been sitting on all day, and Calder Marr was eight feet from her, and he did not go over to him, and he did not go over to him at about ten or at eleven or at two, and he went out of the door at about four and stood on the mole for a while with his back to the shed.**

She came out after a minute.

"You could go over," she said.

"No."

"He has been on that bench since ten."

"I know what hour he has been on that bench, because it is in the book and I wrote it, and it is in the book for every day since the sixth of October and it is going to be in the book for every day until one of us is dead or one of us says why, and it is not going to be today because I am thirty-two and I am not going yet and he is sixty-one and his lungs are the inside of a wet coat and I have watched him for a hundred days and I have not learned one single thing about the inside of that coat that I did not already know from a chair on a mole in the year I was twenty-two."

"That is a long speech and you said it to a doorframe."

"I said it to a doorframe," he said, "and I meant it, and I am not going to say it to him, and if you put it in the book put down the words and not the reason, because the reason is not checkable from a mole and the words are."

---

*First column, entered on Sunday the twenty-first day of March next at about half past nine in the evening in this shed: that the holder of that writ came up that coast road on the said Sunday in a cart belonging to a man of about forty-five who sells lime and chalk, nineteen days after he went up it on the second of March, and that the price of that going was named on the night of the first of that month at about eleven o'clock, before the day and not after it, being four days there and four days back, two men off the water and about twenty-four marks, and that the price was not paid then and has not been paid since, and that the said man took no money for the cart and would not let the holder pay for the way back, and that there is no figure for that and there is not going to be one.*

*And that four lines in a hand that is not that of either person in that shed came down that coast road in a coat and were put on a bench on the said Sunday, being two of them a place where the water is, one of them a date with a month in it that is not a month anybody on this coast uses, and one of them a sentence, and that the said man did not ask for the copy and it was given to him, and that the said four lines are a copy of somebody else's page and are not that page.*

*And that the sentence so brought down is that four people will not send a man to a coast where the man who writes things down is the one everybody remembers, and that the holder of that writ asked them once on the Wednesday of that going for a witness, alive, who had been on that water, and that the woman of about sixty-eight said no and the man who has been deaf in one ear since a warp took him said no and one of two sisters over the water said no, and that the sentence was read to him after the three noes and not before, and that all four of them are alive and that the holder has entered that they are alive and has not entered where they keep the page.*

*And that on the said Sunday the holder of that writ read the form of the fourth of March from the top to the bottom in about four minutes standing, held it at the bottom edge with two fingers, said that there was nothing in it he could not have said out loud on a mole, and put it back in the drawer in a place that is not the place the charter had been, and counted the drawer and came out with six.*

*And that the far bench was occupied on the said Sunday from about ten until about four, that the holder of that writ came up that hill at about half past two and did not go over to him at any hour of the day, and that a man of sixty-one was not asked for anything on either of the said two days and asked nothing on either of them, and that there was no fragment and no report talk and no second story.*

*Second column, not checkable from a mole, entered on the said Sunday at about half past nine in the evening in this shed, and no name is against it: that the holder of that writ lost four days to the body on that road, being two on the way up and two on the way down, and that a day lost is a day and carries no figure, because there is no figure for a day, and that the reason against the line is the reason that has been against it every day since the sixth of October past and has no name in it and is not going to have one in this volume.*

*And that nineteen days produced four lines and a sheet of paper and no witness, and that the four lines are a copy of somebody else's page and are not that page, and that a copy of somebody else's page is the fifth thing learned on that road and is the only one of the five that went in the first column, and that the other four went nowhere at all and are not going to be improved on by anybody.*

*And that the answer of the four people on that coast is not a refusal of this yard and is a refusal of a coast, and that the difference between the two is the whole of the difference between a channel and a door, and that the holder has entered the difference and has not decided what to do about it.*

**Nothing came into this yard on either of the said two days and nothing went out of it on either of them, and sixty-nine marks, two shillings and five pence is in the bag, which is eleven thousand and sixty-nine pence, and the figure the bag is short of the wage of four marks is ten thousand four hundred and twenty-nine pence. The whole of what has gone out of that yard since the sixth of December past is four marks, four shillings and eight pence in seven transactions at five counters, and nothing has come into that bag since the fourteenth of March past, being the last day of work at the face of the second seam on a hirer's own account, and the reason that there has been nothing since is the form of the fourth of that month. The wage column stands at twenty-six lines and one hundred and four marks, the fifth of March the oldest, twenty envelopes on that bench, and twenty-one weeks have fallen due with no envelope standing against them, and the column did not move on the said Saturday or on the said Sunday and neither of those two days was a wage Tuesday. Nothing in a tin since the twenty-eighth of April. Three marks owing to a man of Ashtide, unmoved, and he is not in that book and nobody was sent for him. Seven pence to that yard's own shipwright, unpaid. Eight shillings and tenpence to a woman of that harbor with her name on the figure. Eight marks and a day of a boat owing to that harbor's own bellwright since the twenty-third of May past, unargued, and he was not asked anything in these two days. Forty marks first after food to a person of no office, unpaid since the fifteenth of October past, and that yard has not asked her for anything since the twenty-sixth of December past and did not ask her today. Forty marks stand whole to a boy of twenty-two, the three nots stand, the third column is not filled, nobody is appointed to it, and the boy's hand is at the head of it. The far bench was not occupied on the said Saturday and was occupied on the said Sunday, and that is two days in the twenty-one days of this volume so far, being the nineteenth and the twenty-first, and neither brother has said why on either of them. The two objects of nobody's are about two hundred and eighty-three days up the crown of that mole, about nine feet apart, under two tarpaulins, and neither has moved. The bench under that pump is bare for a fiftieth night, counting from the Sunday of the thirty-first of January instant, and it is the only bare thing in that shed.**
