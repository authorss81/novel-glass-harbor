# Chapter 291

*Wednesday the Fifth of May, Six Hundred and Forty-Nine Pence In, a Man of Sixty-One With a Boat, and a Crown Seal Named Out Loud in a Shed*

Wednesday the fifth of May, in the shed on the mole, and on the water off the face of the second seam in the morning.

**The wall did not come down on the said Wednesday and the cistern was not charged on it, and the said Wednesday was not a wage Tuesday. Six hundred and forty-nine pence came into that yard and nothing went out of it.**

**And the far bench was not occupied on the said Wednesday at any hour, and the holder of that writ did not go over to him at any hour of it, and a man of sixty-one was not asked for anything and asked nothing, and nothing was put on that bench and read as a gift.**

---

The man of about fifty-five was in the shed at eight with his own hand on the doorframe and the tide making up behind the mole. He had a boat of his own inside the harbor line with about four feet of her own ground under her and eleven feet under the other end, and he wanted somebody to go and look at what was under her, and he said why out loud before anybody asked him for one.

"**I have had her up once and there is something along her starboard side and I want to know whether it is mine or whether it was there before I bought her. That is the whole of it. I am not claiming anything and I have not got a charter and I would not know what to do with one.**"

"**Then it is a day's work and not a salvage and not a claim and not work under any instrument,**" said Calder Marr, "**and it goes in the first column as what it is. Three marks for a day's work, being four hundred and eighty pence, and it is not going up by a penny in a year in which it has not had a penny. A mark and eightpence for the boat and the man in her, being a hundred and sixty-eight. And a penny of line.**"

The man of about fifty-five counted it onto the bench in three pieces and Nessa Pike wrote the whole of it in her own left hand in about four minutes, and six hundred and forty-nine pence came into that yard on the fifth of May, and it is the first money that has come into that yard since the eleven marks of Elias Voke on the sixth of April past, and nobody said one word about that at the time.

They were off the water at half past two and were never in it at any hour of the day.

---

The man of sixty-one came up those steps at about four in the afternoon and he was not asked to and he came, and he had been on no page of that yard's book since a long time before the winter, and he is a retired salvage foreman and he is half deaf and he uses a whistle and he has a boat of about nineteen tons that is not this yard's and a hand of about forty he cannot do without.

He was asked for the boat. He was not asked for anything else.

"**Your boat,**" said Calder Marr. "**Off the south end, on Thursday. There is a day of plant and air coming off a book at Ternwatch and that day is fourteen marks, three shillings and fourpence, and I am going to say the four figures out loud before anybody agrees to any of it, and then I am going to say the thing I cannot pay for, and then what we are not going to do down there. Say your condition now and say it before I have said mine.**"

"**My boat goes and I take her,**" said Tobias Wren. "**Nobody stands in her but me at the wheel. And if anybody in that boat gives me an order I put her alongside and I go home, and that is the whole of it and I am not going to improve on it and I do not want to be thanked.**"

"**That is your condition and it goes in the book.**"

---

Then the ship's hand read the head of the copy out loud, because the copy was out of the drawer and on the bench with the parting corner showing, and the name of the chamber at the head of the face is a name this yard has not been able to place in any book in that shed or in the sheet behind the tide table.

She read it once and then stopped with her finger under it. Tobias Wren leaned over the bench about four inches and read it himself, and took a while, and then he said:

"**I have seen that name cut in stone. On a stair. Cut in the cheek of it, about two feet above the bottom step, and the letters about the size of my thumb, and I was on it in a boat in a summer in the sixties with a man who is dead and I have not been back to that coast since and I was about nineteen.**"

Nobody asked him a second question. He was not asked where the stair was, nor what the stair was for, nor why a name of that kind would be cut into the cheek of it, and the reason against the line of all three is in that yard's own book.

"**Now the other thing,**" he said, "**because you have been chewing at it since Tuesday and you have not asked me and I am going to say it anyway, and I am saying it because I have seen one and not because I know anything.**"

"**Go on.**"

"**A Crown Seal is not a stamp.**" He said it the way a man says a thing he has had to explain to a younger man twice. "**It is a round of glass the size of a saucer in a collar of metal, with a wheel in the collar and a screw through it, and it sits in a bed cut in a sill, and the screw goes down onto the bed and takes a load off a thing made of the same glass as itself. It is a machine and it is a seal at the same time, because the mark of its name is cut into the round and the round is the mark, and the collar is what holds the mark on. The collar is a good collar and the screw goes in about two turns and stops.**"

He turned his hand over and looked at it and did not go on for a moment.

"**And it is a term and not a lock,**" he said. "**Whoever cut that thing wanted a thing held and wanted it held for so long and no longer, and they cut a figure on the sill beside it so that the next man would know when. That is the whole of a Crown Seal. It holds a thing for so long and then it wants looking at, and there is nobody in this country who ever looks at one.**"

The pump dripped.

"**You have not seen one in this country,**" said Nessa Pike.

"**No.**"

"**Then it is a thing a man saw once, and this yard is not going to enter it in the first column,**" she said, "**and the reason against that line is that this yard is not going to spend the once on a word.**"

"**Agreed,**" said Calder Marr. "**The once is tomorrow.**"

---

Then the price, and he named it out loud at that bench, in the same words, and in the same order, and nothing was agreed to until the whole of it had been said.

"**Nine marks a day for the compressor, the housing, the pipe, the gauges and one man off that book, being one thousand four hundred and forty pence. Two marks and eightpence a day for the ship's hand's man, being three hundred and twenty-eight. A mark and two shillings a day for a hand on the hose, being a hundred and eighty-four. And a second man off that same book at two marks and eightpence a day, being three hundred and twenty-eight. That is the day at fourteen marks, three shillings and fourpence, and it is the same four figures the eighth, ninth and tenth of April past came to, to the penny, and the same four the twenty-eighth of April came to, to the penny, and the book sends the same man every time.**"

"**And who pays it,**" said the ship's hand.

"**We do not. We cannot.**" He put his thumb on the edge of the bench. "**There are five marks, one shilling and fourpence in that drawer and the day is fourteen marks, three shillings and fourpence, and a yard does not agree to a thing it cannot pay for.**"

Garrow Fisk, who had been in and out of that shed all week doing nothing that gets into a book, said: "**Then it does not happen.**"

"**It does,**" said Tobias Wren.

Nobody asked him how.

"**Say it out loud so it is a record and not a story,**" said Nessa Pike, "**and then I will write it in the first column.**"

"**It goes off a book at a pressure house at Ternwatch, and a man of sixty-one who did a job in that house in the autumn can ask at that counter, and the man has not got to be asked to be sent, because that book sends the man.**" He took his hand off the bench. "**And not one penny of it is going out of your book, and if a man in a city ever wants to know why a day's set went to a mole four hundred miles from any pressure house, it will come out with a man's name in it.**"

"**A man this yard has paid is a man this yard can be asked about,**" said Calder Marr. "**And I do not know what a man this yard is being paid by is, and I am telling you that out loud before I agree to it and not after.**"

"**No,**" said Tobias Wren. "**You don't. That is the whole of what I have got and it is not enough and I am not going to improve on it.**"

---

And then the limits, and the holder of that writ put them on the bench the way a man puts down a tool he does not intend to pick up again.

"**One. The bar is four hundred yards off the outer marks and we are going off the south end and round the corner from all of it, and nobody and nothing of this yard is inside that bar tomorrow, and the bar has not been narrowed by anything and is not going to be asked to be.**"

"**Two.** The six conditions are read at the boat before a foot goes in her, in order, and there is no first entry, and there is no harbor authority's book to go in, and this yard's own book is not that book."

"**Three.** The man of fifty-one is not asked and is not sent for and his ground is not in it, and the sentence about a man this yard has paid is not improved on because it is being used again. **And four, and this is the one I am putting on the bench at four o'clock on a Wednesday, before anybody has agreed to a single thing.**"

He looked at the copy on the bench with the parting corner at the fold.

"**If there is a chamber down there and if it is open enough to be read, this yard will not read it and will not take one piece of it out. And the reason is that a record a man cannot check is worth less than nothing, and this yard has said that in six movements out loud, and the day we proved it on somebody else is the day we stopped being the party that says what it is not.**"

"**Say the price of it,**" said Mara Venn, who had come up the steps about ten minutes before and had said nothing until then, and there were four of them in that shed and nobody else.

"**The only proof this yard will ever have of anything about a drowned city will be in a thing that closed,**" he said, "**and a proof in a thing that closed is a proof nobody can check, and this yard will lose it on a day when it wanted it and will not be able to prove that it ever had it. And I am naming that now because the rule of this yard is that the price is named before the day and not after it, and a man who names it after it is a man who did not have to.**"

"**Agreed,**" said Nessa Pike, "**and it goes in the first column with the date on it and the price in it, and the fourth column takes the other half.**"

---

*And in the fourth column of the leaf ruled on the twenty-seventh of March past, that evening, in the ship's left hand, the nineteenth line:*

*What it is not: — that a machine with a figure cut beside it is not a lock and is not a keeper and is not answered to, and that a man who saw one thirty years ago has not given this yard a fact; and that a day's set got off a man's book is not a day's work bought by this yard; and that a refusal made before anybody has agreed to anything is a decision and not a mood.*

---

*First column, entered on Wednesday the fifth day of May next at about half past nine in the evening in this shed: that six hundred and forty-nine pence came into that yard on the said day, being three marks for a day's work, one mark and eightpence for a boat and a man in her, and a penny of line; that the work was done on a private person's account and for a private person's purpose and was not salvage and was not a claim and was not work under any instrument; that the party went off the water at half past two in the afternoon and was never in it at any hour of the said day; and that no person of that yard went inside the bar of four hundred yards off the outer marks.*

*And that the price of a descent to be made on the Thursday following was named out loud at that bench at about four o'clock in the afternoon of the said day, before anybody had agreed to any part of it, being nine marks a day for the compressor, the housing, the pipe, the gauges and one man off a Crown house book, two marks and eightpence a day for the ship's hand's man, a mark and two shillings a day for a hand on the hose, and a second man off the said book at two marks and eightpence a day, the whole of the said day being fourteen marks, three shillings and fourpence; and that not one penny of the said sum went out of that yard's own book, and that the said day's set was asked for at a pressure house at Ternwatch by a man of sixty-one with a boat that is not that yard's, and that the said man said out loud at that bench that a man in a city would be able to find out why a day's set went to a mole four hundred miles from any pressure house, and that it would come out with his name in it.*

*And that a man of sixty-one gave a condition on the said afternoon, being that his boat goes and he takes her and that nobody stands in her but him at the wheel, and that he said he was not going to be thanked; and that the holder of that writ said out loud at that bench on the said afternoon that if there is a chamber and if it is open enough to be read, this yard will not read it and will not take one piece of it out, and he named the price of that before it was agreed to, being that the only proof this yard will ever have of anything about a drowned city will be in a thing that closed, and that this yard will lose it on a day when it wanted it and will not be able to prove that it ever had it.*

*Second column, not checkable from a mole, entered on the said Wednesday at about half past nine in the evening in this shed, and no name is against it: that a word was said out loud in that shed on the said afternoon by a man of sixty-one who has seen a thing of that name once, more than sixty years ago, cut in the cheek of a stair on a coast he has not been back to, and that the said word is not entered in the first column, and that the reason against that line is that a man's memory of a thing he saw a long time ago is a thing a man can go and look at exactly once; and that the said man was not asked where the stair was, nor what the stair was for, nor who cut the name, and that the reason against all three of those lines is the reason against the line of asking a man of sixty-one three questions in one afternoon.*

*And that a day's set was got off a man's book and not off this yard's, and that the reason against the line of this yard's paying for it is that there were five marks, one shilling and fourpence in the drawer and the day was fourteen marks, three shillings and fourpence, and that the reason against the line of a man this yard is being paid by is that this yard has a rule about a man it has paid and has not got a rule about a man paying for it.*

---

**Six hundred and forty-nine pence came into this yard on the said Wednesday and nothing went out of it, and there is one thousand four hundred and sixty-five pence in the bag, which is nine marks, two shillings and one penny. Fourteen marks, three shillings and fourpence of plant and air went off a book at a pressure house at Ternwatch and not one penny of it came near this yard, and a man of sixty-one asked for it, and a man of sixty-one is not on a page of this book as a favor and is not going to be. The two objects of nobody's are three hundred and twenty-eight days up the crown of that mole and the bench under that pump is bare for the ninety-fourth night, and neither of them has moved, and nobody went near either of them on the said day.**
