# Chapter 263

*The Cut Made on a Bench Above the Water, and the Book That Is Not There*

Wednesday the seventh of April, in the shed on the mole, on the bench outside it, and at the head of that mole in about four fathoms of water on the tide.

**The said Wednesday was not a wage Tuesday and the column did not move, and the wall did not come down and the cistern was not charged, and no figure is entered against a day this party did not see.**

**And a day of a boat and four fathoms of line went out of this yard on the said Wednesday, and the pot was charged at about four in the afternoon to eighty-four pounds by a gauge on a face of a hundred and fifty that a man could see the needle of, and a shackle of this yard's own iron with three turns of wire on the water side of it was made on that bench in about an hour and a half and was not paid for in money.**

---

They made the cut on the bench outside the shed because the light was better there and because the ship's hand does not do ironwork in a shed if she can help it.

It was a shackle about the size of a man's fist, out of a bar of iron that had been in that yard's rack since October and was not on anybody's account, and it was swaged and closed cold, and the closing was done with a drift and three blows of a sledge, and the whole of that took about a quarter of an hour and the whole of the rest of it took the rest of the morning.

The three turns were made on the water side.

That is the side that faces the sea when a thing is hanging. It is not the side this yard turns on, because this yard turns on the landward side and has turned on the landward side for as long as anybody can remember, and because when you part your eyes you look at the side that is towards you and the side that is towards you when you are standing at a bench with the water behind you is the landward side, and so that is the side you make and that is the side your hands know.

So Nessa Pike turned the shackle over on the anvil and made them on the other, and the wire went on in three turns, tight, and the ends were cut flush and the whole was seized with a serving of smaller wire and the serving was put on with the turns a half turn out of true, which is a fault everybody makes and which is a fault you can see.

"A hand that made this in the dark in a hurry would not have had the serving half a turn out," said she. "That is in the book too, and it is in the fourth column and not the first, because a thing that is not is not a thing a man did."

"It is the same fault as the one on the second seam," said Calder Marr.

"It is the same fault as the one on the second seam," she said, "and there is no more to it than that, and if anybody in this harbor says that the shackle on that seam was made by this yard I will read them the fourth column out loud, and it is in the fourth column, and it says a man on a mole with a spanner in his hand is not seeing a name."

Then she rove a bight of shot line through it and seised the bight with five turns and a thimble, and Fen Barrow, who was twenty-six and had been hired that morning at a mark and two shillings a day, held the bight while she made the turns, and he was careful with it in the way a man is careful with a thing that is going into the water on somebody else's account.

"That is the cut," said Garrow Fisk, from the step. "I have seen a hundred of them and about four of them were made by the same pair of hands twice."

"It is the cut," said Nessa Pike, "and it is going on the bight, and on Thursday morning when the pot is on the bottom and the gear is off him, somebody standing on that crown is going to take a knife to that wire and the two ends are going to fall in the water and there is going to be nothing on the surface holding anything at all."

"And you have made it so that the man in the water is a man and not a handle," said Fisk.

"I have made it so that the man in the water is a man and not a handle," she agreed, "and it is my shackle and my turns and my bight, and if it parts then it is a line that parted and not a hull and not a bell, and there is a difference and the difference is the whole of what I have done since Saturday."

---

The six conditions were read aloud at the boat at about half past nine, in order, by Calder Marr, and the boat was the man of the second lay's, and he was in it, and he was not on the crew, and the tide was making.

They are short. They are six sentences and this yard has read them out at a boat more times than anybody has counted, and nobody in that harbor has ever asked for them and nobody has ever been refused them.

Then the seventh thing, which is not a condition and is the only part of it that has changed since the thirty-first of March past.

**"A stated reason goes in the harbor authority's book before anybody goes in the water," said Calder Marr, "and I am going to say the rest of that sentence out loud and I am going to say it in front of all of you, because we are three days from putting a man under this quay's own foot and nobody in this boat is going to find out what I knew from a book on Wednesday.**"

Nobody said anything.

"**There is no harbor authority's book to go in.** The ledger is spent, and the season of that office ended on the thirty-first of March, and no instrument has been issued out of it since, and the clerk keeps his own page and will not show it to anybody who has not asked him for it and this yard has not asked him. The thing we used to write the reason in is a spent ledger and a clerk's page, and there is no season on either. That is the whole of it. That is what has happened to us between the first of March and this morning.**"

"Then where does it go," said Fen Barrow, who was twenty-six and who had not been in that shed before the seventh of April.

"It goes in ours," said Calder Marr, "in the first column, with the day on it, and the reason against the line, and the price named before it was agreed, which was named yesterday, in that shed, out loud, with about nine people on the crown of this mole, and I am not going to tell you the price in this boat because you do not need it and because a price in a boat is a price in about nine boats by Friday."

"And that is not the same thing," said Nessa Pike. She had said it before, on the previous day, but she said it again in front of the boat, and she said it to the man of the second lay and not to Calder Marr. "**Our book is our book. It is not that office's book, and it is not the same thing, and it cannot be the same thing, and this yard is going into the water on Thursday without a thing it had for four months, and I am going to write that sentence in the second column in my own hand and I am not going to call it a formality and I am not going to call it the same.**"

"No," said the man of the second lay. "It is not. I have been on this water nineteen years and I have had a reason in a book twice and I have had a reason in somebody's own book about nine hundred times, and I will tell you the truth about the difference, and the difference is that the first one could be read by a man in a city in four years and the second one can be read by me, tonight, at about nine o'clock, and the second one is the only one of the two that anybody in this harbor will ever look at."

Then the man of the second lay asked the question a man of nineteen years asks when he is told a place, which is how far off, and Calder Marr said about a mile and a half off the south end, on the top of the flood, in the cut, in about forty fathoms, and Fisk said the rest of it before the man had finished asking.

"**Then that is the edge of the bar and not the middle of it. The outer marks are a mile and a quarter off the head of this mole and the bar is four hundred yards off the outer marks, and the bar begins about a mile and a half out on that line, and that line is the north-west one and the cut is off the south end and round the corner from all of it.** Nobody has narrowed that bar and this party is not going to ask anybody to narrow it, and on the three days we are going out there is no person and no boat and no lead of this yard inside it, and if there is one of the three of us in there tomorrow I want to hear it from you before the boat is cast off and not after."

"Then put it in the boat," said Calder Marr, "and put it in the book with the day on it and with the price on it, and not on its own."

"It was never on its own," said Fisk. "Nothing is on its own."

---

The pot was charged at about four in the afternoon on the crown of the mole with the compressor's own pipe and a foot of hose and a tap, and the needle went up and stopped, and Fisk read the dial out loud in a hand that is not the yard's, and Nessa Pike wrote it down, and he did not sign it.

"**Eighty-four pounds,**" he said, "on a face of a hundred and fifty, and eighty-four is not near the end of it. And on Friday it will be a different number, and on Saturday it will be another one, and those three numbers are the only figures in this whole business that anybody can go and look at, and they are on a gauge in a shed at Ternwatch as well as in a book in this shed, and a man in that shed can put a finger on the mark the pencil left on the face where the needle stood, and if the two ever differ then a man in a city can take one of the two books and put them side by side and that is the end of the argument."

"What is the argument," said Fen Barrow.

"There is no argument," said Fisk. "That is what I am telling you. I am telling you there is no argument, and I am telling you it now, before there is one, and if I am wrong about that then somebody will use the two books against one another in about four years, and I would rather it was written down that I said this first."

---

*First column, entered on Wednesday the seventh day of April next at about half past nine in the evening in this shed: that a shackle of that yard's own iron was made on a bench outside that shed on the said Wednesday in about an hour and a half, and that it was swaged and closed cold with a drift and three blows of a sledge, and that three turns of seizing wire were put upon it on the water side of it against that yard's own practice, and that the serving on those turns was put on half a turn out of true; and that a bight of shot line was rove through that shackle and seised with five turns and a thimble, and that a man of twenty-six named Fen Barrow was hired that morning at a mark and two shillings a day and held the bight while the turns were made.*

*And that the serving being half a turn out of true was entered in the fourth column of the leaf ruled on the twenty-seventh of March past and not in the first, and that the reason against that is that a thing which is not is not a thing a man did.*

*And that the six conditions of a first entry were read aloud at the boat in order by the holder of that writ at about half past nine in the morning on the said Wednesday, and that there was no first entry and nothing to enter and the said six conditions were read because that is the practice of that shed and not because there was a thing to enter.*

*And that the holder of that writ then said out loud in that boat that there is no harbor authority's book to go in, the ledger being spent and the season of that office ended on the thirty-first of March past and no instrument issued out of it since, and that the ship's hand said out loud in that boat that that yard's own book is not that office's book and is not the same thing and cannot be the same thing, and that that yard is going into the water on the eighth day of that month without a thing it held for four months.*

*And that the man of about thirty-four of a pressure house at Ternwatch said out loud in that boat at about half past nine in the morning on the said Wednesday that the outer marks are a mile and a quarter off the head of that mole and the bar of four hundred yards off the outer marks begins about a mile and a half out on the north-west line, and that the said cut is off the south end and round the corner from it, and that nobody has narrowed that bar and that party did not ask to have it narrowed and did not ask for it in advance and did not ask for it in that boat, and that no person and no boat and no lead of that yard is inside that bar on any of the three days named, and that the holder of that writ said out loud in the same boat that the said line goes in the first column with the day and the price on it and not on its own, and that the said man said out loud that nothing is on its own.*

*And that one mark and twelvepence went out of that yard's own book on the said Wednesday, being a day of the boat of the man of the second lay at one mark and eightpence and four fathoms of line at a penny a fathom.*

*And that the pot was charged at about four in the afternoon on the said Wednesday to eighty-four pounds on a face of a hundred and fifty by the gauge, and that the said gauge's reading was read out loud by the said man of about thirty-four of a pressure house at Ternwatch in a hand that is not that of the ship's hand, and that he did not sign it, and that the said figure was entered in this book, and that a mark was left on the face of that gauge at the place where the needle stood and the same face is in a shed at Ternwatch.*

*Second column, not checkable from a mole, entered on the said Wednesday at about half past nine in the evening in this shed, and no name is against it: that a party that goes into the water with a reason in its own book and not in the harbor authority's book is not doing the same thing as a party that goes into the water with a reason in that office's book, and that the two were never the same thing, and that the first column of this book can be looked at by anybody and always has been, and that what cannot be looked at by anybody is whether the reason was stated, and that the reason was stated, in a boat, at about half past nine in the morning, in front of five people.*

*And that the man of about thirty-four said out loud that a Crown house book has a column for a depth and no column for a year, a name or a reason, and that he entered that in this column in his own hand and unsigned, and that a line in this column in a hand that is not the ship's hand is a thing this book has not had before and that the shipwright has not objected to.*

*And that a hand hired at a mark and two shillings a day was paid for a day at a mark and two shillings a day, and that there is no figure for the half of a party and that the half of a party is a woman with four stitches in a right hand writing with her left hand at about twice the time it takes her, and that she was not entered as a half in this book and is not to be.*

---

**One mark and twelvepence went out of this yard on the said Wednesday and nothing came into it, and seventy-four marks, twelve shillings and sevenpence is in the bag, which is eleven thousand nine hundred and ninety-one pence, and the whole of what has gone out of that yard since the sixth of December past is nine marks, seven shillings and twopence in twelve transactions at five counters. And with that figure the sentence this book has carried since the third week of March past — the figure the bag is short of the wage of four marks — came to an end, and it did not come to an end by being paid, it came to an end because there is no longer any such figure to state, and there has not been one since the seventh of April, and nobody in that shed said one word about it at the time and a woman in a shed is the only person in this story who would have.**
