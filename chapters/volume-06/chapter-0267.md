# Chapter 267

*Nine Feet of Chain, a Boy Who Came Down on His Own Account, and a Day With No Figure Against It*

Sunday the eleventh of April, in the shed on the mole, and on the bench outside it.

**Nothing was charged on the said Sunday and nothing was wiped on it and no wage fell due on it, and no money came into that yard on it and no money went out of it, and the figure in the bag is the figure it was on Saturday night, and that has happened twice this month and both of the times were because nothing could be done and not because nothing needed doing.**

**And the holder of that writ lost a day to the body on the said Sunday, and that day is in the second column with a reason against the line and no name in it, and a day lost is a day and carries no figure, because there is no figure for a day. And the far bench was not occupied on the said Sunday at any hour and the holder of that writ did not go over to him at any hour of it, and a man of sixty-one was not asked for anything and asked nothing.**

---

They hung the chain on a nail.

That is the whole of the handling it got, and it took about a minute, and it is in the first column because a man did it and not because it means anything.

Nessa Pike put it over the head of a nail in the shed wall at about nine in the morning, the way you hang a coil of small stuff where it is out of the wet, and then she stood and looked at it from the middle of the shed, and the chain went from the nail down about two feet of wall because nine feet of chain is nine feet of chain and the wall is not nine feet high and it lay on the floor of that shed in a heap the width of a dinner plate.

A man of about fifty of the second lay came in at half past ten on his own account, because there is nothing to do on a Sunday, and looked at it, and did not touch it.

"That is not chain that came off a boat," he said. "I have seen that. That is chain that was made. And I could not use nine feet of it and I would not take it if I could, because a man does not turn up on a Sunday in a shed and take a thing off a nail."

"I know that," said Nessa Pike. "I am not hanging it up for anybody. I am hanging it up because it is wet and it is not a thing to be put in a box and there is no box in this shed that is not the drawer."

---

Garrow Fisk's book had gone back to Ternwatch in the coach on the Friday night and came up the coast road again on the Saturday night, behind the coach that had brought the man of thirty-one up, and that coach is a night's road each way, and he read the entry out at about ten on the Sunday morning with the shed door open and about four people in the street.

The boy came down at about eleven on his own account.

He had not been on the stone since the fifteenth of May past and there was nothing on the stone to be on, and he came down the mole steps in a coat that had been somebody's, and he stood at the door of that shed the way a man stands at a door he has not been asked into for six months.

Nobody put him on anything.

Nobody asked him one word about the omissions he has entered, and there were four of them above his named line and they were four and they were still four, and this party did not open that subject and would not open it in a shed with a chain on a wall and a man asleep upstairs.

Nessa Pike read the whole of the said day's entry back to the two of them at about half past eleven, from the book, in her own left hand, slowly, all of it, including the second column, and she read the reason against the line for the lost day out loud with the same voice she read the rest in, which is what a woman does who has been in this harbor long enough to know that a thing read in a different voice is a different thing.

And when she had finished, the boy said the only thing he said all day.

"**The second column is getting full,**" he said. "It was not getting full in the spring."

"Yes," said Nessa Pike. "It is. Sit down or go, and do not stand in that door being right at me."

He went, at about twelve, and he was not on the stone on the Monday either, and nobody has asked him since.

---

**The day is gone. That is the way it goes and there is nothing else to be said about it and everybody in that shed has known somebody it went from.**

He was up at eight and he sat down at about half past nine to write the Saturday's figures out fair, from the ship's slate, in the book, and he got two lines and then he put his hand flat on the bench and left it there, and he said, "I have got to go and stand at something for a bit," which is the only sentence anybody in this harbor has ever used for it, and which he did not coin and has not been asked about.

So he stood at the bench outside the shed for the rest of the morning with his hands on it, and did not go inside, and the ship's hand brought him out a cup of water at about eleven and no more than that, because a cup of water and no more is the whole of what this shed has ever known how to do for anybody.

He was better in the afternoon and he was not better in a way anybody could check, and he worked on the fourth column of the leaf in the afternoon, and he got one more line into it than he would have got in the morning, which is nothing at all and which Nessa Pike entered in the book as a figure because it is the only figure she had.

---

What it is not, under the head written with a blunt nib on the twenty-seventh of March past, that same afternoon, in the ship's left hand:

*What it is not: — that nine feet of chain and a bar of square iron is not a record, and that a socket cut and chiselled in the face of one stone is not a signature, and that a figure cut in a stone at the end of a course is not a hand, and that a hand of about four hundred years putting a figure in a stone is not a person that this yard has heard of, and that this yard is not going to say anything about it, and that the reason is the fourth of the same line and the fourth of the same line is that the reason a mason cut those figures is a reason about a course of stone and not about anything else.*

---



"**Amos Searle, twenty-eight, of this house, is carried as unfit for five weeks from the ninth day of April,**" he said, "and then as fit for light work only, and then as fit, on the fifth day of June, **and the word light is the house's word and not mine and I have been in this trade nineteen years and I have never once been able to make a man light.** And that is the whole of what is in that book about him and it is four lines and there is no money in it and the money is the other book, and the other book says the same thing in one line with a figure at the end of it, and I am not going to read you that figure out in a street because it is his and not mine."

"How long is five weeks," said a man at the door.

"**Thirty-five days,**" said Fisk. "And he is twenty-eight and he is a holder and a holder's trade is a man's back and a man's arms and a man's hands, and there is no light work in it, and somebody else will be carrying what he cannot carry, and that man will be carrying it on the same book and at the same figure, and there is nothing in the world I can do about that and I have not stopped thinking about it since Saturday morning."

---

*First column, entered on Sunday the eleventh day of April next at about half past nine in the evening in this shed: that a chain of nine feet was hung on a nail in the wall of that shed at about nine in the morning, the way a coil of small wet work is hung where it is out of the wet, and that it lay on the floor of that shed in a heap about the width of a dinner plate, and that it was not measured and it was not cleaned and it has not been touched since the said morning.*

*And that a man of about fifty of the second lay came into that shed on the said morning on his own account and looked at that chain and did not touch it, and said that it was not chain that came off a boat and that it was chain that was made, and that he could not use nine feet of it and would not take it if he could, and that the ship's hand said that it is not hung up for anybody and that there is no box in that shed that is not the drawer.*

*And that a boy of twenty-two came down that mole on the said Sunday at about eleven on his own account, and that nobody put him on anything, and that the ship's hand read the whole of the said day's entry back out of that book in her own left hand, including the second column, and that the boy said one thing and the one thing he said was that the second column is getting full, and that it was not getting full in the spring, and that he went at about twelve and was not on the stone on the Monday and has not been asked anything since.*

*And that no question was asked that boy on the said Sunday about the omissions he has entered, and that the four of them above his named line are four, and that this party did not open that subject.*

*And that one line was entered in the fourth column of the leaf ruled on the twenty-seventh of March past, and that the ship's hand entered one figure in the book on the said afternoon, being the number of lines that could be got into that leaf in the afternoon, and that the reason against that line is that a figure is a thing a man can check and the day is not.*

*Second column, not checkable from a mole, entered on the said Sunday at about half past nine in the evening in this shed, and no name is against it: that the holder of that writ lost the said Sunday to the body, and that the reason against that line is the reason that has been against that line on every lost day since the sixth of October past and has no name in it and is not going to have one, and that a day lost is a day and carries no figure because there is no figure for a day, and that it was lost on a Sunday, and that it is not the first time that a day has been lost on a Sunday in this book and that nobody has counted the Sundays.*

*And that the man of about thirty-four read out of that book in that shed on the said Sunday morning at about ten, with the door open and about four people in the street, and that the said book had gone back to Ternwatch in the coach on the Friday night and came up the road in the same coach on the Saturday night, behind the coach that brought the man of thirty-one, and that the entry carried in it is that the man of twenty-eight is unfit for five weeks from the ninth of April and as fit for light work after and as fit on the fifth of June, and that he said that the word light is his house's word and not his and that he has been in that trade nineteen years and has never been able to make a man light, and that he said that a holder's trade is a man's back and a man's arms and a man's hands and that there is no light work in it and that somebody else will be carrying what he cannot carry on the same book and at the same figure, and that the figure is that man's and not his and was not read out in a street.*

*And that a boy of twenty-two said in a shed door that the second column is getting full and that it was not getting full in the spring, and that this is entered in the first column because a boy said it, and that what is entered in the second column is that a boy of twenty-two knows which column this yard writes its refusals in and knows that it has begun to fill, and that nobody asked him how he knew.*

---

**Nothing came into this yard on the said Sunday and nothing went out of it, and twenty-four marks, seven shillings and sevenpence is in the bag, which is three thousand nine hundred and thirty-one pence. The whole of the money that has come into that yard since the sixth of December past is one thousand seven hundred and seventy-six pence. The whole of what went out of that yard since the sixth of December past is sixty marks and tenpence in seventeen transactions at six counters. The wage column stands at twenty-six lines and one hundred and four marks and there are twenty envelopes on that bench, and twenty-four weeks have fallen due with no envelope standing against them, and the last wage Tuesday was the sixth of April and the next is the thirteenth. Nothing in a tin in that shed since the twenty-eighth of April past. Three marks owing to a man of Ashtide, unmoved, and he is in no book and nobody was sent. Eight marks and a day of a boat owing to that harbor's own bellwright since the twenty-third of May past, unargued, and he was not asked anything in these ten days and the chest was not opened. Forty marks first after food to a person of no office, unpaid, and nothing was asked of her and nothing was written back. Forty marks stand whole to a boy of twenty-two, the three nots stand, the third column is not filled, nobody is appointed to it, and the boy's hand is at the head of it. A working harbor of that coast is poorer on the eleventh day of April than it was on the twenty-third day of February past: one herring berth of two is out, the third lay filled on the first of April and is a lay short of two and stays a lay short, the north berth is out, the mole-head light is not on because a light costs oil, the fire party's hose is dead until the spring, the tide gang is four men short, the hard at Ashtide is unanswered, and a reeve's office shut for the season on the thirty-first of March with nothing issued out of it.**
