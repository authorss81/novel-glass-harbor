# Chapter 652

*Tuesday the Third Day of May, the Eightieth Wage Tuesday, the Column on the Lid of That Step Read Out at About Ten in the Morning in Four Minutes and Standing at Twenty-Six Lines and One Hundred and Four Marks and Not Moved for the Eightieth Time, the Chest With the Twenty Envelopes in It Shut from Ten Until Four, the Eightieth Week Standing Fallen Due at the Close of That Day, an Account Entered at About Eleven in the First Column of a Close-Sea Boat of About Forty Tons Going Alongside the Bell Buoy on the Saltmarch Weatherway at About Six on the Morning of Sunday the Second and Taking the Moorings Up and Going Off at About Half Past Seven, the Name of That Boat Not Given and Not Asked for Twice, and a Belt of Green on the Shackle About an Inch Thick With the Shackle Bright Where the Green Is Not, Nothing Read Out of a Gap and No Figure Moved by One, the Bag at Twenty-Eight Pence, Being Two Shillings and Fourpence, the First Column at Three Hundred and Eighty-Five and the Second at One Thousand Seven Hundred and Ninety-Nine*

Tuesday the third day of May, with about forty people on the boards at the back of that market from ten in the morning, a yard below that market with a shed standing in it, and about nine people in that shed at half past five that afternoon.

**The eightieth wage Tuesday, and not a rota day and not a wipe, and that butt behind the market covered from eight until four with no charge on it. On the lid of that step the column stood at twenty-six lines and one hundred and four marks and the man of about thirty-one of the second lay read it out at about ten and it took him four minutes and it did not move, and behind that door the chest with the twenty envelopes in it stood shut from ten until four. The eightieth week stands fallen due at the close of today behind the seventy-ninth, and nobody in that market has ever asked that man what he thinks of reading the same lid every Tuesday and nobody is going to start. At about eleven a woman of about twenty-four of the third lay came into that yard with a wet sleeve and put an account on the bench stone and it went into the first column with the day and the hour against it and no name against it and no name of any boat against it. Nothing went onto the money sheet and nothing came off it, and a hundred and sixty-eight pence came in at about ten and a hundred and sixty-eight went out at about four, and what it finished the day with under that slate was twenty-eight pence, being two shillings and fourpence. Set under a wage of four marks, being six hundred and forty pence, that is six hundred and twelve pence short, being three marks eleven shillings.**

**Nine things in this harbor can be had off a wall by anybody with the patience to want them. That notice of the seventeenth of May past, three hundred and fifty days. A bellwright of this harbor, not asked for, three hundred and fifty-two. Two objects of nobody's on that crown, six hundred and ninety days. The bench under that pump, bare for the four hundred and fifty-sixth night. The two lights at about a mile off that point, two hundred and seventy-four days. The six-line sheet at the back of that end stall, its two hundred and twenty-ninth day. The paper of four lines in Fish Street, its two hundred and forty-fourth. The nine-line sheet with a seal on it in that front street, its two hundred and sixty-first day. A man of about fifty-one, two hundred and ninety-two days without a word to anybody about a page of six names. Five papers are on that board at the head of that mole and two have never been opened by anybody in this harbor and nothing has been nailed on that board this month. Those four lines have not come out of an upper room over that market and nothing has ever been written under the fourth of them, and the answer to them is still no on four out of six with the fifth not asked. In that boathouse a basket stands with the paper in it and nothing else in it. About two sea miles off the head of that mole lie nine hulls this afternoon and they were nine there on the twenty-first of January, and nobody in this harbor has said that any of them has eased. A man of about twenty-nine can hear out of the left side of his head and it is one hundred and fifty days, one more than yesterday, and that is not a gift, no figure is set against it, and he is not on the water.**

---

The lid was read out at about ten in the morning in four minutes and it did not move, and the man of about thirty-one of the second lay has read the same lid out on eighty Tuesdays and the number has not changed on any of them, and the chest with the twenty envelopes in it stood shut behind that door from ten until four.

Behind the eighty stands behind the seventy-ninth, and behind that one stands behind the seventy-eighth, and there is nobody in that market who has ever asked what those envelopes are for because nobody in that market has ever been told.

---

At about eleven a woman of about twenty-four of the third lay came into that yard with her sleeve wet to the elbow and put an account on the bench stone in forty-eight words and would not read it out.

"**A close-sea boat of about forty tons went alongside the bell buoy on the Saltmarch Weatherway at about six in the morning on Sunday the second, put two people onto it in a dinghy, took the moorings up off the ring and went off about half past seven.**"

That is forty-eight words and that is the whole of the account and she would not read it out and she did not give the name of the boat and this yard did not ask her for it and did not ask her for it a second time.

Then she said the thing that is worth writing down, and she said it standing, not sitting.

The buoy was fouled before that boat came and not after. She can tell the difference because the green was on that shackle in March and the shackle was bright where the green was not, and a bright shackle under a belt of green is a shackle somebody handled. And there is about an inch of that belt on it now, and an inch is three months and not three years, and this buoy was foul in March and it was foul in April and it was foul on Friday and none of that was the doing of a Crown boat that was there for an hour and a half.

---

At about four in the afternoon the man of about thirty-two said out loud, in about nine people, in about one hundred and ninety-six words, what this yard is not.

"**A buoy on a route nobody here holds is not this yard's to take back, and the route it is on is the only one anybody on this coast is holding at the moment, and this party does not hold it either. We lost the Morrow Line in a storm that was not a weather and nobody has said one word about that on nine pages. A Crown boat took up a mooring this morning and I am not going to say out loud in this shed that that was wrong, because I have not checked one thing about it and the woman who did check it says it was there before the green.**

**And I will say the other thing, which is that this yard is not the body that decides where any boat goes, and that has been true on every page this yard has written and it is going to be true this morning whatever anybody wants it to be. If that buoy is wanted back then the people who put it there are the ones who have to say so, in their own words, on their own page, and not in mine.**"

Nobody agreed with him and nobody argued with him and nobody thanked him and about four people in that nine heard the whole of it and the rest of that market was not told.

---

And at about half past five on that Tuesday the man who keeps the leaf took the shortfall out loud at that bench stone, in about two hundred and forty-seven words, to about nine people.

"**One hundred and one thousand two hundred and thirty-six pence short, being six hundred and thirty-two marks, nine shillings and eightpence. Not one line on that sheet since the twentieth of April and not one hand on it since. Ten thousand six hundred and forty pence has ever gone into it, being sixty-six marks, six shillings and eightpence. Four hundred and fifty pence is still on that board in the front street. That butt behind that market stood covered from eight until four with no charge on it, because a rota day is a Monday and a Thursday and today is neither.**

**Twenty-six lines and one hundred and four marks on that lid for the eightieth time, read out at about ten in four minutes by a man of about thirty-one of the second lay who is not on the water and has not been on the water, and the eightieth week stands fallen due at the close of today. And a hundred and sixty-eight pence in at ten and a hundred and sixty-eight out at four and twenty-eight plus one hundred and sixty-eight less one hundred and sixty-eight is twenty-eight, and under that slate is twenty-eight pence, being two shillings and fourpence, and that is six hundred and twelve pence short of the wage of four marks, being three marks eleven shillings. No tin in that shed has been opened since the twenty-ninth of April past and nothing that happened this morning came out of it.**"

**An account went into that leaf at about eleven with the day and the hour against it and no name against it and no name of any boat against it, and I am going to say what that account is worth, because nobody else in this yard is going to. It is worth nothing as evidence and it is worth a great deal as a date. A boat went alongside that buoy at about six on Sunday morning and took a mooring up and was gone by about half past seven, and a woman of about twenty-four has been looking at that ring since March and can tell a bright shackle from a fouled one at a distance of thirty yards in this light, and the fouling was there before the boat came.**

**And here is what I have not got an answer to and am not going to get one to today. A boat that takes up a mooring is not a man and cannot be argued with in a shed, and it did not say anything, and there is nothing in the world to be cross-examined about a boat. If I write down that a Crown boat went alongside a bell buoy that only one route on this coast is holding, then in nine days somebody in Veyr will write down the hour and the tide and the colour of the paint and it will be a different afternoon with a different boat on it, and that is not the same thing, and it is going to be read in this harbor as if it were. So what I have written is what a woman said, and when she said it, and what she could see.**

Nobody was thanked for the arithmetic and nobody was thanked for forty-eight words that nobody read out loud, and that shed stood empty by about six, and that leaf had an account in it and a lid read out in four minutes and no figure on it had moved.

**THE COLUMN ON THE LID OF THAT STEP STOOD AT TWENTY-SIX LINES AND ONE HUNDRED AND FOUR MARKS AND WAS READ OUT AT ABOUT TEN IN THE MORNING IN FOUR MINUTES AND DID NOT MOVE, AND THE CHEST WITH THE TWENTY ENVELOPES IN IT STOOD SHUT FROM TEN UNTIL FOUR, AND THE EIGHTIETH WEEK STANDS FALLEN DUE AT THE CLOSE OF THAT DAY. AN ACCOUNT WENT INTO THAT LEAF AT ABOUT ELEVEN WITH THE DAY AND THE HOUR AGAINST IT AND NO NAME OF ANY BOAT AGAINST IT, BEING THAT A CLOSE-SEA BOAT OF ABOUT FORTY TONS WENT ALONGSIDE THE BELL BUOY ON THE SALT MARCH WEATHERWAY AT ABOUT SIX ON THE MORNING OF THE SECOND, PUT TWO PEOPLE ONTO IT IN A DINGHY, TOOK THE MOORINGS UP OFF THE RING AND WENT OFF AT ABOUT HALF PAST SEVEN, AND THAT THE GREEN ON THAT SHACKLE WAS ON IT IN MARCH AND IS ABOUT AN INCH THICK NOW AND THAT AN INCH IS THREE MONTHS AND NOT THREE YEARS. THE MONEY SHEET IS AT ONE HUNDRED AND ELEVEN THOUSAND EIGHT HUNDRED AND SEVENTY-SIX PENCE OUT AGAINST TEN THOUSAND SIX HUNDRED AND FORTY PENCE IN AND IS ONE HUNDRED AND ONE THOUSAND TWO HUNDRED AND THIRTY-SIX PENCE SHORT. NOTHING WAS READ OUT OF A GAP AND NO FIGURE ON THE SHEET OF THE FOURTEEN WENT UP OR DOWN BY ONE. UNDER THAT SLATE IS TWENTY-EIGHT PENCE, BEING TWO SHILLINGS AND FOURPENCE.**

*First column, entered on Tuesday the third day of May at about six in the evening in that shed: that the said day was the eightieth wage Tuesday and was not a rota day and was not a wipe, and that the butt behind that market was covered from eight in the morning until four in the afternoon with no charge on it; and that the column on the lid of that step was read out at about ten in the morning in four minutes by a man of about thirty-one of the second lay and stood at twenty-six lines and one hundred and four marks and did not move, and that the chest with the twenty envelopes in it was shut from ten until four; and that the eightieth week stands fallen due at the close of that day; and that at about eleven in the morning a woman of about twenty-four of the third lay came into that yard with her sleeve wet to the elbow and put an account of forty-eight words on the bench stone and would not read it out, and that it went into this column with the day and the hour against it and no name against it and no name of any boat against it, and that the account is that a close-sea boat of about forty tons went alongside the bell buoy on the Saltmarch Weatherway at about six in the morning of Sunday the second, put two people onto it in a dinghy, took the moorings up off the ring and went off at about half past seven; and that she did not give the name of that boat and this yard did not ask her for it and did not ask her for it a second time; and that she said out loud standing up that the green on that shackle was on it in March and the shackle was bright where the green was not, and that a bright shackle under a belt of green is a shackle somebody handled, and that there is about an inch of that belt on it now, and that an inch is three months and not three years, and that this buoy was foul in March and foul in April and foul on Friday and none of that was the doing of a boat that was there an hour and a half; and that at about four in the afternoon the man of about thirty-two said out loud, in about nine people and in about one hundred and ninety-six words, that a buoy on a route nobody here holds is not this yard's to take back, that this party does not hold that route either, and that this yard is not the body that decides where any boat goes, and that if that buoy is wanted back then the people who put it there are the ones who have to say so in their own words on their own page and not in his; and that nobody agreed with him and nobody argued with him and nobody thanked him and about four people in that nine heard the whole of it and the rest of that market was not told; and that nothing went onto that money sheet and nothing came off it, and that a hundred and sixty-eight pence came into that yard at about ten in the morning and a hundred and sixty-eight went out at about four in the afternoon, and that twenty-eight plus one hundred and sixty-eight less one hundred and sixty-eight is twenty-eight; and that the first column takes one entry a day whatever else that day prints, and that three hundred and eighty-three and one is three hundred and eighty-four at the close of it, and that the second takes five a day and one thousand seven hundred and eighty-nine and five is one thousand seven hundred and ninety-nine.*

*Second column, not checkable from a mole, entered on the said Tuesday at about six in the evening in that shed, and no name is against it: that at about ten in the morning the reason I have written down four minutes is that the number of minutes is the only figure on that Tuesday anybody outside this yard can get at, and the figure on the lid is the same as it was on the first of these eighty Tuesdays and a man who reads it out eighty times knows exactly how long it takes and that is worth writing down because in five years it will be five minutes and the lid will be on a wall in another town; and that at about eleven in the morning the reason I put no name of a boat against that account is that the woman did not give one and did not want to, and a yard that writes down the name of a boat off a woman's mouth is a yard that has made her account into a charge sheet, and an account and a charge sheet are two different pieces of paper and the second one is what the first one refuses to be; and that at about four in the afternoon the reason I wrote that the man of about thirty-two said this yard is not the body that decides where any boat goes, in his own words and in nine people, is that it is the third time this yard has said that out loud in front of somebody who wanted a boat moved, and each time it has been said it has been said after the boat was wanted and not before, and a rule that is only ever spoken after the fact is a habit and not a rule, and that is the fault this yard has and it is mine; and that at about half past four in the afternoon the reason I have put nothing in this column about who took that mooring up is that a boat is not a man and there is nothing in the world to be cross-examined about a boat, and that if I write down the hour and the paint and the tide then in nine days somebody in Veyr will write down the hour and the paint and the tide of a different afternoon with a different boat on it and this harbor will read it as if it were the same thing, and that at about five in the afternoon the reason I said out loud in that shed that the account is worth nothing as evidence and a great deal as a date is that a woman who has been looking at a ring since March is the only kind of witness this coast has got, and that she cannot read a column and does not want to and was not asked to; and that at about half past five in the afternoon the reason this slip opens on the shortfall and not on the account is that the shortfall is the only thing in this yard that has been the same every day for sixty days, and if a man is going to write down one figure that has not moved then he had better start with that one.*
