# Chapter 665

*Monday the Sixteenth Day of May, a Rota Day and the Hundred and Fiftieth Charge on That Cistern, Eleven Trips by Hand and Walked on by None of It and the Covers Left Lying on It from About Eleven Until Four, a Man of About Sixty-One Who Came to That Harbor in April and Pays by the Week over a Chandler's Shop Standing at the End Stall at About Half Past Twelve and Asking That the One of the Four Names That Is His Be Taken Off That Page of Days, and the Man Who Keeps the Leaf Writing Beside It That the Figure Against That Line Goes From Four to Three and Does Not Go Anywhere Else, Nothing Read Out of a Gap and No Figure Moved by One, the Money Sheet Untouched and the Bag at Twenty-Eight Pence, Being Two Shillings and Fourpence, the First Column at Three Hundred and Ninety-Eight and the Second at One Thousand Eight Hundred and Sixty-Four*

Monday the sixteenth day of May, with about forty people on the boards at the back of that market from ten in the morning, a yard below that market with a shed standing in it, and about nine people in that shed at half past five that afternoon.

**A rota day and not a wage Tuesday and not a wipe, and that cistern charged in eleven trips by hand between about eight in the morning and about eleven and walked on by none of it, and the hundred and fiftieth charge, and the covers left lying on it from about eleven until about four in the afternoon. At about half past twelve a man of about sixty-one, who came to that harbor in April and pays by the week over a chandler's shop two doors up that lane, stood at that end stall and asked that the one of the four names against the Morrow Line that is his be taken off that page of days, and he asked it in about nine people and nobody argued with him and nobody asked him why, and the man who keeps the leaf wrote beside the four that the figure against that line goes from four to three and went no further than that. Nothing went onto the money sheet and nothing came off it, and a hundred and sixty-eight pence came into that yard at about ten in the morning and a hundred and sixty-eight went out of it at about four in the afternoon, and under that slate at the close of the day is twenty-eight pence, being two shillings and fourpence, and no tin in that shed has been opened since the twenty-ninth of April past.**

**Nine things in this harbor can be had off a wall by anybody with the patience to want them, and not one of the nine was found by looking for it. That notice of the seventeenth of May past has stood three hundred and sixty-three days. A bellwright of this harbor has not been asked for three hundred and sixty-five days, and his chest stays shut. Two objects of nobody's on that crown stand seven hundred and three days. The bench under that pump has stood bare for the four hundred and sixty-ninth night. Two lights at about a mile off that point have been two hundred and eighty-seven days without a figure against them. A six-line sheet at the back of that end stall is on its two hundred and forty-second day. The paper of four lines in Fish Street is on its two hundred and fifty-seventh. A nine-line sheet with a seal on it in that front street is on its two hundred and seventy-fourth day. A man of about fifty-one has said nothing to anybody about a page of six names for three hundred and five days. Five papers are on that board at the head of that mole and two of the five have never been opened by anybody in this harbor, and nothing has been nailed on that board this month, and in that boathouse a basket stands with a paper in it and nothing else in it and nobody has put a hand in to look. About two sea miles off the head of that mole lie nine hulls this afternoon and they were nine there on the twenty-first of January, and nobody in this harbor has said that any of them has eased. A man of about twenty-nine can hear out of the left side of his head and it is one hundred and sixty-three days, one more than yesterday, and no figure is set against it, and he is not on the water.**

---

That cistern was charged in eleven trips by hand between about eight and about eleven and walked on by none of it, and it is the hundred and fiftieth, and that is the same eleven trips and the same four men and the same stairs on the inside of that wall, and the covers went on at about eleven and came off again at about four.

---

At about half past twelve that man of about sixty-one came down to that end stall where he had been sitting on Saturday, and he was not asked to come, and nobody had sent for him.

He said that his name is on a page of days against a route he has never been on in his life and that he came here in April to get a room and that he has asked the people he works for whether he was ever asked to put it there and that they told him he was not.

He said that he is not able to read and that he put a mark and not a name and that he did not know the page was a page of days.

He said that he is not saying anybody did it on purpose and that he does not know anything about anybody's hands and that he was not at that bench stone on Friday and does not know what a rule is for.

---

Then he asked for it to be taken off.

Nobody argued with him. Nobody asked him why. Nobody thanked him. A woman of about thirty of no office stood about two yards away and did not say anything at all, which was the correct thing to do and which cost her something to do, and a man of about fifty-three with a boat of about fourteen tons said one short thing, which was that a man who asks to come off a page has more right to be on it than a man who never asked.

Nobody agreed with him.

---

At about one in the afternoon the man who keeps the leaf took the piece of chalk and rubbed that name out on that page of days, and he wrote the day and the hour beside the three that were left, and the figure at the head of that line, which had been four since Wednesday the ninth of May, went to three and stayed at three.

He did not scratch the name out. It is still legible under the chalk on that page and anybody with a thumb can get it back, and he wrote that on the face of the page too, and he wrote why: because a rubbed-out name that cannot be read back is a name that has been made into nothing, and this yard does not make people into nothing.

---

At about half past two the man of about thirty-two of that harbor was asked, in about nine people, whether removing that name was the right thing to do.

He said it was, and then he said the half of it that nobody else in that yard was going to say, which is that the man who asked has just been made, by this yard's own page, into a man who was used, and that a yard that can do that to somebody by writing down what a person's own hand did is a yard that has become the thing it spends its whole time refusing to be.

Nobody argued with him. The woman of about thirty of no office said that she did not want that yard to become that thing either and that the two of them were in the same position on that page and had been since Wednesday, and that neither of them was going to win it.

---

At about four in the afternoon the man who keeps the leaf wrote it in the first column with the day and the hour against it and wrote the figure of three against the Morrow Line with the day and the hour beside it, and wrote that the sum of that page of days is now eighteen and that it was nineteen on Wednesday the eleventh of May and that both figures are readable and the day and the hour are against each.

He did not write what the man of about sixty-one said about his hands on Friday, and nobody in that yard has asked him about it.

---

And at about half past five on that Monday the man who keeps the leaf took the shortfall out loud at that bench stone, in about four hundred and ninety-nine words, to about nine people, and about four of those nine were the woman of thirty of no office and the man of about thirty-two and a man of about thirty-one of the second lay.

"**One hundred and one thousand two hundred and thirty-six pence short, being six hundred and thirty-two marks, nine shillings and eightpence. One line on that sheet since the twentieth of April and not one hand on it since. Ten thousand six hundred and forty pence has ever gone into it, being sixty-six marks, six shillings and eightpence. Four hundred and fifty pence is still on that board in the front street. That cistern was charged in eleven trips by hand this morning between about eight and about eleven and walked on by none of it, and it is the hundred and fiftieth, and the covers lay on it from about eleven until about four.**

**A hundred and sixty-eight pence in at ten and a hundred and sixty-eight out at four and twenty-eight plus one hundred and sixty-eight less one hundred and sixty-eight is twenty-eight, and under that slate is twenty-eight pence, being two shillings and fourpence, and that is six hundred and twelve pence short of the wage of four marks, being three marks eleven shillings. No tin in that shed has been opened since the twenty-ninth of April past.**

**A man of about sixty-one asked at about half past twelve today to be taken off that page of days and he asked it in about nine people and nobody argued with him and nobody asked him why and nobody thanked him, and at about one this afternoon the figure at the head of that line went from four to three and the sum on that page went from nineteen to eighteen, and both figures are readable and the day and the hour are against each.**

**I want three things said about that. One: I did not send for him and nobody in that yard sent for him. He came because his name is on a page and four days ago a woman read that page out in a market of sixty people. Two: he is not able to read and he put a mark and not a name and he did not know what he had done. Three: I did not scratch it out, and I want that on the record twice, because a rubbed-out name that cannot be read back is a name that has been made into nothing, and this yard does not make people into nothing even by accident and least of all by being right.**

**And the fourth thing, which is the one I do not like. That man of about thirty-two said, and he is right, that this yard's own page has just made a person into a man who was used. I have spent this week refusing to write one word about two names and two hands, and the result of that care is that the only thing this harbor can say out loud about that man of about sixty-one is that somebody put him on a page. That is not a defence of the care. That is what the care did.**"

Nobody was thanked for the arithmetic and nobody was thanked for a name that came off a page, and that shed stood empty by about six, and that name was still legible under the chalk on that page of days, and the wooden tally slate was still lying flat on that bench stone with nothing on it.

**THAT CISTERN WAS CHARGED IN ELEVEN TRIPS BY HAND BETWEEN ABOUT EIGHT IN THE MORNING AND ABOUT ELEVEN AND WALKED ON BY NONE OF IT AND IT IS THE HUNDRED AND FIFTIETH CHARGE, THE COVERS LAYING ON IT FROM ABOUT ELEVEN UNTIL ABOUT FOUR IN THE AFTERNOON. AT ABOUT HALF PAST TWELVE A MAN OF ABOUT SIXTY-ONE WHO CAME TO THAT HARBOR IN APRIL AND PAYS BY THE WEEK OVER A CHANDLER'S SHOP TWO DOORS UP THAT LANE ASKED AT THAT END STALL THAT THE ONE OF THE FOUR NAMES AGAINST THE MORROW LINE THAT IS HIS BE TAKEN OFF THAT PAGE OF DAYS, AND HE SAID THAT HE IS NOT ABLE TO READ AND THAT HE PUT A MARK AND NOT A NAME AND THAT HE DID NOT KNOW THE PAGE WAS A PAGE OF DAYS, AND THAT HE IS NOT SAYING ANYBODY DID IT ON PURPOSE AND THAT HE WAS NOT AT THAT BENCH STONE ON FRIDAY AND DOES NOT KNOW WHAT A RULE IS FOR. NOBODY ARGUED WITH HIM AND NOBODY ASKED HIM WHY AND NOBODY THANKED HIM. AT ABOUT ONE IN THE AFTERNOON THE FIGURE AT THE HEAD OF THAT LINE WENT FROM FOUR TO THREE AND THE SUM ON THAT PAGE WENT FROM NINETEEN TO EIGHTEEN, AND THE NAME WAS NOT SCRATCHED OUT BUT ONLY RUBBED WITH CHALK AND IS STILL LEGIBLE AND CAN BE READ BACK. THE MONEY SHEET IS AT ONE HUNDRED AND ELEVEN THOUSAND EIGHT HUNDRED AND SEVENTY-SIX PENCE OUT, BEING SIX HUNDRED AND NINETY-NINE MARKS AND THREE SHILLINGS, AGAINST TEN THOUSAND SIX HUNDRED AND FORTY PENCE IN, BEING SIXTY-SIX MARKS, SIX SHILLINGS AND EIGHTPENCE, AND IT IS ONE HUNDRED AND ONE THOUSAND TWO HUNDRED AND THIRTY-SIX PENCE SHORT, BEING SIX HUNDRED AND THIRTY-TWO MARKS, NINE SHILLINGS AND EIGHTPENCE, AND IT CARRIES ONE LINE AND NO SECOND. NOTHING WAS READ OUT OF A GAP AND NO FIGURE ON THE SHEET OF THE FOURTEEN WENT UP OR DOWN BY ONE. UNDER THAT SLATE IS TWENTY-EIGHT PENCE, BEING TWO SHILLINGS AND FOURPENCE.**

*Second column, not checkable from a mole, entered on the said Monday at about six in the evening in that shed, and no name is against it: that at about one in the afternoon the reason that name was rubbed and not scratched is that a scratch is a way of saying a thing is finished and a rubbing is a way of saying a thing is not there now and can be got back, and that this page of days is going to have to be readable in twenty years by somebody who does not know any of us; and that at about half past two in the afternoon the reason the man of about thirty-two was asked whether it was the right thing to do is that everybody in that yard already thought it was and a question nobody is asked is a yard that has already decided, and this harbor has refused that for thirteen volumes; and that at about three in the afternoon the reason I have not written what that man of about sixty-one said about hands on the Friday is that he told me he was not there and does not know what a rule is for, and that is the answer to the question I wanted to ask him on Friday, and it came back on Saturday without being asked, and that is not a small thing and it is not going into this column as a small thing; and that at about four in the afternoon the reason both figures are left readable on that page and not only the new one is that a page which shows only what is true today cannot be checked tomorrow, because tomorrow's figure has nothing to be different from, and the whole standing of that leaf in this yard is that a stranger can put both numbers on it and see the day between them; and that at about half past four in the afternoon the reason the sum of that page is written here in this slip as eighteen and not nineteen is that nineteen is the sum that was said out loud in nine people on the eleventh of May, and that a figure which has been said out loud to people must stay said out loud to people, and that a figure which has only ever been on a page need never have been said out loud at all.*
