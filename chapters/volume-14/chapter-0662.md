# Chapter 662

*Friday the Thirteenth Day of May, Not a Rota Day and Not a Wage Tuesday and Not a Wipe, the Butt Behind That Market Covered from Eight Until Four With No Charge on It, a Man of About Thirty-Two of That Harbor Going Into the Page of Days With a Rule and Measuring the Height of Four Names and Finding Two of Them Written at the Height of a Hand and Two of Them Written at the Height of Another, and the Two at the Second Height Being Neither of Them One of the Two That Wrote the Other Two, Nothing Read Out of a Gap and No Figure Moved by One, the Money Sheet Untouched and the Bag at Twenty-Eight Pence, Being Two Shillings and Fourpence, the First Column at Three Hundred and Ninety-Five and the Second at One Thousand Eight Hundred and Forty-Nine*

Friday the thirteenth day of May, with about forty people on the boards at the back of that market from ten in the morning, a yard below that market with a shed standing in it, and about nine people in that shed at half past five that afternoon.

**Not a rota day and not a wage Tuesday and not a wipe, and no charge placed on that cistern and its covers lying on it from nine in the morning until four in the afternoon. At about two in the afternoon a man of about thirty-two of that harbor went into that page of days with a rule and measured the height of the four names that stand against the Morrow Line, and two of them stand at the height of one hand and two of them stand at the height of another, and the two at the second height are neither of them one of the two that wrote the other two. Nothing went onto the money sheet and nothing came off it, and a hundred and sixty-eight pence came into that yard at about ten in the morning and a hundred and sixty-eight went out of it at about four in the afternoon, and under that slate at the close of the day is twenty-eight pence, being two shillings and fourpence, and no tin in that shed has been opened since the twenty-ninth of April past.**

**Nine things in this harbor can be had off a wall by anybody with the patience to want them, and not one of the nine was found by looking for it. That notice of the seventeenth of May past stands at three hundred and sixty days. A bellwright of this harbor has not been asked for three hundred and sixty-two days, and his chest stays shut. Two objects of nobody's on that crown stand seven hundred days. The bench under that pump has stood bare for the four hundred and sixty-sixth night. Two lights at about a mile off that point have been two hundred and eighty-four days without a figure against them. A six-line sheet at the back of that end stall is on its two hundred and thirty-ninth day. The paper of four lines in Fish Street is on its two hundred and fifty-fourth. A nine-line sheet with a seal on it in that front street is on its two hundred and seventy-first day. A man of about fifty-one has said nothing to anybody about a page of six names for three hundred and two days. Five papers are on that board at the head of that mole and two of the five have never been opened by anybody in this harbor, and nothing has been nailed on that board this month. In that boathouse a basket stands with a paper in it and nothing else in it and nobody has put a hand in to look. About two sea miles off the head of that mole lie nine hulls this afternoon and they were nine there on the twenty-first of January, and nobody in this harbor has said that any of them has eased. A man of about twenty-nine can hear out of the left side of his head and it is one hundred and sixty days, one more than yesterday, no figure is set against it, and he is not on the water.**

---

That butt behind that market was covered from eight in the morning until four in the afternoon, because a rota day is a Monday and a Thursday and today is a Friday, and nobody has ever printed that rule anywhere except on a slip.

---

At about two in the afternoon the man of about thirty-two of that harbor put a rule on the bench stone against that page of days and measured the four names that stand against the Morrow Line.

He did it because on Thursday a woman found that two of the four are not on that route's own page, and because a yard that has found a difference has to find out how wide the difference is before it decides whether the difference is anything.

Two of those names stand at the height of one hand. Two of them stand at the height of another hand, and that hand is a smaller hand and it comes down further at the end of a letter than the first one does.

The two at the first height are the two that are also on the Morrow Line's own page.

---

The two at the second height are not.

That is a third piece of information and it took about nine minutes, and it is the first thing found this week that is not a matter of what a name is. A name is a name and a name can be on two pages or one. A height of writing is a thing about the same pen.

Nobody in that yard has ever kept a record of handwriting. There is no column for it anywhere in this harbor and there is not going to be one, and that is not a gap anybody is going to be asked about.

---

At about half past two a woman of about forty-four with a chandler's counter four hundred yards up that lane stopped at that shed door on her way back from the market and looked at that page of days over the man's shoulder, and did not go in, and said one thing into the doorway, which was that the height of a name is not a thing anybody has ever been asked for, and then went up the lane.

Nobody called her back and nobody answered her.

---

At about half past three a woman of about thirty-nine who mends net came past that bench stone with a basket, and she looked at the two names and said that they were written by somebody who writes with their wrist and not their fingers, and that there is one such hand in this harbor and she is not going to say whose it is and nobody has asked her.

That is not an answer and it is not an accusation. She was thanked for nothing and nobody thanked her for that either, and she went on to the market with her basket.

---

At about half past three the man of about thirty-one of the second lay, who reads that lid out on a Tuesday and has never been asked to do anything else in this harbor, said that he was there in April when that page of days was started, and that he remembers the whole of that morning, and that he does not remember four names because there were four names. Nobody argued with him and nobody asked him where he was in April, because where he was in April is not a question this yard has ever asked him.

---

At about four o'clock the woman of thirty of no office came back through that yard with that page in her hand and stopped at that bench stone and said one thing out loud, in about nine people, and what she said was that a smaller hand is a smaller hand and that a page with a height in it is a page about pens, and that if anybody in this harbor wants to make those two names into a thing about two men then they should go and do it without this yard's page underneath them.

Nobody agreed with her and nobody argued with her. The man who keeps the leaf said that the page was going to carry the figures whatever anybody did with them, because a figure is a figure and it does not need a reason to stand on a page, and that what people did with figures was their own business and had been since before the frost.

---

At about four in the afternoon the man who keeps the leaf wrote the heights into the first column with the day and the hour against them and no name against them, and he wrote that one of the two smaller names is not the same height as the other by about a sixth of an inch, and that a sixth of an inch is a figure a stranger can put a rule to.

Nobody has said out loud what any of that is about, and the man who keeps the leaf has not written one word under it.

---

And at about half past five on that Friday the man who keeps the leaf took the shortfall out loud at that bench stone, in about four hundred and twenty words, to about nine people.

"**One hundred and one thousand two hundred and thirty-six pence short, being six hundred and thirty-two marks, nine shillings and eightpence. One line on that sheet since the twentieth of April and not one hand on it since. Ten thousand six hundred and forty pence has ever gone into it, being sixty-six marks, six shillings and eightpence. Four hundred and fifty pence is still on that board in the front street. That butt behind that market stood covered from eight until four with no charge on it.**

**A hundred and sixty-eight pence in at ten and a hundred and sixty-eight out at four and twenty-eight plus one hundred and sixty-eight less one hundred and sixty-eight is twenty-eight, and under that slate is twenty-eight pence, being two shillings and fourpence, and that is six hundred and twelve pence short of the wage of four marks, being three marks eleven shillings. No tin in that shed has been opened since the twenty-ninth of April past and nothing that happened this morning came out of it.**

**And this morning I learned something about a hand and I have not learned one single thing about a person. Two of those four names are written by the same hand as the two that are on the Morrow Line's own page and that is nothing, because a route has one hand. The other two are written by another hand and that hand is smaller and it comes down further at the end of a letter, and the two of those are not the same as each other by about a sixth of an inch.**

**Here is what I am not going to do with a sixth of an inch. I am not going to write it in a column called a hand, because this yard has refused six different pieces of paper in thirteen volumes that would each have kept a list of people, and a column with a height in it is a column that ends up with a name in it inside four months. What I have done is put a figure a stranger can check beside two names, and stop there.**

**And I am not going to stand here and tell nine people what I think a smaller hand means, because I do not think anything yet. I have a difference and I have a height. A difference and a height are two facts and they are not a reason and they are not the beginning of a reason I am entitled to.**"

Nobody was thanked for the arithmetic and nobody was thanked for a sixth of an inch, and that shed stood empty by about six, and that wall had three lines on it and a page beside them, and that page of days had four names against the Morrow Line and two of them had been measured and neither of the two measurements was about anybody.

---

**NOT A ROTA DAY AND NOT A WAGE TUESDAY AND NOT A WIPE, AND THAT BUTT BEHIND THAT MARKET WAS COVERED FROM EIGHT IN THE MORNING UNTIL FOUR IN THE AFTERNOON WITH NO CHARGE ON IT. AT ABOUT TWO IN THE AFTERNOON A MAN OF ABOUT THIRTY-TWO OF THAT HARBOR MEASURED THE HEIGHT OF THE FOUR NAMES THAT STAND AGAINST THE MORROW LINE ON THAT PAGE OF DAYS, AND TWO OF THEM STAND AT THE HEIGHT OF ONE HAND AND TWO OF THEM STAND AT THE HEIGHT OF ANOTHER, AND THAT HAND IS SMALLER AND COMES DOWN FURTHER AT THE END OF A LETTER, AND THE TWO OF THOSE ARE NOT THE SAME AS EACH OTHER BY ABOUT A SIXTH OF AN INCH, AND THE TWO AT THE FIRST HEIGHT ARE THE TWO THAT ARE ALSO ON THE MORROW LINE'S OWN PAGE. A WOMAN OF ABOUT THIRTY-NINE WHO MENDS NET SAID THAT THE SECOND HAND IS A HAND THAT WRITES WITH THE WRIST AND NOT THE FINGERS, AND THAT THERE IS ONE SUCH HAND IN THIS HARBOR, AND SHE IS NOT GOING TO SAY WHOSE IT IS AND NOBODY HAS ASKED HER. NOBODY HAS SAID OUT LOUD WHAT ANY OF THAT IS ABOUT. THE MONEY SHEET IS AT ONE HUNDRED AND ELEVEN THOUSAND EIGHT HUNDRED AND SEVENTY-SIX PENCE OUT, BEING SIX HUNDRED AND NINETY-NINE MARKS AND THREE SHILLINGS, AGAINST TEN THOUSAND SIX HUNDRED AND FORTY PENCE IN, BEING SIXTY-SIX MARKS, SIX SHILLINGS AND EIGHTPENCE, AND IT IS ONE HUNDRED AND ONE THOUSAND TWO HUNDRED AND THIRTY-SIX PENCE SHORT, BEING SIX HUNDRED AND THIRTY-TWO MARKS, NINE SHILLINGS AND EIGHTPENCE, AND IT CARRIES ONE LINE AND NO SECOND. NOTHING WAS READ OUT OF A GAP AND NO FIGURE ON THE SHEET OF THE FOURTEEN WENT UP OR DOWN BY ONE. UNDER THAT SLATE IS TWENTY-EIGHT PENCE, BEING TWO SHILLINGS AND FOURPENCE.**

*Second column, not checkable from a mole, entered on the said Friday at about six in the evening in that shed, and no name is against it: that at about two in the afternoon the reason a rule was used and not an opinion is that a rule can be put on the page of this yard by a stranger and will give the same answer, and that the whole standing of a keeper of a page in this harbor is that anybody can take it up and get the figure again; and that at about half past two in the afternoon the reason the two names at the first height are written down as nothing is that a route has one hand, so a second name in the same hand is not a second person but the same person coming back to a page twice, and that this yard is not going to make a finding out of a thing that ordinary people do four times a week; and that at about half past three in the afternoon the reason I have not written down which hand is the wrist hand is that I know, and that knowing is not the same as being entitled, and that a page on which the keeper has written that he knows which hand it is is a page where the next keeper has to be told what to look at, and that is how a page of findings becomes a page of instructions, and this harbor has refused enough of those; and that at about four in the afternoon the reason the sixth of an inch is in this column and not in this slip is that it is a figure a stranger can check with a rule and the rest of today is not; and that at about five in the afternoon the reason I have not written one word under any of it is that two facts stacked on top of each other look exactly like a reason to anybody reading them standing up, and that the only thing standing between this page and a story about somebody is a gap, and I am not going to close the gap in the same week I noticed it.*
