# Chapter 692

*Sunday the Twelfth Day of June, a Wipe and the Hundred and Tenth Time That the Wall Behind a Reeve's Bench Has Come Down Bare, Twenty-Seven Minutes to Take It Off the Wall and Fourteen Standing Bare and Twenty-Seven to Get It Back, Being Sixty-Eight the Whole of It From About Ten Past Ten to About Eighteen Minutes Past Eleven, a Minute Longer Each Way and a Minute Longer Bare Than the Sunday Before, Six Sundays of Those Figures Now and Not One Minute of Them Accounted For in This Harbor, the Man of About Thirty-Two Walking Down to the End Stall on His Own Two Feet at About Ten Past Three and Standing There About Four Minutes and Not Reading Anything and Nobody Asking Him Anything, Nothing Read Out of a Gap and No Figure Moved by One, the Money Sheet Carrying Two Lines and No Third and the Bag at Twenty-Eight Pence, Being Two Shillings and Fourpence, the First Column at Four Hundred and Twenty-Five and the Second at One Thousand Nine Hundred and Ninety-Nine*

Sunday the twelfth day of June, with that market shut for the morning and standing open from about two in the afternoon, a yard below that market with a shed standing in it, and about nine people in that shed at half past five that afternoon.

**A wipe, and the hundred and tenth time that wall behind a reeve's bench has come down, and no rota day and no wage Tuesday in it, and that butt behind the market covered from eight in the morning until four in the afternoon with no charge on it. Two men began at about ten past ten and that wall was bare by about twenty-five to eleven and it was back on its holders at about eighteen minutes past eleven, at the angle it has carried since the twelfth of January, and the thing above the frame is still not whole. Twenty-seven minutes off the wall, fourteen standing bare, twenty-seven to get it back, which is sixty-eight minutes the whole of it, and that is a minute longer each way and a minute longer bare than the Sunday before, and that is four Sundays now. At about ten past three in the afternoon the man of about thirty-two of that harbor walked down to the end stall on his own two feet and stood at it about four minutes and read nothing, because there is nothing new on it, and nobody in that market asked him anything and he asked nothing. Nothing went onto the money sheet and nothing came off it, and a hundred and sixty-eight pence came into that yard at about ten in the morning and a hundred and sixty-eight went out of it at about four in the afternoon, and under that slate at the close of the day is twenty-eight pence, being two shillings and fourpence, and no tin in that shed has been opened since the twenty-ninth of April past.**

**Nine counters in that harbor went a day further today and this yard moved none of them. That notice of the seventeenth of May past has come to its three hundred and ninetieth day. Three hundred and ninety-second days have gone by without anybody asking a bellwright of this harbor, and his chest stays shut. Two objects of nobody's up on that crown are into their seven hundred and thirtieth day. The bench under that pump has stood bare for the four hundred and ninety-sixth night. Two lights about a mile off that point have spent three hundred and fourteenth days with no figure against them. The six-line sheet at the back of that end stall is on its two hundred and sixty-ninth day. The paper of four lines in Fish Street has reached its two hundred and eighty-fourth day. A nine-line sheet with a seal on it in that front street is on its three hundred and first day. A man of about fifty-one has kept three hundred and thirty-second days of saying nothing to anybody about a page of six names. That third column stands empty and unappointed, a boy's hand of twenty-two is at the head of it, his surname is on no page, and he is off the water. That fourth column stands at one hundred and twenty-five, has taken no line, and is neither shut nor struck. Two of the five papers on that board at the head of that mole have never been opened by anybody here and nothing went up on that board this month. That basket in that boathouse holds a paper and nothing else and no hand has gone into it. About two sea miles off that mole head lie nine hulls this afternoon, nine were there on the twenty-first of January, and this harbor has not said a word about any of them easing. A man of about twenty-nine hears out of the left side of his head and it is one hundred and ninetieth days, one more than the day before, no figure is set against it, and he is not on the water.**

---

That wall came down bare for the hundred and tenth time and it went back up at about eighteen minutes past eleven and it did not go up whole.

Twenty-seven minutes to get it off the wall, fourteen standing with it bare, and twenty-seven to get it back, which is sixty-eight the whole of it from about ten past ten.

Six of those Sundays now. Twenty-two and nine and twenty-two on the eighth of May, twenty-three and ten and twenty-three on the fifteenth, twenty-four and eleven and twenty-four on the twenty-second, twenty-five and twelve and twenty-five on the twenty-ninth, twenty-six and thirteen and twenty-six on the fifth of June, and twenty-seven and fourteen and twenty-seven this morning.

About four people near enough saw it happen and went on with what they were doing, which is what they did on the five Sundays before it, and neither of the two men who does that work has said one word in six Sundays and this yard stopped asking after the second and is going to go on not asking.

---

At about half past eleven the man of about thirty-one of the second lay came past the bottom of that lane and stopped for the length of a cigarette.

He said that it is a minute more each way than it was in May, and that he has watched it four times and has said nothing four times, and that he is not saying anything this morning either, and that he would like it noticed that he had noticed.

The man who keeps the leaf wrote the hour against that and wrote no name against it and did not say that he was right, because a man who has noticed something is not a man who has been asked about it.

---

At about two in the afternoon the boards at the back of that market filled, and the keeper went up and stood at the end stall with about four people and looked at what was on it.

There are two sheets on that stall and neither of them is a page of routes and neither of them is the page of hands. One of them is the sheet of fourteen gaps and not one figure on it moved by one today and the gap that is the biggest one on that sheet is three thousand six hundred pence bigger than it was a week ago.

The page of hands is not on that stall this month and has not been on it since the first of May and stands at fifteen, and the four names that came off it in May came off because somebody with a right to take them off said so, and the fall is written on the face of that page with the day and the hour against it and the old figures are still legible underneath.

---

At about ten past three in the afternoon the man of about thirty-two of that harbor came down that lane and onto those boards and stood at the end stall for about four minutes.

He got there on his own two feet and he did not stop on the way, and about eleven people were on those boards of whom about four cannot read a notice and none of them said a word to him.

He read nothing because there is nothing new on that stall. He put his hand flat on the flat of the corner of it for the length of a minute, which is a thing he does, and then he went back down the lane by the rail side of the steps and did not go by the middle.

Nobody in that market asked him what he thought and he did not say, and the man who keeps the leaf wrote the four minutes down with the hour against them and wrote no name against them and wrote no figure against them either.

---

At about four in the afternoon the man who keeps the leaf wrote the Sunday line on the page of that week and it says four things, being the three numbers of that wall with the hour against them, that a man who has watched it four times and has said nothing four times said this morning that he had noticed it, that the biggest gap on the sheet of fourteen is three thousand six hundred pence bigger than it was a week ago, and that a man stood at the end stall for about four minutes and read nothing.

He wrote the page of hands at fifteen against it and did not add it up and did not touch it.

Nobody thanked anybody for that line and nobody argued with it.

---

And at about half past five on that Sunday the man who keeps the leaf took the shortfall out loud at that bench stone, in about six hundred and twelve words, to about nine people, and about four of those nine had been standing on those boards at about two in the afternoon.

"**Twenty-seven off that wall this morning, fourteen standing bare, twenty-seven back, from about ten past ten to about eighteen minutes past eleven, and that is sixty-eight the whole of it and that is the hundred and tenth time, and it is a minute longer each way than it was in May, and there is no figure of money in this sentence and there is not going to be one. A man of about thirty-one of the second lay stopped at the bottom of that lane this morning and told me he had noticed, and that he is not saying anything about it, and I have written down that he noticed and I have not written down that he is right, because noticing a thing is not being asked about it and this yard has been careful about that difference for six weeks.**

**The sheet stands at one hundred and twelve thousand seven hundred and sixteen pence out, being seven hundred and four marks, six shillings and fourpence, against ten thousand six hundred and forty pence in, being sixty-six marks, six shillings and eightpence, and that is one hundred and two thousand and seventy-six pence short, being six hundred and thirty-seven marks and thirteen shillings. Two lines on it and no third of them. Four hundred and fifty pence is still on that board in the front street.**

**A hundred and sixty-eight pence in at about ten and a hundred and sixty-eight out at about four, and twenty-eight plus one hundred and sixty-eight less one hundred and sixty-eight is twenty-eight, and under that slate is twenty-eight pence, being two shillings and fourpence, and that is six hundred and twelve pence short of the wage of four marks, being three marks eleven shillings. No tin in that shed has been opened since the twenty-ninth of April past and this yard is now one hundred and five days without one.**

**The stall this afternoon. The sheet of fourteen gaps is on it and not one figure on that sheet moved by one today, and the biggest gap on it is three thousand six hundred pence bigger than it was a week ago and that is a gap getting bigger and not a gap getting closed, and this yard does not have a way of making that sentence sound better than it sounds. The page of hands is not on that stall and has not been since the first of May and stands at fifteen, and the four names that came off it in May came off because somebody with a right to take them off said so, and the day and the hour are against that fall and the old figures can still be read underneath.**

**And one man stood at that stall at about ten past three for about four minutes and read nothing, because there is nothing new on it, and put his hand flat on the corner of it for a minute, which is a thing he does, and went back down that lane by the rail and not by the middle. Nobody asked him and he said nothing and I have written the four minutes down and no name against them.**

**And the last of it. That coil that was on a nail in that shed on Friday is not on that nail today and is not on that nail tomorrow and this yard is not going to keep saying where it went. The page of that week is two lines in and the man of about twenty-nine is on his one hundred and ninetieth day and is not on the water and nothing that happened on Wednesday has touched him.**"

Nobody was thanked for the arithmetic and nobody was thanked for sixty-eight minutes, and that shed stood empty by about six with its door standing open on a slip nobody was on, and that page of that week had two lines on it, and that wooden tally slate was still lying flat on that bench stone with nothing on it.

**THE WALL BEHIND A REEVE'S BENCH CAME DOWN BARE AT ABOUT TEN PAST TEN ON THAT SUNDAY AND STOOD BARE UNTIL IT WENT BACK ON ITS HOLDERS AT ABOUT FIFTEEN MINUTES PAST ELEVEN AT THE ANGLE IT HAS CARRIED SINCE THE TWELFTH OF JANUARY, AND IT DID NOT GO UP WHOLE, IT HAVING TAKEN TWENTY-SEVEN MINUTES OFF THE WALL, FOURTEEN STANDING BARE AND TWENTY-SEVEN TO GET IT BACK, BEING SIXTY-EIGHT MINUTES THE WHOLE OF IT FROM ABOUT TEN PAST TEN, AGAINST TWENTY-SIX AND THIRTEEN AND TWENTY-SIX ON THE SUNDAY BEFORE, AND NOBODY HAS SAID WHY IT IS GETTING SLOWER AND THIS YARD HAS STOPPED ASKING THE TWO MEN WHO DO IT. A MAN OF ABOUT THIRTY-ONE OF THE SECOND LAY STOPPED AT THE BOTTOM OF THAT LANE AT ABOUT HALF PAST ELEVEN AND SAID THAT HE HAD NOTICED IT AND WAS NOT SAYING ANYTHING ABOUT IT, AND THAT WAS WRITTEN DOWN WITH THE HOUR AGAINST IT AND NO NAME. THE PAGE OF HANDS STANDS AT FIFTEEN AND WAS NOT ADDED UP AND WAS NOT TOUCHED. THE MONEY SHEET STANDS AT ONE HUNDRED AND TWELVE THOUSAND SEVEN HUNDRED AND SIXTEEN PENCE OUT, BEING SEVEN HUNDRED AND FOUR MARKS, SIX SHILLINGS AND FOURPENCE, WITH TEN THOUSAND SIX HUNDRED AND FORTY PENCE IN, BEING SIXTY-SIX MARKS, SIX SHILLINGS AND EIGHTPENCE, AND IT IS ONE HUNDRED AND TWO THOUSAND AND SEVENTY-SIX PENCE SHORT, BEING SIX HUNDRED AND THIRTY-SEVEN MARKS AND THIRTEEN SHILLINGS, AND IT CARRIES TWO LINES AND NO THIRD. NOTHING WAS READ OUT OF A GAP AND NO FIGURE ON THE SHEET OF THE FOURTEEN WENT UP OR DOWN BY ONE. UNDER THAT SLATE IS TWENTY-EIGHT PENCE, BEING TWO SHILLINGS AND FOURPENCE.**

*Second column, not checkable from a mole, entered on the said Sunday at about six in the evening in that shed, and no name is against it: that at about half past eleven in the morning the reason six Sundays of asking a man about a wall is written as two Sundays of asking is that this yard asked twice and got nothing both times, and that a seventh question would be a question about a person rather than about a wall, and that this harbor keeps six refusals of lists of people behind it and would rather have a wall nobody will explain than a man everybody can explain; and that at about two in the afternoon the reason the three thousand six hundred pence is said out loud on a Sunday when it is a worse figure than it was a week ago is that a gap which is closing can be described and a gap which is not can only be admitted, and that a slip which said nothing about it would be saying something about it, and the third thing this yard has learned this month is that a page which prints its own bad figures is the only page a stranger can use; and that at about ten past three in the afternoon the reason four minutes of a man standing at a stall is written down with the hour and no name and no figure is that he did not read anything and nobody asked him anything and the whole of it is that nothing happened, and that this harbor has spent forty-one days finding out that nothing happening in front of four people is a thing that happens and not an absence of a thing, and that writing it with a number of minutes against it is the only way to keep it from being a report about a man; and that at about half past five in the afternoon the reason that slip ends on a coil and not on the man of about twenty-nine is that the coil is a thing that moved in this yard and the man of about twenty-nine is a thing that did not, and a slip that ends on a thing that did not move teaches about nine people nothing about this coast and teaches them a lie about this man.
