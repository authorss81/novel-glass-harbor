# Chapter 651

*Monday the Second Day of May, a Rota Day and the Hundred and Forty-Sixth Charge on That Cistern, Eleven Trips by Hand and Walked on by None of It, One Line of Eleven Words Going Up on the Wall of That Shed at About Two in the Afternoon in About Nine People and Read Out Once and Not Twice Because It Is One Line and It Is Not This Yard's, a Man of About Forty-One of the Third Lay Four Days Down That Coast Road in the Morning With a Burn on the Flat of His Right Hand, Nothing Read Out of a Gap and No Figure Moved by One, the Money Sheet Untouched at One Hundred and Eleven Thousand Eight Hundred and Seventy-Six Pence Out and Ten Thousand Six Hundred and Forty Pence In and One Hundred and One Thousand Two Hundred and Thirty-Six Pence Short, the Bag at Twenty-Eight Pence, Being Two Shillings and Fourpence, the First Column at Three Hundred and Eighty-Four and the Second at One Thousand Seven Hundred and Ninety-Four*

Monday the second day of May, with a market that trades on a Monday above it, a yard below that market with a shed standing in it, and about nine people in that shed at half past five that afternoon.

**A rota day and not a wage Tuesday and not a wipe, and that cistern at the back of that market charged in eleven trips by hand between about eight in the morning and about eleven, and walked on by none of it, and the hundred and forty-sixth time that cistern has been charged since the second of August of the year before last, and the covers left lying on it from about eleven until about four in the afternoon. A sheet of days came into that harbor in the night and lay under a lump of stone at the head of the boards at the back of that market, and the boards were swept at about seven in the morning and the stone came away and left the top of it wet and the bottom of it dry, and that is the whole of how anybody in that harbor knows when a thing arrives. Nothing went onto the money sheet and nothing came off it, and the last hand to touch that sheet was on the twentieth of that same April and has not touched it since, and a hundred and sixty-eight pence came into that yard at about ten in the morning and the same figure went out of it at about four in the afternoon. A man came down that coast road this morning who is about forty-one and is of the third lay of a place four days down that coast where the cart takes four days each way, and he has a burn on the flat of his right hand about the width of a two-inch rope and it is not fresh, and he read one line out loud in about nine people at the back of that shed at about two in the afternoon and then he put that line on the wall himself with four tacks out of his own pocket, and nobody in this yard wrote one word of it and nobody in that yard is entitled to.**

**Nine things in this harbor can be had off a wall by anybody with the patience to want them, and not one of the nine was found by looking for it. That notice of the seventeenth of May past has now stood three hundred and forty-nine days. A bellwright of this harbor has not been asked for three hundred and fifty-one days, and his chest stays shut. Two objects of nobody's on that crown stand six hundred and eighty-nine days. The bench under that pump has stood bare for the four hundred and fifty-fifth night. Two lights at about a mile off that point have been two hundred and seventy-three days without a figure against them. A six-line sheet at the back of that end stall is on its two hundred and twenty-eighth day. The paper of four lines in Fish Street is on its two hundred and forty-third. A nine-line sheet with a seal on it in that front street is on its two hundred and sixtieth day. A man of about fifty-one has said nothing to anybody about a page of six names for two hundred and ninety-one days. Five papers are on that board at the head of that mole and two of the five have never been opened by anybody in this harbor. Nine hulls lie about two sea miles off the head of that mole this afternoon and they were nine there on the twenty-first of January, and not one word about any of them easing has been said in this harbor. A man of about twenty-nine can hear out of the left side of his head; it is one hundred and forty-nine days, no figure stands against it, and he is not on the water.**

---

That cistern was charged in eleven trips by hand between about eight in the morning and about eleven, and four men this yard does not know did the carrying and none of them was paid and none of them was asked what the water was for, and every one of the eleven trips went up the same steps on the inside of that wall and every one of them came down the same steps, and the covers were lying on it from about eleven until about four in the afternoon.

Nobody walked on that water. That is not a rule anybody wrote down. It is what has happened every time that cistern has been charged since the autumn, and the man who keeps the leaf has stopped expecting it to change and writes the same sentence on the same page each time.

---

At about two in the afternoon the man of about forty-one put his bag down on the bench stone and took a folded paper out of it and gave it to the woman of thirty of no office and did not give it to the man of about thirty-two, and she read it and gave it back to him and he put it on the wall himself.

The line is this.

**We will not carry an echo this route cannot answer for.**

It is eleven words and it took him a while to say the last of them, and there is nothing under it and nothing above it and no seal at the foot of it and no name. He said out loud that it belongs to the Saltmarch Weatherway and that he is of the third lay there and that the four days of that coast road took him two nights in a cart because the boat was not going on the Monday.

Nobody read it out twice. It is one line and it is his and this yard is not the body that decides what goes on that wall, and about four people in that nine heard the whole of it and the rest of that market was not told. Nobody agreed with him and nobody argued with him and nobody thanked him and nobody gave him a cup of anything, and the man of about thirty-two asked him one question and got one answer and did not ask it again.

The question was who answers for an echo that cannot be answered for. The answer was the route. And then the man of about thirty-two asked whether the route means the people of that route, and the man of about forty-one said that the route means the route, and would not break it down any further, and the yard did not press him on it.

---

At about half past three in the afternoon the man who keeps the leaf asked, in the same nine people and in thirty-nine words, whether this party has a line of its own.

"**Does this party have a line of its own, and if it has one, who wrote it, and if nobody wrote it, is that because we have not got one or because we have not got round to it.**"

Nobody answered him. A man of about thirty-one of the second lay looked at the wall and then at the floor. A woman of about forty-four with a chandler's counter four hundred yards up that lane said she would not put her name to anything before she knew what the other two meant, which is not a no and was not taken as one. The man of about forty-one packed his bag and said that a yard that will not say what it will not carry is at least being honest about the size of itself, and went up to the boards, and nobody thanked him for that either.

---

And at about half past five on that Monday the man who keeps the leaf took the shortfall out loud at that bench stone, in about two hundred and eighty-one words, to about nine people, and about five of the nine were looking at the wall behind him and not at him.

"**One hundred and one thousand two hundred and thirty-six pence short, being six hundred and thirty-two marks, nine shillings and eightpence. That sheet has one line on it and the line is a bill for a storm that was not a weather, and it has been one line since the twentieth of April, and not one hand has touched it since. Ten thousand six hundred and forty pence has ever gone into it, being sixty-six marks, six shillings and eightpence, and there is no second line and there is not going to be one. Four hundred and fifty pence is still the figure on that board in the front street and it did not move this morning and nobody in this market has asked for it to.**

**A cistern was charged in eleven trips by hand this morning between about eight and about eleven and nobody walked on the water after it, and that is the hundred and forty-sixth time, and the covers lay on it from about eleven until about four, and that is what a rota day is. A hundred and sixty-eight pence came in at ten and a hundred and sixty-eight went out at four, and twenty-eight plus one hundred and sixty-eight less one hundred and sixty-eight is twenty-eight, and under that slate is twenty-eight pence, being two shillings and fourpence, and the wage of four marks is six hundred and forty pence, so that what is under that slate is six hundred and twelve pence short of it, being three marks eleven shillings. No tin in that shed has been opened since the twenty-ninth of April past and nothing that happened this afternoon came out of it.**"

**And one line went up on that wall at about two o'clock and it is eleven words and it belongs to the only route anybody on this coast is holding at the moment, and it is on that wall in another man's hand with four tacks out of his own pocket, and nobody in this yard wrote one word of it and nobody in this yard is entitled to take one word of it down. It says they will not carry an echo they cannot answer for. That is a limit and not a reason, and a limit with no reason under it is worth more than a reason with no limit on it, because anybody can check a limit against what a route does tomorrow and there is nobody in this harbor who can check a reason.**

**And then I asked, in nine people, at about half past three this afternoon, whether this party has a line of its own, and nobody in that yard answered me, and there is a space on that wall beside that line about two hands wide and nothing in it, and I am not going to fill it this week, and I am not going to fill it with a sentence either. A yard that writes down what it will not carry in the same week that somebody else does it is copying. And a yard that waits to find out what it will not carry after the Crown has told it what it will not do is being told.**

Nobody was thanked for the arithmetic and nobody was thanked for eleven words on a wall, and that shed stood empty by about six with its door standing open on a slip nobody was on, and that wall had one line on it and a space beside it, and the wooden tally slate was still lying flat on the bench stone where it had been put down on the first of May and nobody had picked it up and nothing had been written on it.

---

**THAT CISTERN WAS CHARGED IN ELEVEN TRIPS BY HAND BETWEEN ABOUT EIGHT IN THE MORNING AND ABOUT ELEVEN AND WALKED ON BY NONE OF IT AND IT IS THE HUNDRED AND FORTY-SIXTH CHARGE, THE COVERS LAYING ON IT FROM ABOUT ELEVEN UNTIL ABOUT FOUR IN THE AFTERNOON, AND A SHEET OF DAYS CAME INTO THAT HARBOR IN THE NIGHT AND LAY UNDER A STONE AT THE HEAD OF THOSE BOARDS WITH THE TOP OF IT WET AND THE BOTTOM OF IT DRY. A LINE OF ELEVEN WORDS WENT UP ON THE WALL OF THAT SHED AT ABOUT TWO IN THE AFTERNOON IN ABOUT NINE PEOPLE, IT WAS READ OUT ONCE AND NOT TWICE BECAUSE IT IS ONE LINE AND IT IS NOT THIS YARD'S, AND IT SAYS THAT THE SALT MARCH WEATHERWAY WILL NOT CARRY AN ECHO THAT ROUTE CANNOT ANSWER FOR, AND NOBODY WROTE ONE WORD OF IT HERE AND NOBODY IN THIS HARBOR IS ENTITLED TO TAKE ONE WORD OF IT DOWN. A SPACE ABOUT TWO HANDS WIDE STANDS BESIDE IT AND NOTHING IS IN IT. THE MONEY SHEET IS AT ONE HUNDRED AND ELEVEN THOUSAND EIGHT HUNDRED AND SEVENTY-SIX PENCE OUT, BEING SIX HUNDRED AND NINETY-NINE MARKS AND THREE SHILLINGS, AGAINST TEN THOUSAND SIX HUNDRED AND FORTY PENCE IN, BEING SIXTY-SIX MARKS, SIX SHILLINGS AND EIGHTPENCE, AND IT IS ONE HUNDRED AND ONE THOUSAND TWO HUNDRED AND THIRTY-SIX PENCE SHORT, BEING SIX HUNDRED AND THIRTY-TWO MARKS, NINE SHILLINGS AND EIGHTPENCE, AND IT CARRIES ONE LINE AND NO SECOND. NOTHING WAS READ OUT OF A GAP AND NO FIGURE ON THE SHEET OF THE FOURTEEN WENT UP OR DOWN BY ONE. UNDER THAT SLATE IS TWENTY-EIGHT PENCE, BEING TWO SHILLINGS AND FOURPENCE.**

*First column, entered on Monday the second day of May at about six in the evening in that shed: that the said day was a rota day and was not a wage Tuesday and was not a wipe, and that the cistern at the back of that market was charged in eleven trips by hand between about eight in the morning and about eleven and was walked on by none of it, and that it is the hundred and forty-sixth charge and that the covers lay on it from about eleven until about four in the afternoon; and that a sheet of days came into that harbor in the night and lay under a lump of stone at the head of the boards at the back of that market and that the boards were swept at about seven in the morning and the stone came away and left the top of that sheet wet and the bottom of it dry; and that at about two in the afternoon a man of about forty-one of the third lay of a place four days down that coast, who came down that coast road in the morning and took two nights in a cart because the boat was not going on the Monday, stood in about nine people at the back of that shed and read out one line of eleven words and then put that line on the wall of that shed himself with four tacks out of his own pocket, and that the line says that the Saltmarch Weatherway will not carry an echo that route cannot answer for, and that it belongs to that route and not to this yard, and that no word of it was written in this yard and nobody in this yard is entitled to take one word of it down; and that it was read out once and not twice because it is one line and it is his and this yard is not the body that decides what goes on that wall, and that about four people in that nine heard the whole of it and the rest of that market was not told; and that at about half past three in the afternoon the man who keeps the leaf asked, in the same nine people and in about thirty-nine words, whether this party has a line of its own, and that nobody in that yard answered him, and that a woman of about forty-four with a chandler's counter four hundred yards up that lane said she would not put her name to anything before she knew what the other two meant, which is not a no and was not taken as one; and that the man of about forty-one said out loud that a yard that will not say what it will not carry is at least being honest about the size of itself, and that nobody thanked him for that; and that a space about two hands wide stands on that wall beside that line and nothing is in it and this yard did not fill it this week and will not fill it with a sentence; and that the wooden tally slate still lies flat on the bench stone where it was put down on Sunday the first of May and nobody has picked it up and nothing has been written on it; and that nothing went onto that money sheet and nothing came off it, and that the last hand to touch it was on the twentieth of April past; and that a hundred and sixty-eight pence came into that yard at about ten in the morning and a hundred and sixty-eight went out at about four in the afternoon, and that twenty-eight plus one hundred and sixty-eight less one hundred and sixty-eight is twenty-eight, and that under that slate is twenty-eight pence, being two shillings and fourpence; and that the first column takes one entry a day whatever else that day prints, and that three hundred and eighty-three were on that leaf before the first of this run, and that one has gone in on this day, and that three hundred and eighty-three and one is three hundred and eighty-four at the close of it, and that the second column takes five a day, and that one thousand seven hundred and eighty-nine and five is one thousand seven hundred and ninety-four at the close of it.*

*Second column, not checkable from a mole, entered on the said Monday at about six in the evening in that shed, and no name is against it: that at about seven in the morning the reason I have written down that the top of that sheet was wet and the bottom of it was dry is that a thing which arrived some time in the night is a thing nobody in that harbor had to be caught doing, and a paper is a very different thing to catch a man with, and the wet and the dry is the only part of the arrival anybody can check in a week; and that at about two in the afternoon the reason I did not read that line out twice is that I did not write it and this yard is not the body that decides what goes on that wall, and a line read twice by a yard that did not write it is a line that has started to belong to the yard, and there is one line on that wall now and there ought to be one line on that wall now; and that at about half past three in the afternoon the reason I asked whether this party has a line of its own, in nine people, on our own wall, is that a yard that hangs nine other people's limits and has nothing of its own is a passage and not a party, and that I asked it out loud in nine people because a yard that decides that question in a back room has decided it by not answering; and that at about four in the afternoon the reason I have written nothing on the space beside that line is that I do not know what this party will not carry, and that I have known that for some months, and that writing down a limit in order to have one is the same thing as the schedule in the sense that matters, which is that it makes a page smooth where a gap was, and that at about five in the afternoon the reason I said the thing about a limit with no reason under it is that a reason cannot be checked against anything and a limit can be checked against tomorrow, and that nobody in this harbor is going to say one word about that back to me and nobody is going to argue with it either; and that at about half past five in the afternoon the reason this slip opens on that cistern and not on the line on that wall is that a cistern is a thing a stranger can watch from a mole and a line on a wall is a thing a stranger has to walk in to read, and this yard has spent sixty days finding out what a page is for and it keeps arriving at the same answer, which is that a page is for the people at the end of the bench.*
