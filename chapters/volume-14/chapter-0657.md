# Chapter 657

*Sunday the Eighth Day of May, a Wipe, the Hundred and Fifth Time, the Wall Behind a Reeve's Bench Down Bare at About Half Past Ten in the Morning and Up Again at About Eleven at the Angle It Has Carried Since the Twelfth of January and Unrepaired and Not Whole, Twenty-Two Minutes to Get It Off the Wall and Nine Standing Bare and Twenty-Two to Get It Back, Being Fifty-Three the Whole of It and a Minute Longer Each Way and a Minute Longer Bare Than the First of This Month and Nobody Having Said Why, a Bell Buoy of About Two Tons Hung at About Three in the Afternoon by About Six Men of the Third Lay of That Route on That Route's Own Moorings With This Yard Sending a Hand and Not Touching the Shackle, the Second Line Going Up on That Wall at About Five in the Afternoon in About Nine People and It Being Nine Words and Read Out Once and Not Twice Because It Is One Line and It Is Theirs, Nothing Read Out of a Gap and No Figure Moved by One, the Bag at Twenty-Eight Pence, Being Two Shillings and Fourpence, the First Column at Three Hundred and Ninety and the Second at One Thousand Eight Hundred and Twenty-Four*

Sunday the eighth day of May, with that market shut for the morning and open for the afternoon, a yard below that market with a shed standing in it, and about nine people in that shed at half past five that afternoon.

**A wipe, and the hundred and fifth time that wall behind a reeve's bench has come down, and no rota day and no wage Tuesday in it, and that butt covered from nine until four with no charge on it. Two men began to take that wall down at about ten past ten and it was bare by about half past ten and it was back on its holders at about eleven, at the angle it has carried since the twelfth of January, and the thing above the frame is still not whole. Twenty-two minutes off the wall, nine minutes standing bare, twenty-two minutes to get it back, which is fifty-three minutes the whole of it, and that is a minute longer each way and a minute longer bare than the first of this month, and nobody in this harbor has said why it is getting slower and this yard has stopped asking the two men who do it. At about three in the afternoon a bell buoy of about two tons was hung by about six men of the third lay of that route on that route's own moorings, and this yard sent a hand and did not touch the shackle. At about five in the afternoon the second line went up on that wall in about nine people and it is the Lowfen Reed's and it is nine words and it was read out once and not twice because it is one line and it is theirs. Nothing went onto the money sheet and nothing came off it, and a hundred and sixty-eight pence came into that yard at about ten in the morning and a hundred and sixty-eight went out of it at about four in the afternoon, and under that slate at the close of the day is twenty-eight pence, being two shillings and fourpence, and the reason the figure in chalk keeps running on a Sunday is that a berth fund pays a day for a day and does not care what a sea is under.**

**Nine things in this harbor can be had off a wall by anybody with the patience to want them, and not one of the nine was found by looking for it. That notice of the seventeenth of May past has now stood three hundred and fifty-five days. A bellwright of this harbor has not been asked for three hundred and fifty-seven days, and his chest stays shut. Two objects of nobody's on that crown stand six hundred and ninety-five days. The bench under that pump has stood bare for the four hundred and sixty-first night. Two lights at about a mile off that point have been two hundred and seventy-nine days without a figure against them. A six-line sheet at the back of that end stall is on its two hundred and thirty-fourth day. The paper of four lines in Fish Street is on its two hundred and forty-ninth. A nine-line sheet with a seal on it in that front street is on its two hundred and sixty-sixth day. A man of about fifty-one has said nothing to anybody about a page of six names for two hundred and ninety-seven days. Five papers are on that board at the head of that mole and two of the five have never been opened by anybody in this harbor and nothing has been nailed on that board this month. In that boathouse a basket stands with a paper in it and nothing else in it. About two sea miles off the head of that mole lie nine hulls this afternoon and they were nine there on the twenty-first of January, and nobody in this harbor has said that any of them has eased. A man of about twenty-nine can hear out of the left side of his head and it is one hundred and fifty-five days, one more than yesterday, and no figure is set against it and he is not on the water.**

---

Two men began to take that wall down off its holders at about ten past ten and it was bare by about half past ten, for the hundred and fifth time, and about four people near enough to see it happen saw it happen and did not stop what they were doing.

It went back on at about eleven at the angle it has been at since the twelfth of January and it did not go up whole.

Twenty-two minutes to get it off the wall, nine standing with it bare, and twenty-two to get it back, being fifty-three minutes the whole of it from about ten past ten to about eleven, and that is a minute longer each way and a minute longer bare than the first of this month, and neither of the two men who do that work has said one word about it and this yard has stopped asking them.

---

At about three in the afternoon a bell buoy of about two tons came alongside that mole with six men of the third lay of that route in her and a man of about forty of that route's third lay standing on her foredeck with his hand on the ring.

They hung it themselves. They made up their own shackle and they took their own turns on it and they used their own grease, and this yard sent a hand and that hand carried a coil of line and did not touch the shackle and did not touch the ring and stood on that deck for about an hour and a half and said almost nothing.

The man of about forty-one of the third lay of that route, who came down that coast road on Monday morning and put the line on that wall himself with four tacks out of his own pocket, was on that deck and he said not one word about Monday, and about four people near enough to see it heard him say that a fouled bell answers wrong, in the voice of a man who has said it before and does not need to say it again.

Nobody thanked those six men. They did not stop for that, and they were not asked to.

---

At about five in the afternoon the second line went up on that wall, in about nine people, on four tacks, in different words from the first.

**We carry only what we are asked to carry.**

It is nine words. It is the Lowfen Reed's, and it came down that coast road with a boat that goes in a day and a night where the cart takes four days each way, and it is in a different hand from the line of eleven words and a different length and there is nothing under it and nothing above it.

It was read out once. It was not read out twice, and that was not an oversight. About four people in that nine heard it and the rest of that market was not told, and there are people in this harbor who cannot read a column and were not told and were not asked.

---

And at about half past five on that Sunday the man who keeps the leaf took the shortfall out loud at that bench stone, in about two hundred and seventy-one words, to about nine people.

"**One hundred and one thousand two hundred and thirty-six pence short, being six hundred and thirty-two marks, nine shillings and eightpence. One line on that sheet since the twentieth of April and nothing came off it today and nothing went on it, and a hundred and sixty-eight pence came in at ten and a hundred and sixty-eight went out at four, and twenty-eight plus one hundred and sixty-eight less one hundred and sixty-eight is twenty-eight, and under that slate is twenty-eight pence, being two shillings and fourpence, and that is six hundred and twelve pence short of the wage of four marks, being three marks eleven shillings. No tin in that shed has been opened since the twenty-ninth of April past. And I am writing out on a Sunday for the same reason I have written it out on every Sunday since the frost, which is that a berth fund pays a day for a day and does not care what a wall behind a reeve's bench is doing, and if I stopped writing it down on a Sunday then the figure would still be there and I would not know what it was.**

**Fifty-three minutes the whole of it this morning. Twenty-two off the wall, nine standing bare, twenty-two back, from about ten past ten to about eleven, and that is the hundred and fifth time and I am going to say the minutes and not the number, the same as last Sunday and the Sunday before. Nobody has said why it is getting slower. I have not asked either man who does it and I am not going to.**"

**And two lines went up on that wall this week and they are not the same length and they are not in the same hand and one of them is eleven words and one of them is nine, and I did not write either of them and I am not entitled to take either of them down, and this yard has now got a wall that is made entirely out of other people's sentences and that is not a thing this yard did and is not a thing this yard can put a figure on.**

**Here is what I have been turning over since about three o'clock this afternoon and I will say it once. Six men from a route four days down that coast hung a bell buoy on their own moorings this afternoon while a wall came down and went up behind a reeve's bench, and neither of those two things has any connection with the other one, and I have written both of them in the same place in this leaf and that is correct, and I want it written down that it is correct and not convenient. A page that only keeps the things that go with each other is a page of arguments. This one is going to have fifty of these days on it and most of them are going to be like today, and the whole of what is on it will be a coast doing about nine separate things on the same afternoon.**

Nobody was thanked for the arithmetic and nobody was thanked for fifty-three minutes or for nine words on a wall, and that shed stood empty by about six with its door standing open on a slip nobody was on, and that wall stood at the angle it has carried since the twelfth of January and did not go up whole.

---

**THAT WALL BEHIND A REEVE'S BENCH CAME DOWN BARE AT ABOUT HALF PAST TEN ON THAT SUNDAY AND WENT UP AGAIN AT ABOUT ELEVEN AT THE ANGLE IT HAS BEEN AT SINCE THE TWELFTH OF JANUARY AND DID NOT GO UP WHOLE, AND IT TOOK TWENTY-TWO MINUTES OFF THE WALL, NINE STANDING BARE AND TWENTY-TWO TO GET IT BACK, BEING FIFTY-THREE MINUTES THE WHOLE OF IT, AGAINST TWENTY-ONE AND EIGHT AND TWENTY-ONE ON THE FIRST OF THIS MONTH, AND NOBODY HAS SAID WHY. A BELL BUOY OF ABOUT TWO TONS WAS HUNG AT ABOUT THREE IN THE AFTERNOON BY ABOUT SIX MEN OF THE THIRD LAY OF THAT ROUTE ON THAT ROUTE'S OWN MOORINGS, AND THIS YARD SENT A HAND AND DID NOT TOUCH THE SHACKLE. THE SECOND LINE WENT UP ON THAT WALL AT ABOUT FIVE IN THE AFTERNOON IN ABOUT NINE PEOPLE AND IS NINE WORDS AND SAYS THAT THEY CARRY ONLY WHAT THEY ARE ASKED TO CARRY, AND IT WAS READ OUT ONCE AND NOT TWICE BECAUSE IT IS ONE LINE AND IT IS THEIRS. THE MONEY SHEET IS AT ONE HUNDRED AND ELEVEN THOUSAND EIGHT HUNDRED AND SEVENTY-SIX PENCE OUT AGAINST TEN THOUSAND SIX HUNDRED AND FORTY PENCE IN AND IS ONE HUNDRED AND ONE THOUSAND TWO HUNDRED AND THIRTY-SIX PENCE SHORT. NOTHING WAS READ OUT OF A GAP AND NO FIGURE ON THE SHEET OF THE FOURTEEN WENT UP OR DOWN BY ONE. UNDER THAT SLATE IS TWENTY-EIGHT PENCE, BEING TWO SHILLINGS AND FOURPENCE.**

*First column, entered on Sunday the eighth day of May at about six in the evening in that shed: that the said day was a wipe and was the hundred and fifth time that wall behind a reeve's bench has come down, and that it was not a rota day and was not a wage Tuesday, and that no charge was placed on that cistern and that the covers lay on it from nine in the morning until four in the afternoon; and that two men began to take that wall off its holders at about ten past ten in the morning and that it was bare by about half past ten and that it was back on the holders at about eleven at the angle it has carried since the twelfth of January and that it did not go up whole; and that it took twenty-two minutes to get off the wall, nine standing bare and twenty-two to get it back, which is fifty-three minutes the whole of it from about ten past ten to about eleven, and that that is a minute longer each way and a minute longer bare than the first of this month, and that nobody in this harbor has said why it is getting slower and that this yard has stopped asking the two men who do it; and that at about three in the afternoon a bell buoy of about two tons came alongside that mole with six men of the third lay of that route in her, and that a man of about forty of that route's third lay stood on her foredeck with his hand on the ring; and that they hung it themselves and made up their own shackle and took their own turns on it and used their own grease, and that this yard sent a hand and that hand carried a coil of line and did not touch the shackle and did not touch the ring and stood on that deck for about an hour and a half and said almost nothing; and that at about five in the afternoon the second line went up on that wall in about nine people on four tacks, being that the Lowfen Reed carry only what they are asked to carry, being nine words, in a different hand from the line of eleven words and a different length and with nothing under it and nothing above it, and that it came down that coast road in a boat that goes in a day and a night where the cart takes four days each way; and that it was read out once and not twice and that the not reading it twice was not an oversight, and that about four people in that nine heard it and the rest of that market was not told and there are people in this harbor who cannot read a column and were not told and were not asked; and that the wooden tally slate still lies flat on that bench stone where it was put down on Sunday the first of May and nothing has been written on it; and that nothing went onto that money sheet and nothing came off it, and that a hundred and sixty-eight pence came into that yard at about ten in the morning and a hundred and sixty-eight went out at about four in the afternoon, and that twenty-eight plus one hundred and sixty-eight less one hundred and sixty-eight is twenty-eight, and that the first column takes one entry a day and three hundred and eighty-three and one is three hundred and ninety at the close of it, and that the second takes five a day and one thousand seven hundred and eighty-nine and five is one thousand eight hundred and twenty-four.*

*Second column, not checkable from a mole, entered on the said Sunday at about six in the evening in that shed, and no name is against it: that at about ten past ten in the morning the reason I took the twenty-two minutes off that wall down this morning with the fifth instrument in this yard is that the minutes are the only figure on this page a stranger can get at with a watch, and the number of times is a number I could have copied out of an old book and would have got wrong; and that at about eleven o'clock the reason I did not ask either of those two men why it is taking a minute longer each way is that the first time I asked, in the winter, one of them told me it was the damp, and the damp was in March, and now it is May and it is a minute longer again, and that a man who has been told a thing once by somebody who lives here and then heard it turn out to be wrong has to decide whether to ask again, and I decided not to, and that decision is mine and I have written it down; and that at about three in the afternoon the reason I did not send anybody up that coast road this morning to warn that route that their buoy was going to be hung this afternoon is that nobody in this yard asked them to and they would have had to be asked, and that six men from that route hung a bell this afternoon and I have written down that they hung it themselves and that this yard's hand did not touch the shackle, and that if this yard had sent word it would have been a word with no reason in it; and that at about five in the afternoon the reason that second line was read out once and not twice is that it is theirs and a line read twice by this yard becomes a line this yard has read, and there is one reading of it and it was by the woman of thirty of no office and it is in that yard's memory and not on that yard's page, and that at about half past five in the afternoon the reason I said in that shed that a page which only keeps the things that go with each other is a page of arguments is that I have caught myself twice this week about to write two days together because they went together, and both of those were the wall and the bell buoy and neither of them had anything to do with the other, and if I do that for fifty days then at the end of it this page will be a story and not a record; and that at about six in the afternoon the reason this slip ends on two walls and one buoy is that this yard has had five days of other people on its walls and one morning of its own hands on the water, and that is the correct proportion and I intend to keep it, and the wooden tally slate is still on that bench stone with nothing on it and nobody has picked it up.*
