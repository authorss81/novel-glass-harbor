# Chapter 698

*Saturday the Eighteenth Day of June, Not a Rota Day and Not a Wage Tuesday and Not a Wipe, That Cistern Not Charged and Its Covers Lying on It From Nine in the Morning Until Four in the Afternoon, the Wooden Tally Slate Lying Flat on That Bench Stone Where It Was Put Down on the First of May Turned Over Where It Lies at About Eleven in the Morning With a Hand on Its Edge and Not Lifted, What Is on the Other Side Not Rubbed Out and Nothing Written on It and the Slate Turned Back the Same Way at About One, the Wall in That Shed Looked At From the Floor at About Two With Two Lines on It and About Eleven Inches of Empty Plaster Between Them and Nothing Counted, and the Page of Hands Looked at in Front of About Four People and Standing at Fifteen With Four Names Gone off It and the Fall Written With the Day and the Hour Against It and the Old Figures Still Legible Underneath, Nothing Read Out of a Gap and No Figure Moved by One, the Money Sheet Carrying Two Lines and No Third and the Bag at Twenty-Eight Pence, Being Two Shillings and Fourpence, the First Column at Four Hundred and Thirty-One and the Second at Two Thousand and Twenty-Nine*

Saturday the eighteenth day of June, with that market open all day and about seventy people on those boards before eleven in the morning, a yard below that market with a shed standing in it, and about nine people in that shed at half past five that afternoon.

**Not a rota day and not a wage Tuesday and not a wipe, and no charge placed on that cistern and its covers lying on it from nine in the morning until four in the afternoon, and that butt behind the market covered from eight in the morning until four in the afternoon with no charge on it either. At about eleven in the morning the wooden tally slate that has been lying flat on that bench stone since it was put down on the first of May was turned over where it lies, with a hand on its edge and not lifted, and what is on the other side was not rubbed out and nothing was written on it, and it was turned back the same way at about one in the afternoon. At about two in the afternoon the wall in that shed was looked at from the floor by about four people and it carries two lines and about eleven inches of empty plaster between them and nothing on it was counted. The page of hands was looked at in about four people in the afternoon and stands at fifteen with four names gone off it and the fall written on the face of that page with the day and the hour against it and the old figures still legible underneath. Nothing went onto the money sheet and nothing came off it, and a hundred and sixty-eight pence came into that yard at about ten in the morning and a hundred and sixty-eight went out of it at about four in the afternoon, and under that slate at the close of the day is twenty-eight pence, being two shillings and fourpence, and no tin in that shed has been opened since the twenty-ninth of April past.**

**Nine counters went a day further today and eight of the nine are about things nobody has done. That notice of the seventeenth of May past is on its three hundred and ninety-sixth day. A bellwright of this harbor has not been asked for three hundred and ninety-eighth days, and his chest stays shut. Two objects of nobody's up on that crown are into their seven hundred and thirty-sixth day. That bench under that pump has stood bare for the five hundred and second night. Two lights about a mile off that point have spent three hundred and twentieth days with no figure against them. A six-line sheet at the back of that end stall has reached its two hundred and seventy-fifth day. The paper of four lines in Fish Street is on its two hundred and ninetieth. A nine-line sheet with a seal on it in that front street has stood three hundred and seventh days. A man of about fifty-one has kept three hundred and thirty-eighth days of not saying anything to anybody about a page of six names. That third column is empty and unappointed and a boy's hand of twenty-two is at the head of it and his surname is on no page and he is off the water. That fourth column stands at one hundred and twenty-five and has taken no line and is neither shut nor struck. Two of the five papers on that board at the head of that mole have never been opened by anybody in that harbor and nothing has gone up on that board this month. That basket in that boathouse holds a paper and nothing else in it and nobody has put a hand in. About two sea miles off that mole head lie nine hulls this afternoon and nine lay there on the twenty-first of January, and not one word has been said in this harbor about any of them easing. A man of about twenty-nine hears out of the left side of his head and it is one hundred and ninety-sixth days, one more than the day before, no figure is set against it, and he is not on the water.**

---

The slate was turned over where it lies.

It has been lying flat on that bench stone since it was put down on the first of May and nobody had picked it up on any of the forty-eight days since, and at about eleven this morning the man who keeps the leaf put four fingers on the edge of it and turned it over without lifting it off the stone, and about four people in that shed saw him do it.

It is a wooden tally slate and the other side of it is a wooden tally slate, and there are marks on it, and they are older than this yard is.

---

He did not rub them out and he did not write on them.

There are four groups of strokes cut into the slate itself with the corner of something hard, and they are worn pale in the middle of each stroke the way a thing that has been rubbed by a thumb for forty years goes, and there is no name on it and no day against any of them and nothing written in ink on either side.

About four people in that shed looked at it for about six minutes. One of them said that it was not his family's and the man who keeps the leaf said that he had not said it was.

Nobody in that shed said anything about what those four groups of strokes meant and nobody asked, and the man who keeps the leaf turned the slate back the same way at about one in the afternoon, with four fingers on the edge and without lifting it off the stone.

---

At about two in the afternoon four people stood up in that shed and looked at that wall from the floor.

It carries two lines on it and about eleven inches of empty plaster between them, and that plaster has been empty since the twentieth of May and has not been whitewashed and there is nothing on it and no nail hole in it that is new.

About four people looked at it for about a minute and a half each and nobody counted anything and nobody said a figure out loud, and the man who keeps the leaf said out loud that he was not counting that wall today and that if anybody wants to measure the plaster they can bring a rule and do it themselves.

About eleven inches. That is the only figure on that wall anybody can get without a ladder and he is not going to be the man who puts it in a slip.

---

At about half past three in the afternoon the man who keeps the leaf took the page of hands off its nail and put it on the flat of that bench stone in front of about four people.

He read the nine names down the side of it without saying any of the figures out loud, and he put his finger on the row where the four names used to be and the figures under them are still there and can still be read, and above them is one line that says four hands came off that page and names the day and the hour.

That is the whole of the fall and it is on the face of the page in two places and nobody took anything off it today and the sum was not added up.

He hung it back on its nail and about four people in that yard looked at the bare plaster where it had been.

---

At about four in the afternoon the man who keeps the leaf wrote the Saturday line on the page of that week and it says four things, being that the wooden tally slate was turned over where it lies at about eleven and turned back at about one and nothing was rubbed out and nothing was written on it, that four groups of strokes are cut into it and are older than this yard and carry no name and no day, that that wall carries two lines and about eleven inches of empty plaster and nothing on it was counted today, and that the page of hands was looked at in about four people and stands at fifteen with the four names gone off it and the fall written on the face of it.

Nobody thanked anybody for that line and nobody argued with it.

---

And at about half past five on that Saturday the man who keeps the leaf took the shortfall out loud at that bench stone, in about six hundred and eighty-seven words, to about nine people, and about four of those nine had been looking at the other side of a slate at about eleven this morning.

"**I turned that slate over at about eleven with four fingers on the edge and without lifting it off the stone it has lain on since the first of May, and I want nine people to know that I did that, and that nobody asked me to and that I did not do it for anybody. There are four groups of strokes cut into the other side of it and they are older than this yard and there is no name on that slate and no day against any of them and nothing in ink on either side. I did not rub them out. I did not write anything on them. I turned it back the same way at about one, and nobody in that shed asked me what they meant and I did not say.**

**And the reason I am saying it out loud is that a thing which has lain still for forty-eight days can be turned over without anybody deciding anything about it, and if it can be turned over it can be read, and the first person in this yard who wanted to know what was under it would not have needed anybody's permission. That is what happened on that wall in May and it cost about nine people a morning. I would rather nine people heard that from me in a shed than worked it out at two o'clock on a Monday.**

**Now that wall. Two lines on it and about eleven inches of empty plaster between them, and that plaster has been empty since the twentieth of May and there is no new nail hole in it. Four of us looked at it from the floor this afternoon and nobody counted anything and I said out loud that I was not counting that wall today and that anybody who wants a rule can bring a rule and do it themselves. That eleven inches is the only figure on that wall a person can get without a ladder and this yard is not going to be the one who writes it down.**

**The page of hands was on that stone at about half past three in about four people. It stands at fifteen. Four names came off it in May because somebody with a right to take them off said so, and the day and the hour are on the face of it, and the figures under those four names can still be read. Nobody added it up today and nobody took anything off it and the four of us stood and looked at a row of four empty spaces that are not empty, they are marked.**

**The sheet stands at one hundred and twelve thousand seven hundred and sixteen pence out, being seven hundred and four marks, six shillings and fourpence, against ten thousand six hundred and forty pence in, being sixty-six marks, six shillings and eightpence, and that is one hundred and two thousand and seventy-six pence short, being six hundred and thirty-seven marks and thirteen shillings. Two lines on it and no third. Four hundred and fifty pence is still on that board in the front street.**

**A hundred and sixty-eight pence in at about ten and a hundred and sixty-eight out at about four, and twenty-eight plus one hundred and sixty-eight less one hundred and sixty-eight is twenty-eight, and under that slate is twenty-eight pence, being two shillings and fourpence, and that is six hundred and twelve pence short of the wage of four marks, being three marks eleven shillings. No tin in that shed has been opened since the twenty-ninth of April past and this yard is now one hundred and eleven days without one.**

**And the last of it. That wall in that shed has two lines on it this evening and it has had two lines on it since the twentieth of May, and the eleven inches are empty, and the four names are off that page and the old figures can still be read underneath them. Both of those are plain and neither of them is going to be argued about in this market.**"

Nobody was thanked for the arithmetic and nobody was thanked for four groups of strokes, and that shed stood empty by about six with its door standing open on a slip nobody was on, and that page of that week had one line on it and a new week, and that wooden tally slate was lying flat on that bench stone again with nothing on it.

---

**AT ABOUT ELEVEN IN THE MORNING THE WOODEN TALLY SLATE THAT HAS BEEN LYING FLAT ON THAT BENCH STONE SINCE IT WAS PUT DOWN ON THE FIRST OF MAY WAS TURNED OVER WHERE IT LIES, WITH FOUR FINGERS ON ITS EDGE AND WITHOUT BEING LIFTED OFF THE STONE, AND THERE ARE FOUR GROUPS OF STROKES CUT INTO THE OTHER SIDE OF IT WITH THE CORNER OF SOMETHING HARD, AND THEY ARE OLDER THAN THIS YARD, AND THERE IS NO NAME ON IT AND NO DAY AGAINST ANY OF THEM AND NOTHING IN INK ON EITHER SIDE, AND NOTHING WAS RUBBED OUT AND NOTHING WAS WRITTEN ON IT, AND IT WAS TURNED BACK THE SAME WAY AT ABOUT ONE IN THE AFTERNOON AND NOBODY ASKED WHAT THOSE STROKES MEANT. AT ABOUT TWO IN THE AFTERNOON FOUR PEOPLE STOOD UP IN THAT SHED AND LOOKED AT THAT WALL FROM THE FLOOR AND IT CARRIES TWO LINES AND ABOUT ELEVEN INCHES OF EMPTY PLASTER BETWEEN THEM AND THAT PLASTER HAS BEEN EMPTY SINCE THE TWENTIETH OF MAY AND HAS NOT BEEN WHITEWASHED, AND NOTHING ON IT WAS COUNTED AND NO FIGURE WAS SAID OUT LOUD. THE PAGE OF HANDS WAS LOOKED AT IN ABOUT FOUR PEOPLE AT ABOUT HALF PAST THREE AND STANDS AT FIFTEEN WITH FOUR NAMES GONE OFF IT AND THE FALL WRITTEN ON THE FACE OF THAT PAGE WITH THE DAY AND THE HOUR AGAINST IT AND THE OLD FIGURES STILL LEGIBLE UNDERNEATH, AND THE SUM WAS NOT ADDED UP. THE MONEY SHEET STANDS AT ONE HUNDRED AND TWELVE THOUSAND SEVEN HUNDRED AND SIXTEEN PENCE OUT, BEING SEVEN HUNDRED AND FOUR MARKS, SIX SHILLINGS AND FOURPENCE, WITH TEN THOUSAND SIX HUNDRED AND FORTY PENCE IN, BEING SIXTY-SIX MARKS, SIX SHILLINGS AND EIGHTPENCE, AND IT IS ONE HUNDRED AND TWO THOUSAND AND SEVENTY-SIX PENCE SHORT, BEING SIX HUNDRED AND THIRTY-SEVEN MARKS AND THIRTEEN SHILLINGS, AND IT CARRIES TWO LINES AND NO THIRD. NOTHING WAS READ OUT OF A GAP AND NO FIGURE ON THE SHEET OF THE FOURTEEN WENT UP OR DOWN BY ONE. UNDER THAT SLATE IS TWENTY-EIGHT PENCE, BEING TWO SHILLINGS AND FOURPENCE.**

*Second column, not checkable from a mole, entered on the said Saturday at about six in the evening in that shed, and no name is against it: that at about eleven in the morning the reason that slate was turned over where it lies and not lifted off the stone is that a slate belongs to the bench it is lying on, and that a hand under the edge of it would have been a thing done on purpose in front of four people, and this yard has spent forty-seven days not doing things on purpose in front of people; and that at about eleven in the morning the reason nothing was rubbed out and nothing was written on the other side is that marks cut into a slate in another trade are not this yard's to tidy, and that a man who rubs out what he does not understand on a slate that has lain on a bench stone since the first of May is a man who has decided he owns the bench; and that at about two in the afternoon the reason that wall was looked at from the floor and not counted is that the only figure a person can get off that wall without a ladder is the width of the plaster between the two lines, and that a figure which belongs to one man and one wall stops being that man's the moment a stranger writes it down, and eleven inches has been standing on that wall since the twentieth of May with nobody holding it; and that at about half past three in the afternoon the reason the page of hands was read without a figure said out loud is that this yard took that page at fifteen on the twentieth of May in front of people who came on their own account and said no figure then and has not said one since, and that the four names that came off it came off because somebody with a right to take them off said so, and that the old figures being still legible underneath is the only proof this yard will ever have that it did not lose the page; and that at about half past five in the afternoon the reason that slip ends on two lines and four names is that a wall with two lines on it and a page with four names gone off it are the two halves of what this coast has actually done in fifty days, and that both halves are plain, and a slip which ends on either half alone is a slip arguing for one half.
