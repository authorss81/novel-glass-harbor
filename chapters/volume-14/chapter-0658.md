# Chapter 658

*Monday the Ninth Day of May, a Rota Day and the Hundred and Forty-Eighth Charge on That Cistern, Eleven Trips by Hand and Walked on by None of It and the Covers Left Lying on It from About Eleven Until Four, About Eleven People at the End Stall of About Whom Four Cannot Read a Notice and a Line of Forty-Three Words Going Up on That Wall at About One in the Afternoon and Read Out Twice and the Second Reading Not for the Nine Who Can Read, Nothing Read Out of a Gap and No Figure Moved by One, the Bag at Twenty-Eight Pence, Being Two Shillings and Fourpence, the First Column at Three Hundred and Ninety-One and the Second at One Thousand Eight Hundred and Twenty-Nine*

Monday the ninth day of May, with about forty people on the boards at the back of that market from ten in the morning, a yard below that market with a shed standing in it, and about nine people in that shed at half past five that afternoon.

**A rota day and not a wage Tuesday and not a wipe, and that cistern charged in eleven trips by hand between about eight and about eleven and walked on by none of it, and the hundred and forty-eighth charge, and the covers left lying on it from about eleven until about four. At about one in the afternoon about eleven people stood at the end stall of that market, of whom about four cannot read a notice, and the longest of the three lines on that wall went up at about one and was read out twice, once by a woman of thirty of no office and once by the man who keeps the leaf, and the second reading was not for the nine people who can read. Nothing went onto the money sheet and nothing came off it, and a hundred and sixty-eight pence came into that yard at about ten in the morning and a hundred and sixty-eight went out of it at about four in the afternoon, and under that slate at the close of the day is twenty-eight pence, being two shillings and fourpence.**

**Nine things in this harbor can be had off a wall by anybody with the patience to want them, and not one of the nine was found by looking for it. That notice of the seventeenth of May past has now stood three hundred and fifty-six days. A bellwright of this harbor has not been asked for three hundred and fifty-eight days, and his chest stays shut. Two objects of nobody's on that crown stand six hundred and ninety-six days. The bench under that pump has stood bare for the four hundred and sixty-second night. The two lights at about a mile off that point have been two hundred and eighty days without a figure against them. A six-line sheet at the back of that end stall is on its two hundred and thirty-fifth day. The paper of four lines in Fish Street is on its two hundred and fiftieth. A nine-line sheet with a seal on it in that front street is on its two hundred and sixty-seventh day. A man of about fifty-one has said nothing to anybody about a page of six names for two hundred and ninety-eight days. Five papers are on that board at the head of that mole and two of the five have never been opened by anybody in this harbor and nothing has been nailed on that board this month. In that boathouse a basket stands with a paper in it and nothing else in it. About two sea miles off the head of that mole lie nine hulls this afternoon and they were nine there on the twenty-first of January, and nobody in this harbor has said that any of them has eased. A man of about twenty-nine can hear out of the left side of his head and it is one hundred and fifty-six days, one more than yesterday, and no figure is set against it and he is not on the water.**

---

That cistern was charged in eleven trips by hand between about eight and about eleven and walked on by none of it, and it is the hundred and forty-eighth charge, and the covers went on at about eleven and came off at about four.

At about eleven the woman of about thirty-nine who mends net came past that cistern with a basket and looked at the covers on it and did not say anything, and she is not on anybody's crew and is not on any sheet and has said no once in this harbor and given a ground once, and neither of those is on this page and neither of them is going to be asked about.

---

At about one in the afternoon about eleven people stood at the end stall of that market. Four of them cannot read a notice. Nobody counted them and nobody counted them again, and there is no figure of them on any sheet in this harbor and there is not going to be.

The line that went up on that wall at about one is the Morrow Line's, and it is the third on that wall and it is the longest of the three by a long way, and it is in a third hand, and it has no seal and no name and nothing under it.

**We will not carry an echo of anybody in this harbor unless the person it came from says so in front of us, and a person may say no on any day and the no stands with nothing put in place of it.**

That is forty-three words and it is the plainest thing on that wall and it is about a route that this party lost in a storm that was not a weather in April and has not got back and is not going to get back, and it is written by about eleven people of that harbor, four of whom cannot read what they wrote.

---

It was read out twice.

The first reading was by the woman of thirty of no office, standing in the open at the flat of that bench stone, and about eleven people heard it and about four of them could not have read it themselves.

The second reading was by the man who keeps the leaf, in the same words, and about four people near enough to hear the second reading said afterwards that there was no need to do that again, and he said there was, and he gave the reason.

He said that about eleven people in this harbor cannot read a notice and that a line that is read once has been read to the four, and that there are about nine people who would have come in after the first reading if the market had been busier, and that the reason for the second reading is not politeness and it is not for the four, because a thing read out loud in the open to about eleven people has been read to all eleven whether four of them can follow it or not, and the second reading is for the yard.

The second reading is for the yard, because a yard that has a line on its wall that it has only heard once cannot say a month later that it knows what it says, and this yard is going to be asked a question about this coast in about five weeks and it will have to answer out loud, in about nine people, with no name against it, in a market where about eleven people cannot read a notice.

Nobody agreed with that and nobody argued with it and nobody thanked him for reading the same forty-three words out twice in the open air on a Monday.

---

And at about half past five on that Monday the man who keeps the leaf took the shortfall out loud at that bench stone, in about two hundred and fifty-one words, to about nine people.

"**One hundred and one thousand two hundred and thirty-six pence short, being six hundred and thirty-two marks, nine shillings and eightpence. One line on that sheet since the twentieth of April and the last hand to touch it was on the twentieth of April. Ten thousand six hundred and forty pence has ever gone into it, being sixty-six marks, six shillings and eightpence. Four hundred and fifty pence on that board in the front street. That cistern was charged in eleven trips by hand this morning between about eight and about eleven and walked on by none of it, and it is the hundred and forty-eighth, and the covers lay on it from about eleven until about four. A hundred and sixty-eight pence in at ten and a hundred and sixty-eight out at four and twenty-eight plus one hundred and sixty-eight less one hundred and sixty-eight is twenty-eight, and under that slate is twenty-eight pence, being two shillings and fourpence, and that is six hundred and twelve pence short of the wage of four marks, being three marks eleven shillings. No tin in that shed has been opened since the twenty-ninth of April past.**

**And three lines went up on that wall this week, being eleven words and nine words and forty-three, and they are about three different coasts and none of them is a line this yard wrote. That is the correct state of that wall and I intend to say so on this slip while it is still true.**"

**Here is the thing I have got to write down and I do not like it and I am going to write it anyway. That middle line is about a route this party lost in April in a storm that was not a weather, and about eleven people of this harbor have written down what that route will not carry, and they have written it down in forty-three words with nothing under it, and the fourth of them cannot read it. I have been in this yard sixty-eight days. In all that time not one person in this harbor has asked me to put their name to anything, and today about eleven of them put a sentence on a wall, and neither of those two facts is a reason for the other one, and I am not going to say that it is.**

Nobody was thanked for the arithmetic and nobody was thanked for reading a line out twice, and that shed stood empty by about six, and that wall had three lines on it in three hands of three different lengths, and about four people of this harbor who cannot read what was on it had heard it read out twice in the open air anyway.

---

**THAT CISTERN WAS CHARGED IN ELEVEN TRIPS BY HAND BETWEEN ABOUT EIGHT IN THE MORNING AND ABOUT ELEVEN AND WALKED ON BY NONE OF IT AND IT IS THE HUNDRED AND FORTY-EIGHTH CHARGE, THE COVERS LAYING ON IT FROM ABOUT ELEVEN UNTIL ABOUT FOUR IN THE AFTERNOON. ABOUT ELEVEN PEOPLE STOOD AT THAT END STALL OF ABOUT WHOM FOUR CANNOT READ A NOTICE, AND NOBODY COUNTED THEM AND NOBODY COUNTED THEM AGAIN AND THERE IS NO FIGURE OF THEM ON ANY SHEET IN THIS HARBOR. THE THIRD LINE ON THAT WALL IS FORTY-THREE WORDS AND IS THE MORROW LINE'S OWN, AND IT SAYS THAT THEY WILL NOT CARRY AN ECHO OF ANYBODY IN THIS HARBOR UNLESS THE PERSON IT CAME FROM SAYS SO IN FRONT OF THEM, AND THAT A PERSON MAY SAY NO ON ANY DAY AND THE NO STANDS WITH NOTHING PUT IN PLACE OF IT, AND IT WAS READ OUT TWICE, ONCE BY A WOMAN OF THIRTY OF NO OFFICE AND ONCE BY THE MAN WHO KEEPS THE LEAF, AND THE SECOND READING WAS NOT FOR THE FOUR WHO CANNOT READ IT. THE MONEY SHEET IS AT ONE HUNDRED AND ELEVEN THOUSAND EIGHT HUNDRED AND SEVENTY-SIX PENCE OUT AGAINST TEN THOUSAND SIX HUNDRED AND FORTY PENCE IN AND IS ONE HUNDRED AND ONE THOUSAND TWO HUNDRED AND THIRTY-SIX PENCE SHORT. NOTHING WAS READ OUT OF A GAP AND NO FIGURE ON THE SHEET OF THE FOURTEEN WENT UP OR DOWN BY ONE. UNDER THAT SLATE IS TWENTY-EIGHT PENCE, BEING TWO SHILLINGS AND FOURPENCE.**

*First column, entered on Monday the ninth day of May at about six in the evening in that shed: that the said day was a rota day and was not a wage Tuesday and was not a wipe, and that the cistern at the back of that market was charged in eleven trips by hand between about eight in the morning and about eleven and was walked on by none of it, and that it is the hundred and forty-eighth charge and that the covers were left lying on it from about eleven until about four in the afternoon; and that at about one in the afternoon about eleven people stood at the end stall of that market of whom about four cannot read a notice, and that nobody counted them and nobody counted them again and that there is no figure of them on any sheet in this harbor; and that the third line on that wall is forty-three words and belongs to the Morrow Line and is the third on that wall and the longest of the three, and that it says that they will not carry an echo of anybody in this harbor unless the person it came from says so in front of them, and that a person may say no on any day and the no stands with nothing put in place of it; and that it was written by about eleven people of that harbor of whom about four cannot read what they wrote, and that it is about a route this party lost in April in a storm that was not a weather and has not got back; and that it was read out twice, once by the woman of thirty of no office and once by the man who keeps the leaf, in the same words, and that about eleven people heard the first reading and about four of them could not have read it themselves; and that at about twenty past one in the afternoon about four people near enough to hear the second reading said there was no need to do that again, and that he said there was and gave the reason, which is that a line that is read once has been read to the four, and that the second reading is for this yard, because a yard that has a line on its wall that it has only heard once cannot say a month later that it knows what it says; and that nobody agreed with that and nobody argued with it and nobody thanked him; and that nothing went onto that money sheet and nothing came off it, and that a hundred and sixty-eight pence came into that yard at about ten in the morning and a hundred and sixty-eight went out at about four in the afternoon, and that twenty-eight plus one hundred and sixty-eight less one hundred and sixty-eight is twenty-eight, and that the first column takes one entry a day and three hundred and eighty-three and one is three hundred and ninety-one at the close of it, and that the second takes five a day and one thousand seven hundred and eighty-nine and five is one thousand eight hundred and twenty-nine.*

*Second column, not checkable from a mole, entered on the said Monday at about six in the evening in that shed, and no name is against it: that at about eleven in the morning the reason I have not written down in this column that a woman of about thirty-nine who mends net came past that cistern this morning with a basket and looked at the covers on it is that she is on nobody's crew and is on no sheet and has said no once in this harbor and given a ground once, and that a second column is not a place to keep a person who has already told this harbor no; and that at about one in the afternoon the reason I did not stop that crowd at that end stall and ask them how many of them there were is that the figure of how many people stood there is not a figure this page is for, and that this yard has had a wage column read out in four minutes on eighty Tuesdays and has never once counted the people in front of it; and that at about twenty past one in the afternoon the reason I read that line out a second time, when about four people had already said there was no need, is that about eleven people in this harbor cannot read a notice and that about four of them had just signed a sentence on a wall, and that a man who has put his mark on a thing he cannot read has been done a disservice by anybody who reads it once, and that the second reading does not fix that and is not supposed to and is the smallest thing that can be done about it; and that at about three in the afternoon the reason I have not written down what that forty-three-word line says about April is that the line does not mention April and neither does the page it goes on, and that if this yard wrote about April on that wall it would be writing a fourth sentence on a page that belongs to three other coasts, and that at about four in the afternoon the reason I have written down that in sixty-eight days not one person in this harbor has asked me to put their name to anything and that today about eleven of them put a sentence on a wall is that those two facts sit next to each other in my head all the time and they are not a reason for each other, and that a page that says a keeper is proud of his wall is a page about a keeper; and that at about half past five in the afternoon the reason this slip opens on that cistern is that eleven trips by hand is a thing a stranger can watch from a mole, and about four people of this harbor who cannot read a column were standing at that end stall at one o'clock this afternoon and I would rather this page started with a thing they could see.*
