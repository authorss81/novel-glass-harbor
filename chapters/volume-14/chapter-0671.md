# Chapter 671

*Sunday the Twenty-Second Day of May, a Wipe and the Hundred and Seventh Time That the Wall Behind a Reeve's Bench Has Come Down Bare, Twenty-Four Minutes to Get It Off the Wall and Eleven Standing Bare and Twenty-Four to Get It Back, Being Fifty-Nine the Whole of It From About Ten Past Ten to About Nine Minutes Past Eleven, a Minute Longer Each Way and a Minute Longer Bare Than the Sunday Before and Nobody in That Harbor Having Said Why, a Man of About Fifty of the Lowfen Reed's Own Third Lay Standing at That End Stall at About Two in the Afternoon in About Nine People and Saying Out Loud That His Route Is One Man Short on Wednesday Night and That He Is Not Going to Ask Anybody in This Yard, and This Yard Not Sending for Anybody and Not Asking Him Who He Was Going to Ask, Nothing Read Out of a Gap and No Figure Moved by One, the Money Sheet Untouched and the Bag at Twenty-Eight Pence, Being Two Shillings and Fourpence, the First Column at Four Hundred and Four and the Second at One Thousand Eight Hundred and Ninety-Four*

Sunday the twenty-second day of May, with that market shut for the morning and standing open for the afternoon, a yard below that market with a shed in it, and about nine people in that shed at half past five that afternoon.

**A wipe, and the hundred and seventh time that wall behind a reeve's bench has come down, and no rota day and no wage Tuesday in it, and that butt behind the market covered from nine until four with no charge on it. Two men began to take that wall off its holders at about ten past ten and it was bare by about half past ten and it was back on its holders at about nine minutes past eleven, at the angle it has carried since the twelfth of January, and the thing above the frame is still not whole. Twenty-four minutes off the wall, eleven minutes standing bare, twenty-four to get it back, which is fifty-nine minutes the whole of it, and that is a minute longer each way and a minute longer bare than the Sunday before, and nobody in this harbor has said why it is getting slower and this yard has stopped asking the two men who do it. At about two in the afternoon a man of about fifty of the Lowfen Reed's own third lay stood at that end stall in about nine people and said out loud that his route is one man short on Wednesday night and that he is not going to ask anybody in this yard, and this yard did not send for anybody and did not ask him who he was going to ask. Nothing went onto the money sheet and nothing came off it, and a hundred and sixty-eight pence came into that yard at about ten in the morning and a hundred and sixty-eight went out of it at about four in the afternoon, and under that slate at the close of the day is twenty-eight pence, being two shillings and fourpence, and no tin in that shed has been opened since the twenty-ninth of April past.**

**Nine things in this harbor can be had off a wall by anybody with the patience to want them, and not one of the nine was found by looking for it. That notice of the seventeenth of May past stands at three hundred and sixty-ninth days. A bellwright of this harbor has not been asked for three hundred and seventy-first days, and his chest stays shut. Two objects of nobody's on that crown stand seven hundred and ninth days. The bench under that pump has stood bare for the four hundred and seventy-fifth night. Two lights at about a mile off that point have been two hundred and ninety-third days without a figure against them. A six-line sheet at the back of that end stall is on its two hundred and forty-eighth day. The paper of four lines in Fish Street is on its two hundred and sixty-third. A nine-line sheet with a seal on it in that front street is on its two hundred and eightieth day. A man of about fifty-one has said nothing to anybody about a page of six names for three hundred and eleventh days. The third column of that leaf is empty and unappointed with a boy's hand of twenty-two at the head of it, his surname is on no page, and he is off the water. The fourth column of that leaf stands at one hundred and twenty-five and has taken no line and is neither shut nor struck. Five papers are on that board at the head of that mole and two of the five have never been opened by anybody in this harbor, and nothing has been nailed on that board this month, and in that boathouse a basket stands with a paper in it and nothing else in it and nobody has put a hand in to look. About two sea miles off the head of that mole lie nine hulls this afternoon and they were nine there on the twenty-first of January, and not one word about any of them easing has been said in this harbor. A man of about twenty-nine can hear out of the left side of his head and it is one hundred and sixty-ninth days, one more than the day before, and no figure is set against it, and he is not on the water.**

---

That wall came down bare for the hundred and seventh time this morning and it went up again at about nine minutes past eleven and it did not go up whole.

Twenty-four minutes to get it off the wall, eleven standing with it bare, and twenty-four to get it back, which is fifty-nine the whole of it from about ten past ten, and it is a minute longer each way and a minute longer bare than the Sunday before it, and the Sunday before that was a minute longer than the one before that, and neither of the two men who does that work has said one word about it and this yard has stopped asking.

About four people near enough to see it happen saw it happen and went on with what they were doing, which is what happened on the last four Sundays as well.

---

There were about eleven inches of empty plaster on that wall between the two lines that are on it, and it is not to be whitewashed, and it was not whitewashed this morning while the whole of that shed could hear the ladder going up and down the outside of the wall.

Nobody in this harbor has anything to put in it. That is not a figure and it is not a thing anybody is going to improve.

---

At about two in the afternoon a man of about fifty of the Lowfen Reed's own third lay came down to that end stall, where the sheet of fourteen gaps hangs beside the page of hands, and stood in about nine people and said one thing out loud.

He said that the Reed is one man short on Wednesday night.

He said that the Reed is a route that runs inside written limits and that on Wednesday night there is a lift to be done inside those limits and that one man short is one man short, and that he is not going to take anybody off another boat to make the number come out.

He said that he is not asking anybody in this yard, and that he came down that lane on Saturday for a different thing altogether and that this afternoon he is standing at a stall and talking.

He did not say who he was going to ask. He was not asked.

---

The man of about thirty-two of that harbor was in those nine people and said nothing at all.

Nobody turned to him and nobody said his name, and he was not asked, and this yard wrote down that he said nothing and wrote down not one word about what he said nothing to.

The man who keeps the leaf put the hour against what the man of the Reed said and did not put a name against it, because a man who has come four days down that coast road with a boat that goes in a day and a night has not put himself in this harbor's book by standing at a stall.

Nobody thanked him for coming and nobody thanked him for not asking.

---

And at about half past five on that Sunday the man who keeps the leaf took the shortfall out loud at that bench stone, in about five hundred and two words, to about nine people, and about four of those nine had been in that shed at about ten o'clock that morning.

"**Twenty-four minutes off that wall this morning, eleven standing bare, twenty-four to get it back, from about ten past ten to about nine minutes past eleven, and that is fifty-nine the whole of it, and I am going to give you the three numbers and not the number, because the three numbers are the work and the number is only the sum of them.**

**One hundred and one thousand two hundred and thirty-six pence short, being six hundred and thirty-two marks, nine shillings and eightpence. One line on that sheet since the twentieth of April. Ten thousand six hundred and forty pence has ever gone into it, being sixty-six marks, six shillings and eightpence. Four hundred and fifty pence is still on that board in the front street. No charge on that cistern and the covers lay on it from nine until four.**

**A hundred and sixty-eight pence in at about ten and a hundred and sixty-eight out at about four, and twenty-eight plus one hundred and sixty-eight less one hundred and sixty-eight is twenty-eight, and under that slate is twenty-eight pence, being two shillings and fourpence, and that is six hundred and twelve pence short of the wage of four marks, being three marks eleven shillings. No tin in that shed has been opened since the twenty-ninth of April past and this yard is eighty-four days without one.**

**A man stood at that end stall at about two this afternoon and said his own route is one man short on Wednesday night, and he said it in about nine people, and he said he was not going to ask anybody in this yard. I did not send for him. Nobody in that yard sent for him. He has not told me who he means to ask and I have not asked him and I am not going to, and I want that written down in the same words, because the first thing this harbor does with a gap is send a man, and a man who is sent is a man who was not asked, and a man who was not asked is not a man who said yes.**

**And here is the shape of the day and not the number of it. That wall stood bare for eleven minutes this morning for the hundred and seventh time and it went back up at the angle it has carried since the twelfth of January and it did not go up whole, and it is a minute longer each way and a minute longer bare than the Sunday before, and the two men who do it have not said a word and this yard has stopped asking them. That is the first minute on this coast that nobody can account for. A wall that takes a minute longer every Sunday is a wall telling this yard something, and it is telling it in plaster and not in words, and plaster is what this coast mostly gets out of everybody, the Crown included.**"

Nobody was thanked for the arithmetic and nobody was thanked for fifty-nine minutes, and that shed stood empty by about six with its door standing open on a slip nobody was on, and that wall had two lines on it and about eleven inches of empty plaster between them, and that wooden tally slate was still lying flat on that bench stone with nothing on it.

---

**THAT WALL BEHIND A REEVE'S BENCH CAME DOWN BARE AT ABOUT HALF PAST TEN ON THAT SUNDAY AND WENT UP AGAIN AT ABOUT NINE MINUTES PAST ELEVEN AT THE ANGLE IT HAS BEEN AT SINCE THE TWELFTH OF JANUARY AND DID NOT GO UP WHOLE, AND IT TOOK TWENTY-FOUR MINUTES OFF THE WALL, ELEVEN STANDING BARE AND TWENTY-FOUR TO GET IT BACK, BEING FIFTY-NINE MINUTES THE WHOLE OF IT, AGAINST TWENTY-THREE AND TEN AND TWENTY-THREE ON THE SUNDAY BEFORE, AND NOBODY HAS SAID WHY IT IS GETTING SLOWER AND THIS YARD HAS STOPPED ASKING THE TWO MEN WHO DO IT. AT ABOUT TWO IN THE AFTERNOON A MAN OF ABOUT FIFTY OF THE LOWFEN REED'S OWN THIRD LAY STOOD AT THAT END STALL IN ABOUT NINE PEOPLE AND SAID OUT LOUD THAT HIS ROUTE IS ONE MAN SHORT ON WEDNESDAY NIGHT AND THAT HE IS NOT GOING TO ASK ANYBODY IN THIS YARD, AND HE DID NOT SAY WHO HE WAS GOING TO ASK AND HE WAS NOT ASKED. THE MAN OF ABOUT THIRTY-TWO OF THAT HARBOR WAS IN THOSE NINE PEOPLE AND SAID NOTHING AT ALL AND WAS NOT ASKED TO SAY ANYTHING. THE MONEY SHEET IS AT ONE HUNDRED AND ELEVEN THOUSAND EIGHT HUNDRED AND SEVENTY-SIX PENCE OUT, BEING SIX HUNDRED AND NINETY-NINE MARKS AND THREE SHILLINGS, AGAINST TEN THOUSAND SIX HUNDRED AND FORTY PENCE IN, BEING SIXTY-SIX MARKS, SIX SHILLINGS AND EIGHTPENCE, AND IT IS ONE HUNDRED AND ONE THOUSAND TWO HUNDRED AND THIRTY-SIX PENCE SHORT, BEING SIX HUNDRED AND THIRTY-TWO MARKS, NINE SHILLINGS AND EIGHTPENCE, AND IT CARRIES ONE LINE AND NO SECOND. NOTHING WAS READ OUT OF A GAP AND NO FIGURE ON THE SHEET OF THE FOURTEEN WENT UP OR DOWN BY ONE. UNDER THAT SLATE IS TWENTY-EIGHT PENCE, BEING TWO SHILLINGS AND FOURPENCE.**

*First column, entered on Sunday the twenty-second day of May at about six in the evening in that shed: that the said day was a wipe and was the hundred and seventh time that wall behind a reeve's bench has come down, and that it was not a rota day and was not a wage Tuesday, and that no charge was placed on that cistern and that the covers lay on it from nine in the morning until four in the afternoon; and that two men began to take that wall off its holders at about ten past ten in the morning and that it was bare by about half past ten and that it was back on the holders at about nine minutes past eleven at the angle it has carried since the twelfth of January and that it did not go up whole, and that it took twenty-four minutes to get off the wall, eleven standing bare and twenty-four to get it back, which is fifty-nine minutes the whole of it from about ten past ten, and that that is a minute longer each way and a minute longer bare than the Sunday before it, and that nobody in this harbor has said why it is getting slower and that this yard has stopped asking the two men who do it; and that about four people near enough to see it happen saw it happen and went on with what they were doing, which is what happened on the last four Sundays as well; and that there were about eleven inches of empty plaster on that wall between the two lines that are on it, and that it is not to be whitewashed, and that it was not whitewashed this morning while the whole of that shed could hear the ladder going up and down the outside of the wall; and that nobody in this harbor has anything to put in it, and that that is not a figure and is not a thing anybody is going to improve; and that at about two in the afternoon a man of about fifty of the Lowfen Reed's own third lay came down to that end stall, where the sheet of fourteen gaps hangs beside the page of hands, and stood in about nine people and said one thing out loud; and that he said that the Reed is one man short on Wednesday night, and that the Reed is a route that runs inside written limits and that on Wednesday night there is a lift to be done inside those limits, and that one man short is one man short, and that he is not going to take anybody off another boat to make the number come out; and that he said that he is not asking anybody in this yard, and that he came down that lane on Saturday for a different thing altogether and that this afternoon he is standing at a stall and talking; and that the man who keeps the leaf put the hour against what the man of the Reed said and did not put a name against it, because a man who has come four days down that coast road with a boat that goes in a day and a night has not put himself in this harbor's book by standing at a stall; and that at about six in the evening that shed stood empty and that wooden tally slate was still lying flat on that bench stone with nothing on it.*

*Second column, not checkable from a mole, entered on the said Sunday at about six in the evening in that shed, and no name is against it: that at about half past ten in the morning the reason the hours are written out one at a time and not as a single figure is that a figure is what this page is for and an hour is what a man standing at a stall can check against his own morning, and a stranger who wants to know when that wall came down can be told ten past ten without being told a number; and that at about two in the afternoon the reason no name is against what the man of the Reed said is that this yard has a rule about names and it is that a name goes on a page when a person puts it there, and that standing at a stall and saying a thing out loud in nine people is not putting a name on a page, and that if this yard wrote his name against it on Monday then this yard would be keeping a record of who is short of hands on which route, and that is a different page and a worse one and this coast has refused it six times; and that at about half past two in the afternoon the reason nothing is written about what the man of about thirty-two did not say is that a silence in nine people is not a finding, and that the whole of what this yard has been doing since the second of May is to leave silences alone, and that a page which prints its own silences is a page that has stopped listening; and that at about three in the afternoon the reason the empty plaster is written down this week for the fifth time is that a wall is a thing a stranger can measure and an argument is not, and that the eleven inches have been on that wall since Friday the twentieth and are the only part of that wall anybody can get a figure off without a ladder; and that at about half past five in the afternoon the reason this slip ends on the wall and not on the man of the Reed is that the man of the Reed has been here for an hour and has said one true thing and will go back up that coast road tomorrow, and that a slip is for the things that will still be standing on Monday, and a man of fifty going home is not one of them.*
