# Chapter 421

*Monday the Thirteenth Day of September, the Seventy-First Time the Wall Behind the Reeve's Bench Came Down Bare and Went Up at the Angle It Has Been At Since the Twelfth and Nobody Put It Back, the Eighty-First Time the Cistern Was Charged at About Eight in the Morning and Walked on by None of It at About Half Past Nine on a Rota of a Monday and a Thursday, One Hundred and Sixty-Eight Pence of a Day With a Boat and Two Men at a Berth Coming In at the Figure in Chalk on a Slate at an End Stall, a Man of About Thirty of the Third Lay Called Emmet Slade Who Cannot Read Coming Up a Slip at Two in the Afternoon to Ask Whether There Is a Paper Against Him and Being Told Out Loud That There Is One and That This Yard Knows Nothing About What Is On It, and a Man of About Thirty-Four Off a Screw-Steam Packet Out of Bramble Strait Landing a Sack of Printed Sheets at That Quay at About Four in the Afternoon*

Monday the thirteenth day of September, in that shed, in that market, in Fish Street, and on that mole head.

**The said Monday was not a wage Tuesday and was a wipe, being the seventy-first time the wall behind the reeve's bench came down bare, and it went up at the angle it has been at since the twelfth and nobody put it back, and it was a rota day and the cistern at the back of that market was charged at about eight in the morning and walked on by none of it at about half past nine, being the eighty-first time, and the top name on that rota board is still off it. One hundred and sixty-eight pence came into that yard on the said day, being a day's work with a boat and two men at a berth at the figure in chalk on a slate at an end stall, being one mark and eightpence, and nothing went out of it, and there are five hundred and seventy-six pence in the bag, being three marks eight shillings, and the figure that bag is short of the wage of four marks is sixty-four pence, being five shillings and fourpence, and it is not going to be paid and nobody is to write one word under it saying anybody is going to.**

**And the far bench was occupied on the said Monday from about ten in the morning until about four in the afternoon and was not approached on it, and the holder of that writ did not go over to him, and nothing was put on that bench and read as a gift, and neither brother has said why. Mara Venn was in that harbor and going nowhere, of no office, not on the crew, not restored, not forgiven, forty marks first after food, neither reduced nor paid, and she was at the bench stone in that shed from about two in the afternoon and did not go up the slip. Tobias Wren was on the crown of that mole from ten until four, being sixty-one, with eleven pieces of a boat behind him and a boat's hook in his right hand, and on the said day he got the second piece up off the stones and turned it over twice and set the edge of it in a crack that was already in the stone, and stood looking at the crack for about twenty minutes. The notice of the seventeenth of May past is on its wall in a different street for one hundred and nineteen days and has not been touched. The bellwright of that harbor has not been asked for one hundred and twenty-one days, the chest is shut, and the eight marks and a day of a boat have not been argued, and no digit of anything he ever said is on a page. The three nots standing against the eighth of June past are a tenth week in which nobody in that crew has said one word about them, and the hand of a boy of twenty-two is still at the head of the third column with a name beside it that has not been said out loud since the thirteenth of May past, and that name is not on any page of this harbor. The two objects of nobody's on that crown are four hundred and fifty-nine days old and neither has moved. The bench under that pump is bare for the two hundred and twenty-fifth night. Two lights at about a mile off that point have not moved in forty-three days, counted from the hour they were first seen on the evening of the first of August past. A man of about fifty-one has said nothing to anybody about a page of six names for sixty-one days. A nine-line sheet with a seal on it has been in that front street for thirty days and has been answered by nobody. The paper at the top left of that door in Fish Street is thirteen days old and has been answered by nobody, and the fourth line of it ends on the twentieth day of September next, which is seven days off.**

---

The wall came down at about seven and went up at about ten to eight, the way it has gone since the twelfth of May, and the man who has the first hour of it did not look at it.

Then the cistern was filled at about eight from the butt at the back of that market and it stood there full until about half past nine, and the top name on the rota board is still off it, and it is the eighty-first time, and nobody in that market took any of it.

---

Emmet Slade came up that slip at about two in the afternoon. He is of about thirty and he is of that third lay and he has a boat of about nine tons lying at the head of it, and he has had a claim window on a wreck off the point since the end of July, and the window is a piece of paper with his name on it and an hour on it and it lets him work one wreck and claim what comes up off it.

He could not read. He had never been able to read and he said so in the first half minute, before anybody had offered him anything, because he had been made a fool in a market once before and he would rather have it said first.

He put one hand flat on the bench stone, took it off again and wiped it on his thigh.

"There is a paper against me," he said.

"Who told you there is a paper against you."

"Nobody told me. I have heard there is one."

"Who heard it from."

He thought about that for a while.

"A man at a counter heard it and he said it in front of me and I did not ask him where he got it and I am not going to go back and ask him," said Emmet Slade. "That is the whole of what I have. I have come up here because a man reads for a living in that shed, if he does, and I cannot get at the bottom of it by myself and I have nobody to ask."

Nessa Pike put the slate down.

"I will tell you what I know and it is not much and I am not going to dress it up," she said. "There is a paper. It is not this yard's paper and it is not in this shed and I have not seen it. It is at a counter in an office off the west basin and it is a paper of that harbor and it has to do with a claim and not with a wreck, and that is all I know and that is all anybody in this shed knows."

"Can you read it to me."

"No," said Calder Marr. "Not because of the paper. Because of you. You cannot read and I have got no way of knowing what is on it, and if I stood in a street and read you a version of it out of my own head you would have my head and not the paper, and in about a week you would be telling people in a market what Calder Marr said was on a paper at the office on the west basin, and there would be no way back from that to what is actually on it. That is how it starts. That is exactly how it starts."

"So what do I do."

"You go to the counter and you ask for it and you ask for it to be read to you, and you take somebody with you who can read and who is not me and is not anybody who has been paid this month, and you do not take a paper away in your hand that you cannot read in your own hand."

"What if there is nobody."

"Then you stand at that counter and you say out loud, in front of the man behind it, that you cannot read and that you have asked him to read it to you, and you take a man with you who can hear him say that he has done it," said Calder Marr. "That costs an afternoon and it is the only thing in this harbor that costs a man nothing and it is the only thing that works."

"You have had a man read things to me before."

"You have, and you have come down here and told me you had, which is more than the man who read them to you would have done for you," said Calder Marr. "That is not me being kind. That is me telling you what has already happened to you once."

Emmet Slade stood in the middle of that shed with his hand flat on the bench stone and looked at it for a while.

"Is it against my boat."

"I do not know," said Calder Marr. "Nobody in this yard knows and I am not going to tell you that it is not, because that would be a kindness and it would be about a hundred and sixty pence a day of your work and I have not got that. It may be against your boat. It may be against your name and not your boat. It may be against nothing at all and be a paper somebody put on a counter because there is a counter."

"Then I will go tomorrow."

"Go tomorrow," said Nessa Pike, "and come back and tell me what you were told, and do not tell me what you think it means, and do not tell me what you think it means about the boat either. Bring me the words."

He nodded and went down the slip, and the leaf of that yard was open on the bench stone in front of them with a pencil lying two inches away from it.

---

At about four in the afternoon a screw-steam packet came down that quay and put her hook on and a man of about thirty-four came ashore with a sack on his shoulder that was heavier than paper ought to be.

He is not of this harbor and he is not going to be here long. He is a working man and he does not belong to anything and nobody asked him a question and nobody thanked him and he is not the man who sent whatever is in the sack, and this is the whole of what is known about him and all of it came out of his own mouth in about four minutes on a quay.

The stones were wet and there was about half an hour of light left in them. He put the sack down at the head of the slip and stood over it with his hands on his knees and would not have let anybody he did not know open it, and nobody he did not know came.

"A stall-holder in this harbor takes a packet off me twice a month," he said. "She took one on Thursday and this one is for the same stall, and I put the lot down on her counter and she has not counted it and I am not going to count it for her. It came off the steamer at the far end. It was given to me at the top of the gangway by a man behind a counter with a book open in front of him, and he did not ask my name, and he did not ask what it was for, and he did not ask who it was for."

"Did you look at it."

"I looked at the top sheet," said the man of about thirty-four. "That is all I am going to say about it, and I am not going to be asked about it a second time and I am not going to be asked by anybody in this harbor either."

He put the sack down on the counter of a stall at the end of the fish market, wrote nothing, and went back up the gangway, and the packet's hook was up again in about twenty minutes and she went out on the evening tide.

There is a sheet of paper at the top left of the door of the room over a joiner's shop at the top of Fish Street, and it has been there thirteen days, and it has four lines on it and a drawing-pin under a fold of oiled paper, and the fourth of those four lines ends on the twentieth day of September next, which is seven days off and is a Monday, and nobody in that harbor has answered it and nobody in that harbor is going to.

**One hundred and sixty-eight pence came into that yard on the said Monday at the figure in chalk on a slate at an end stall and nothing went out of it, and the bag is five hundred and seventy-six pence, being three marks eight shillings, and is short of the wage of four marks, being six hundred and forty pence, by sixty-four pence, being five shillings and fourpence. The wall behind the reeve's bench came down bare for the seventy-first time and went up at the angle it has been at since the twelfth and nobody put it back. The cistern was charged at about eight and walked on at about half past nine for the eighty-first time and the top name on the rota board is still off it. A man of about thirty of the third lay called Emmet Slade, who has a boat of about nine tons and a claim window on a wreck off the point and who cannot read, came up that slip at about two in the afternoon and said he had heard there was a paper against him, and was told by the ship's hand that there was a paper at a counter in an office off the west basin and that nobody in that shed had seen it, and was told by the holder of that writ that it could not be read to him there, because he would have the reader's head and not the paper, and was told that nobody in that shed knows whether it is against his boat and that if it were not against his boat that would be a kindness and not a finding. A man of about thirty-four off a screw-steam packet out of Bramble Strait landed a sack of printed sheets at a stall at the end of that market at about four in the afternoon and said out loud that the lot had been given to him at the top of a gangway by a man behind a counter with a book open, that he was not asked his name and not asked what it was for, that he had looked at the top sheet and was not going to say more about it, and that he was not going to be asked a second time; he wrote nothing down and went back up the gangway and the packet went out on the evening tide. The paper at the top left of that door in Fish Street is thirteen days old and its fourth line ends on the twentieth of September next and has been answered by nobody. The far bench was occupied from ten until four and was not approached, and neither brother went near the other at any hour of the said day.**

*First column, entered on Monday the thirteenth day of September next at about half past nine in the evening in that shed: that the said Monday was the seventy-first time the wall behind the reeve's bench came down bare, and that it went up at the angle it has been at since the twelfth and nobody put it back; and that it was a rota day and the cistern was charged at about eight in the morning and walked on by none of it at about half past nine, being the eighty-first time, and that the top name on that rota board is still off it; and that one hundred and sixty-eight pence came into that yard on the said day, being a day's work with a boat and two men at a berth at the figure in chalk on a slate at an end stall, being one mark and eightpence, and that nothing went out of it, and that there are five hundred and seventy-six pence in the bag, being three marks eight shillings, and that the figure the bag is short of the wage of four marks, being six hundred and forty pence, is sixty-four pence, being five shillings and fourpence; and that a man of about thirty of the third lay called Emmet Slade, who has a boat of about nine tons and a claim window on a wreck off the point since the end of July and who cannot read and who said so before anybody offered him anything, came up that slip at about two in the afternoon and said that he had heard there was a paper against him and that a man at a counter had said so in front of him and that he was not going back to ask where that man got it; and that he was told that the paper is at a counter in an office off the west basin and is not that yard's and is not in that shed and has not been seen in that shed; and that he asked whether the paper could be read to him and was refused by the holder of that writ in his own mouth, with his own reason, being that a man who cannot read and a man who has not seen the paper together make a version of it out of the reader's head, and that in about a week that version would be in a market with this yard's name on it; and that he asked whether it was against his boat and was told that nobody in that shed knows and that being told it was not would be a kindness and not a finding; and that he said he would go to the counter on the Tuesday and come back and say what he was told and not what he made of it; and that a man of about thirty-four off a screw-steam packet out of Bramble Strait landed a sack of printed sheets at a stall at the end of that fish market at about four in the afternoon and put them on a counter and wrote nothing down and went out on the evening tide; and that the said man said out loud that the lot had been given to him at the top of a gangway by a man behind a counter with a book open in front of him and that he was not asked his name and not asked what the lot was for and not asked who it was for, and that he had looked at the top sheet and was not going to say more about it and was not going to be asked about it a second time; and that the paper at the top left of that door in Fish Street is thirteen days old and its fourth line ends on the twentieth of September next, being seven days off, and has been answered by nobody.*

*Second column, not checkable from a mole, entered on the said Monday at about half past nine in the evening in that shed, and no name is against it: that the reason the holder of that writ would not read that paper to a man of about thirty who cannot read is in his own mouth and is that a person who has not seen a paper and a person who cannot read one together produce a third thing, which is the reader's head, and that a version of a paper that is in a man's head gets into a market in about a week with this yard's name at the bottom of it; and that the reason the ship's hand put the slate down and told him what she knew and stopped there is that what she knows is the paper's existence and the counter it is on and the words claim and wreck, and that four things is what there is and that a fifth thing would have been somebody deciding what four things mean; and that the reason a man of about thirty-four off a packet put a sack on a counter and wrote nothing down is in his own mouth and is that he carries goods and is not paid to read them and was not asked for his name by the man that gave them to him, and that he said he was not going to be asked a second time, and that no figure is in that sack and none is against it in any column of this yard's leaf; and that the sack is standing on a counter at the end of that market at the close of the said day and nobody in that harbor has opened it and nobody in that yard is going to open it for him; and that the standing list entered in full on the fourteenth of August past is not entered in full on the said Monday and stands, and no part of it was paid, reduced, advanced or improved on that day.*
