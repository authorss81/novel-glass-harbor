# Chapter 411

*Friday the Third Day of September, a Room over a Joiner's Shop at the Top of Fish Street With a Table in It and a Bound Book on the Table and Four People Reading It Through From the Front Page to the Back, a Working Man of About Forty-Six Called Hobb Lee Who Mends Nets on the Third Lay and Who Can Read and Who Is Going to Read Every Head-Note in That Room Out Loud With His Own Name and the Hour Under It, a Bowl Carried Down to the Step at Two o'Clock on a Sunday, a Head at the Front of That Volume Saying the Refusals Are on It Too and Are Not Smaller Than the Rest, a Paper at the Top Left of That Door Which Is Not the Notice of the Seventeenth of May and Which Has Been There Three Days, and a Count of Six and a Count of Five Which Added Together Is Eleven*

Friday the third day of September, in that shed, at the top of Fish Street, and on that mole head.

**The said Friday was not a wage Tuesday and was not a rota day and was not a wipe, and the wall behind the reeve's bench came down and went up whole at the angle it has been at since the twelfth and nobody put it back. Nothing came into that yard on the said day and nothing went out of it, and there are two hundred and eighty-four pence in the bag, being one mark ten shillings and fourpence, and the figure that bag is short of the wage of four marks is three hundred and fifty-six pence, being two marks three shillings, and it is not going to be paid and nobody is to write one word under it saying anybody is going to. Nothing has been in a tin in that shed since the twenty-eighth day of April past, and there is a basin on the bench stone in that shed that has not been emptied since the Thursday before, and a man of about twenty-three was on the flat of the bench stone at about half past three that morning because he cannot sleep, and nobody in that harbor was on that stone with him and he was not asked for that day by anybody and did not offer it.**

**And the far bench was occupied on the said Friday from about ten in the morning until about four in the afternoon and was not approached on it, and the holder of that writ did not go over to him, and nothing was put on that bench and read as a gift, and neither brother has said why. Mara Venn was in that harbor and going nowhere, of no office, not on the crew, not restored, not forgiven, forty marks first after food, neither reduced nor paid, and she carried a crate of mends up the slip at about eleven in the morning for a boat on the second lay, and set it down outside a door at the top of Fish Street, and did not stop at that shed. Tobias Wren was on the crown of that mole from ten until four, being sixty-one, with eleven pieces of a boat behind him and a boat's hook in his right hand, and on the said day he got the seventh piece out of the wind and squared it against the parapet with his boot and went back to the fifth, and the eleven pieces were not moved as a heap and he did not look at the two objects of nobody's. The notice of the seventeenth of May past is on its wall in a different street for one hundred and nine days and has not been touched and has not been taken down. The bellwright of that harbor has not been asked for one hundred and eleven days, the chest is shut, the eight marks and a day of a boat have not been argued The three nots standing against the eighth of June past are a tenth week in which nobody in that crew has said one word about them, and the hand of a boy of twenty-two is still at the head of the third column with a name beside it that has not been said out loud since the thirteenth of May past, and that name is not on any page of this harbor., and no digit of anything he ever said is on a page. The two objects of nobody's on that crown are four hundred and forty-nine days old and neither has moved. The bench under that pump is bare for the two hundred and fifteenth night. Two lights at about a mile off that point have not moved in thirty-three days. A man of about fifty-one has said nothing to anybody about a page of six names for fifty-one days and has been given nothing by anybody since the thirtieth of July. A nine-line sheet with a seal on it has been in that front street for twenty days and has been answered by nobody.**

---

The room is over a joiner's shop at the top of Fish Street, and it has a long table in it that the joiner uses on a week day to glue battens along, and a bench at each side of that table, and a lamp on a nail, and no board on the wall and no list and no name over the door. It has been let out on a Sunday since before anybody in that harbor can remember being asked, and the man who lets it out is a man of about thirty-four called Tom Barlas, who has a shutter business two doors down and who carries a bowl down to the step at two o'clock on a Sunday and takes it in again at six and does not ask who is going in.

The book is on that table.

It is bound in a canvas that has been re-backed twice and it is about two inches thick and it opens flat, and the pages on the left-hand side carry the things and the pages on the right-hand side carry the heads, and the entries are ruled off from one another with a single line, and there are thirty of them in it on this day, and the first of them was entered on the twentieth of April last.

There was nobody in that room at eleven o'clock on the Friday morning but a man of about forty-six who is called Hobb Lee and who mends nets on the third lay and has mended them for nineteen years and can read.

"A joiner keeps his battens along a table six days a week," he said. "On a Sunday he goes to his sister's and this room is empty from six in the morning. It has been empty on a Sunday for longer than I have been on this reach, and Tom Barlas lets it and takes the money in a bowl and asks nobody anything, which is the correct way to let a room. He does not write down who came. If he wrote down who came he would be keeping a list, and a list in a room that is let out is a list of whoever could not afford not to come."

"Whose book is it."

"It is nobody's." He turned the cover back so that the front of it faced up on the table. "It was begun on the twentieth of April by four men in this room after a thing that came in off the third lay, and it has been added to every week since by whoever had something to add, and there is no name at the front of it because a book with a name at the front of it is a man's book and this is not a man's. It is a book of what this harbor holds. That is all it is and it decides nothing and it binds nobody and anybody can stand at that table on a Sunday and read it and nobody can stop them and nothing in it is worth a penny to anybody."

Calder Marr read the head of it. It took him a while because it is five lines and because the second line has been rubbed at the end by about forty years of an elbow on a table.

*This is what this harbor holds. Under the head of each thing, four lines. What the thing is. Where it came from. Who stands where it is. What it does not show.*

*The refusals are on it too and they are not smaller than the rest.*

*Nothing entered in that book is a decision and nothing entered in it binds anybody. Where a man has said no, it is written as he said it, and the reason goes in against his name, and the name of a man who has said no is put in as plainly as the name of a man who has said yes.*

*Head-notes are read out in this room on a Sunday. The name of the man who reads them and the hour go under the head-note in his own hand, on the day, and nothing is read out of that book on a Monday.*

Mara Venn, who had come up the stairs behind him with the crate on her shoulder and had put it down by the door, read the second line twice.

"It is on the second line and it is rubbed at the end," she said. "Somebody has had their arm on that page for a very long time."

"Somebody has," said Hobb Lee. "It was rubbed in the first year. It was put in the first year because of a thing that happened in the first year, and I am not going to tell you what, because the first year is not this year and it is not my telling. But I will tell you that the rule is on the page and that the page is where a rule goes when a rule is going to be held to."

"And do the refusals get the same four lines."

"They get the same four lines or the page says in its own words why they do not." He turned back to the front. "There is one in there now, entered on the ninth of June, of a man of about forty-four on the second lay who would not have either of his boats entered at all, and it has four lines under the head like everything else and his name on it, and the fourth of those four lines is the whole of his reason and it is eleven words long. He is not in this room and he has not asked to be, and he was not asked again, and if you come in on a Sunday you will hear that entry read out in this room in front of about twenty people, and every one of them will hear a man who has said no read out at the same size as a man who has said yes, and that is the only thing on that page that anybody in this harbor is going to be told about, and it is the only thing on that page that matters."

Nessa Pike came up at about half past two with a slate and asked the two questions she always asked, which were what it was going to cost and who was going to do it, and Calder Marr told her that the room was let out on a Sunday by a man with a shutter business two doors down and the reading was to be done by a man who mends nets, and she wrote the two answers on the slate and put the slate on the bench stone on the way out and did not argue with either of them.

---

At about four o'clock in the afternoon Calder Marr went up the stairs again by himself and read the volume through from the first page to the last, which took him until about six, because there are thirty heads and four lines under each of them and he is not a quick reader and he stopped at a good many of them.

Twenty-two of them were pieces of glass out of wrecks, and each of those four lines named the wreck, the day it came in, the day it was landed, and the shelf it is on, and the fourth line under every one of the twenty-two said the same shape of sentence in a slightly different hand, being that the thing shows nothing of what was in the water before it came in, and that a person who wants to know that is asking a different question and should go and ask it of the water.

Three of them were a lamp, a door plate and a box of tally sticks. One of them was a whole bell that came out of a hull on the second lay on the nineteenth of May past and is on a shelf in a room over that fish market with a man paid a shilling a week to wipe it, and that is the only item in that volume with a wage against it. The other four were two doors off a house on the front street, a barometer off a chandler's counter, a hatch cover off a fishing boat and a looking-glass off a lodging house on the second lay.

One of them was a panel out of a hull landed at the third lay on the fourth of June last, and Calder Marr read its four lines twice and put his thumb on the third one and left it there for a while.

*What it is: a panel of worked glass, about eleven inches by eight, out of a hull.
Where it came from: the fourth day of June, landed at the third lay, with a note on it in a man's hand.
Who stands where it is: the store on the second lay, shelf four. No keeper is paid for that store.
What it does not show: —*

There was nothing after the last dash. He looked at the six entries above it and the five below it and every one of those eleven had a line under the third and the lines were not the same words twice, and then he shut the volume and put it down on the table and left it, and went down the stairs, and did not say anything about it to anybody on the way down, and did not write anything about it that night.

---

At about half past six in the evening the man who stacks empties at the fish quay, who is called Joel Harrow and is about twenty-six, came up those stairs with his hands in his pockets, and Hobb Lee let him stand there and look at the open volume, and neither of them said one word about it for about ten minutes.

"I counted six yesterday," said the woman of about fifty-eight at the end stall, before either of them had said anything at all. "Six in my own trade that I know the hand of and know cannot read a word. Four of them have been in this market eleven years and two of them came in this summer."

"And I counted five," said Joel Harrow. "Five on the quay, at the weighing, where a man has to sign a slate and half of them make a mark and a mark is not a name. I counted them in July and again on Monday and it is five both times and they are the same five."

"Six and five."

"Six and five is eleven," said Hobb Lee, "and the eleven is a number and not a list, and the six of yours and the five of his are the same eleven and none of them has been told a thing about that volume, and neither of you is going to send for one of them, and the way the eleven of them will find out what is in that book is that somebody will stand in that room on a Sunday and read it out loud."

"Who reads it out loud."

"I do," said Hobb Lee. "On a Sunday, at two o'clock, off that table, and I will write my name and the hour under each head as I read it, in my own hand, on the day. I mend nets. I have been in this harbor since I was nine and I have never written a word in my life that anybody could put a name against except the ones I write under a head, and that is a thing I am going to start on Sunday and I am not going to be thanked for it and I am not going to be paid for it, and a man of forty-six does not charge a room for reading to a man who cannot read."

Joel Harrow said that was a great deal of sense and went down the stairs, and Hobb Lee wiped the glue marks off the long table with his sleeve, which took a while, because the table is a joiner's table and the glue is a week deep in the grain.

---

There was a paper at the top left of that door, and it had been there three days.

Calder Marr had gone past that door forty times in his life without looking up at it, and he looked up at it on that Friday at about seven in the evening on his way out, and stopped, and came back up four steps and read it.

It was a strip of good paper, about the length of a forearm, and a drawing-pin was through the top left corner of it and a strip of oiled paper had been folded over the pin and stuck, so that the pin could not be seen and could not be taken out without tearing the paper or taking the fold with it.

It had four lines on it and no heading and no seal and nothing at the foot of it.

*A lamp of about four inches was found burning in the above room on the night of the nineteenth of August last at eleven o'clock, and there was nothing in that room with it.*
*On the days following, eleven people of this harbor were asked one at a time whether they had been in that room on that night. One of the eleven was in that room on that night.*
*The person who found the lamp will not say which one of the eleven it was, and has been asked by two persons of this street to say, and has not said.*
*Anybody found to have been in that room on that night is to take himself, or herself, off out of this harbor before the twentieth day of September next.*

Calder Marr read it twice and went down the stairs and out into the street and stood in the middle of Fish Street for a while in the dark, and the light on that mole head was out and has been out since the seventh of May past and the lens has been out of it since then.

"Fish Street," he said, to nobody. "Not the wall in the other street. A different street, a different paper, a different door, and a different harbor's business, and the two of them are two of them and neither of them cancels the other and I am not going to be asked to choose between them."

Then he went down to that shed and put the thing on the bench stone and said out loud, to an empty room, in a working man's flat voice, as a man writes a figure down and not as a man makes a speech:

"That is a door with a paper on it and a date on the paper and eleven names behind the paper that nobody has got, and the twenty-seventh clause of it is a date nobody in that harbor has agreed to, and it is the twentieth day of September next, and it is seventeen days off."

He wrote it in the first column with the hour against it, and he wrote the four lines of the paper in his own words underneath, because a stranger with a pencil ought to be able to take those four lines off a piece of paper in a room over a joiner's shop and go and check them, and there is nobody in that harbor he can ask.

**Nothing came into that yard on the said Friday and nothing went out of it, and the bag is one mark ten shillings and fourpence and did not move on that day, and it is short of that wage by two marks three shillings. A bound volume was read through from the first page to the last by four people in a room over a joiner's shop at the top of Fish Street, and the head of it was read out, and the second line of the head of it has been rubbed at the end for about forty years. A man of about forty-six called Hobb Lee, who mends nets on the third lay and has done since he was nine, is going to read every head-note in that volume out loud at that table on a Sunday at two o'clock and to write his own name and the hour under each one in his own hand on the day. Eleven people in that harbor cannot read and the count is a count of people and not a figure of money and not a list of names, and it was arrived at by two people counting separately, being six by a woman of about fifty-eight in her own trade and five by a man of about twenty-six on a quay, and six and five is eleven, and neither of those two has sent for one of them. One of the thirty heads in that volume carries three lines under it where the other twenty-nine carry four, and its third line ends in a dash, and the holder of that writ read that head twice and put his thumb on it and left it and said nothing about it to anybody. A paper with four lines on it and a drawing-pin under a fold of oiled paper has been at the top left of that door for two days and it is not the notice of the seventeenth of May past. The far bench was occupied from ten until four and was not approached, and neither brother went near the other at any hour of the said day.**

*First column, entered on Friday the third day of September next at about half past nine in the evening in that shed: that a bound volume of thirty heads has been kept on a long table in a room over a joiner's shop at the top of Fish Street since the twentieth of April past, and that on the said Friday four people read it through from the first page to the last, being the holder of that writ, a woman of no office, a working man of about forty-six who mends nets on the third lay, and, at about half past six in the evening, a man of about twenty-six who stacks empties at a fish quay; and that the head of that volume carries four lines to be filled under every head, being what the thing is, where it came from, who stands where it is, and what it does not show, and carries in addition that the refusals are on it too and are not smaller than the rest, and that nothing entered in it is a decision and nothing entered in it binds anybody, and that where a man has said no it is written as he said it with the reason against his name; and that the second line of that head has been rubbed at the end for about forty years of an elbow on a table; and that the man of about forty-six read the head of it out loud in that room on the said Friday, and that a man of about thirty-four who lets that room out on a Sunday carries a bowl down to the step at two o'clock and takes it in at six and does not ask who is going in and does not write down who came; and that a count of eleven people of this harbor who cannot read was arrived at on the said Friday by two people counting separately and adding, being six counted by a woman of about fifty-eight in her own trade on the Thursday and five counted by a man of about twenty-six on the quay on the Monday, and that six and five is eleven, and that that eleven is a count of people and not a list of names and that neither of those two counted for anybody; and that of the thirty heads in that volume on the said Friday twenty-nine carry four lines under the head and one carries three, and that the head of that volume says four, and that the third line of the one that carries three ends in a dash; and that that head is of a panel of worked glass about eleven inches by eight out of a hull landed at the third lay on the fourth day of June last with a note on it in a man's hand, and that it stands on shelf four in a store on the second lay where no keeper is paid; and that the holder of that writ read that head twice, put his thumb on the third line, left the volume shut on the table and came down the stairs and wrote nothing about it that night; and that a paper with four lines on it and a drawing-pin under a fold of oiled paper has been at the top left of the door of that room for three days, and the four lines are printed in full in the body of this entry, and the last of the four is that anybody found to have been in that room on the night of the nineteenth of August last is to take himself, or herself, off out of this harbor before the twentieth day of September next.*

*Second column, not checkable from a mole, entered on the said Friday at about half past nine in the evening in that shed, and no name is against it: that the head of that volume was rubbed at the end of its second line in the first year, and that the reason for the rubbing is in the first year and not in this ledger and that the man of about forty-six who keeps that room declined to give it and gave his reason, being that the first year is not this year and is not his telling; and that a working man who reads a head-note out loud to people who cannot read is doing a work and not holding an office, and that his name and the hour go under the head in his own hand on the day because a person who cannot read cannot afterwards prove what was said to him; and that the eleven who cannot read are not a constituency and are not named and are not on a sheet in that room, and that the way they will find out what is in that volume is that a man stands in that room and reads it; and that a man of about fifty-eight at an end stall counted six in her own trade and a man of about twenty-six on a quay counted five and added the two figures, and that on the said day the six in her own trade had not been told a thing about that volume and the five on the quay had not been told a thing about that volume, and that neither of those two counted a person in order to be found, and that a week stood between the said Friday and the Sunday on which that room was let out next; and that the holder of that writ read one head of that volume twice on the said Friday and put his thumb on a third line and left the volume shut on a joiner's table and said nothing about it to anybody, and that a man who has found a thing and has not said it is not the same thing as a man who has found a thing and has not seen it, and that which of those two it is on the said Friday is not entered here and is not going to be asked about; and that the paper at the top left of that door names no person and no harbor and puts no figure on it and is true enough on its face that a man standing under it cannot go up the street and say it is a lie, and that the last of its four lines is a date that eleven days off the said day, and that a date on a piece of paper is a thing a man can read without a pencil and there are eleven people in that harbor who cannot do that; and that the notice of the seventeenth of May past is on its own wall in a different street for one hundred and nine days and is a different paper on a different door and the two are not one thing and neither of them cancels the other; and that the standing list entered in full on the fourteenth of August past is not entered in full on the said Friday and stands, and no part of it was paid, reduced, advanced or improved on that day.*
