# Chapter 431

*Thursday the Twenty-Third Day of September, the Eighty-Fourth Time the Cistern Was Charged at About Eight in the Morning and Walked on by None of It at About Half Past Nine on a Rota of a Monday and a Thursday, a Boat of About Nineteen Tons Named the Alewife Taken Up the Foul Off the South End in the Night With Nobody in Her and Lying With Her Port Bilge Up in About Five Foot of Water, Nine Men Unstepping and Landing Her Mast in About Fifty Minutes Before the Flood at About Half Past Six in the Evening, One Hundred and Sixty-Eight Pence Going Out of That Yard at About Ten in the Morning for Oakum and Iron and a Length of Three-Inch Stuff, and a Day's Work With a Boat and Two Men at a Berth Coming In at Four in the Afternoon, and the Bag at Five Hundred and Thirty-Two Pence at the Close of the Day as It Was at the Open of It*

Thursday the twenty-third day of September, on the foul off the south end of that mole, at the head of the third lay, in that shed, and on that mole head.

**The said Thursday was a rota day and was not a wage Tuesday and was not a wipe, and the cistern at the back of that market was charged at about eight in the morning and walked on by none of it at about half past nine, being the eighty-fourth time, and the top name on that rota board is still off it. One hundred and sixty-eight pence went out of that yard at about ten in the morning for oakum and iron and a length of three-inch stuff and canvas and nails, and one hundred and sixty-eight pence came back in at about four in the afternoon for a day's work with a boat and two men at a berth at the figure in chalk on a slate at an end stall, and there are five hundred and thirty-two pence in the bag at the close of the said day, being three marks four shillings and fourpence, which is the figure there was in it at the open of it, and the figure that bag is short of the wage of four marks is one hundred and eight pence, being nine shillings, and it is not going to be paid and nobody is to write one word under it saying anybody is going to.**

**And the far bench was occupied on the said Thursday from about ten in the morning until about four in the afternoon and was not approached on it, and the holder of that writ did not go over to him, and nothing was put on that bench and read as a gift, and neither brother has said why. Mara Venn was in that harbor and going nowhere, of no office, not on the crew, not restored, not forgiven, forty marks first after food, neither reduced nor paid, and she was in that shed from about nine o'clock and priced nothing. Tobias Wren was on the crown of that mole from ten until four, being sixty-one, with eleven pieces of a boat behind him and a boat's hook in his right hand, and on the said day he stood the tenth piece up on its edge and looked at it and left it there. The notice of the seventeenth of May past is on its wall in a different street for one hundred and twenty-nine days and has not been touched. The bellwright of that harbor has not been asked for one hundred and thirty-one days, the chest is shut, and the eight marks and a day of a boat have not been argued, and no digit of anything he ever said is on a page. The three nots standing against the eighth of June past are an eleventh week in which nobody in that crew has said one word about them, and the hand of a boy of twenty-two is still at the head of the third column with a name beside it that has not been said out loud since the thirteenth of May past, and that name is not on any page of this harbor. The two objects of nobody's on that crown are four hundred and sixty-nine days old and neither has moved. The bench under that pump is bare for the two hundred and thirty-fifth night. Two lights at about a mile off that point have not moved in fifty-three days, counted from the hour they were first seen on the evening of the first of August past. A man of about fifty-one has said nothing to anybody about a page of six names for seventy-one days. A sheet of six lines at the back of an end stall is on that wall for the eighth day. The paper of four lines at the top left of that door in Fish Street is on that door for the twenty-third day. A nine-line sheet with a seal on it is in that front street for forty days.**

---

Jos Lund found her at about twenty past five in the morning, in the dark, from the top of the mole, by the smell of her.

She is the *Alewife*, of the third lay, of about nineteen tons, and she was lying at the head of that slip with her stern to the north and a warp round a bollard that had been put on in March. He said the warp parted at about two o'clock and that he was in his bed and that he heard her go.

"She went up the foul on the ebb," he said. "There is nothing up there to stop a thing going up the foul. She has gone about a hundred and forty yards and she is lying with her port bilge up."

Nessa Pike wrote the time against it on her own slate before he had finished the sentence.

---

They went down in a punt at about half past six with a lamp and two men on the oar and left the boat's hook standing on the mole stones behind them.

She was where he had said she was, and she was further over than anybody could have told from the top. Five foot of water on her port bilge and eleven foot off her port rail, and the rail itself was under, and her starboard side was out of the water as high as her garboard strake, and the seam of her garboard was open about two foot forward of the keel with the sea going in and out of it in a slow way that made a sound like a man drawing air through his teeth.

Her mast was up. It was up because nobody had been aboard her since the Monday and she was not going to be sailed on the Thursday.

"There is the flood at about half past six this evening," said Jos Lund. "It will be four foot more than it is now, and she will lift about a hand's breadth and set her beam ends, and her port rail will be against this mole's footing stones, and those stones are not laid on sand. They are laid on the rock. She will work that seam open against them and by the middle of the night there will be a hole in her you can put an oar through, and by Friday morning she will be in three pieces on the south end of this mole and every one of the five men ashore will be out of work until the spring."

He put his hand flat on her starboard rail and it was wet to the elbow.

"Nine men," he said. "That is what I have got. Two of them are my own and three are whatever comes, and there is a boy of fifteen here on the pump who is my nephew and is worth about half of one."

---

They got the chain off her first, because the chain was the thing that would do the damage and it took eleven minutes.

Then they took her mast out.

It is a boat of nineteen tons and the mast is thirty-one feet and there were nine men on that deck, four of them of that yard's hands and three of the crew of a boat lying at the mole head and two men off the second lay who came down the slip on the word, and it took from about a quarter to seven until about twenty past seven, being about fifty minutes, and it came down across her starboard rail and over onto the mole stones with a baulk under it and the whole nine hundredweight of it went onto the stones and split a piece out of the coping at the corner where the mole steps come up.

That was the first thing that was broken that day and it was broken by nine men who were not paid for that morning.

The wheel came out and went into a boat. Both anchors were let go and left where they were, in nine foot, on a foul that a harbor with nineteen winters in a list in another city has not had a good survey of since before any of them was born.

Then Calder Marr went over her starboard rail with an oakum hammer and a short iron and a piece of canvas and packed the open seam from the inside, which is the only side a man can get at while she is lying over like that, and drove it home as far as his arm would go and stopped where his arm stopped.

Nessa Pike was on the stones above him the whole of it with the slate against her knee.

"How long does that hold?" she asked.

"Until the flood," he said, from inside the boat.

"That is not a figure I can put on that slate," she said. "You will have to say it out loud in the same words in a week if it is still true."

---

The flood came at about twenty past six, not half past, and that made it worse and not better.

She lifted a hand's breadth and she set her beam ends in about four minutes, and then her port rail went against the mole's footing stones and stayed against them, grinding, for about an hour and ten minutes, in about three feet of water with a falling swell pushing at her, and everybody on that mole head could hear her working and nobody could get a hand to her.

At about half past seven she came off the stones.

She went down the foul fast, with her mast lying across her starboard side and her rail out of the water and nobody in her, and about four hundred yards out she took the ground on a bank and stopped, and the noise of her stopping went along that mole head and off the head of that mole and out over the outer marks.

She was lying in about nine foot of water with her port bilge up and her garboard open about two foot forward of the keel.

---

Nobody in that harbor was paid for that morning.

That yard's own figures carry a day's work with a boat and two men at a berth and a caulker's day, and one mark and eightpence is the figure for each of them, and a Thursday morning with nine men on a deck and a boy on a pump and a woman on the stones with a slate is neither of those things and there is no figure in that harbor for it. The length of three-inch stuff and the iron and the oakum and the canvas and the nails came out of that bag at about ten in the morning at the figure of a caulker's day and were not a caulker's day, and the woman of fifty-eight with the end stall did not ask what they were for and was not asked and put the figure on her own slate against a day's work with a boat and two men and it did not matter what it was for.

Nessa Pike stood on the stones at about eleven and looked at her own slate for a while.

"I have got two figures in this harbor and one of them is what a boat earns in a day and one of them is what a man with an oakum hammer earns in a day," she said. "And I have got nothing at all for a man in the water at four in the morning, and I am not going to make one up, and if I make one up it will be a lie in about a fortnight and it will be the kind of lie that people quote."

"The flood is at about twenty past six in the evening," said the holder of that writ, "and the ebb at about a quarter to four in the morning, and she is in nine foot and there is a foot of water over her keel at the bottom of that ebb and about two foot at the top of the flood."

"So write it down and do not dress it up."

"That is a foot and about two hours of it in every twenty-four, and a company of boats cannot be on that bank at a quarter to four in the morning every night of a week, and I have not got anybody in this harbor who can be in that water every tide of every week, and there is not one man in this yard who can stand in a boat all night and then go out again."

"You have got a woman who prices things," said Nessa Pike, "and you have told me a foot and two hours and you have told me that a company of boats is a thing that wants a person on it. The person is the thing that costs. And this yard's book has no column for a person and no figure for one, and I am not going to put a figure against a person."

She shut the slate.

"Then somebody has to be paid to be there," she said, "and the only way I know to pay a man to be there is to have a day for him, and the day costs what a day costs, and you are going to have to write that on a piece of paper and ask one man at a time whether he will come and stand in it."

---

There is no flour in that market and there has been none since the spring and none is coming this month. The mole-head light is not lit and its lens has been out since the seventh of May past and it is the kind of dark on the twenty-third of September on that coast that a man cannot see the top of his own mast in and that everybody in that harbor talks about every year and nobody has a light for. The sheet of six lines at the back of that end stall is on that wall for the eighth day on the outside of a slate carrying four hundred and fifty pence with the first of June against it, and that figure was not taken down first and cannot be taken off again. The paper of four lines in Fish Street is on that door for the twenty-third day and its fourth line ended on the twentieth and has been answered by nobody. The nine-line sheet with a seal on it is in that front street for forty days and is a different paper from the six-line sheet and is on a different wall in a different street. The wage column is at twenty-six lines and one hundred and four marks with twenty envelopes in a chest in a front room and forty-eight weeks stand fallen due with no envelope against any of them, and the forty-ninth falls on the Tuesday after this day.

---

**A boat of about nineteen tons of the third lay named the *Alewife*, with a warp round a bollard that had been on her since March, went up the foul off the south end of that mole in the night of the twenty-second and lay in about five foot of water with her port bilge up, and nine men unstepped and landed her mast in about fifty minutes before the flood and split a piece out of the mole coping at the corner where the steps come up, and her wheel was shipped out and both her anchors were let go and left in nine foot on a foul nobody has surveyed, and her garboard was packed from the inside with oakum and a canvas patch by the holder of that writ to about eleven foot, and the woman of that yard who prices things asked how long it would hold and was told until the flood. The flood came at about twenty past six in the evening and set her beam ends and put her port rail against that mole's footing stones for about an hour and ten minutes and she came off at about half past seven and took the ground on a bank about four hundred yards down the foul in about nine foot of water with her port bilge up and her mast lying across her starboard side and her garboard open about two foot forward of the keel, and nobody was paid for that morning, and one hundred and sixty-eight pence of material went out of that yard at about ten in the morning and one hundred and sixty-eight pence of a day's work at a berth came in at about four in the afternoon, and the bag stands at five hundred and thirty-two pence, being three marks four shillings and fourpence, where it stood at the open of the day. A woman who prices things told the holder of that writ in the open at about eleven o'clock on that mole that she had two figures in that harbor and had nothing at all for a man in the water at four in the morning, and would not make one up, and told him that the person is the thing that costs and that a day for a person costs what a day costs and has to be written on a piece of paper and asked one man at a time.**

*First column, entered on Thursday the twenty-third day of September next at about half past nine in the evening in that shed: that a boat of about nineteen tons of the third lay named the Alewife, lying at the head of that slip with a warp round a bollard that had been on her since March, went up the foul off the south end of that mole in the night of the twenty-second at about two o'clock with nobody in her, and lay in about five foot of water with her port bilge up, eleven foot off her port rail and her starboard side out of the water as high as her garboard strake, with her garboard open about two foot forward of the keel; and that nine men unstepped and landed her mast from about a quarter to seven until about twenty past seven, being about fifty minutes, and landed it across her starboard rail onto that mole's stones with a baulk under it, and split a piece out of the coping at the corner where those steps come up; and that her chain came off in about eleven minutes and her wheel was shipped out and both her anchors were let go and left where they were in nine foot on a foul with no survey against it; and that the holder of that writ packed the open garboard from the inside at about eleven foot with oakum and a canvas patch and said out loud that it would hold until the flood; and that the flood came at about twenty past six in the evening and set her beam ends in about four minutes and put her port rail against that mole's footing stones for about an hour and ten minutes and she came off them at about half past seven and took the ground on a bank about four hundred yards down that foul in about nine foot of water with her port bilge up and her mast lying across her starboard side; and that five men of that crew were ashore at about half past five in the morning and a boy of fifteen was on the pump; and that one hundred and sixty-eight pence went out of that yard at about ten in the morning for oakum and iron and a length of three-inch stuff and canvas and nails, and one hundred and sixty-eight pence came back in at about four in the afternoon for a day's work with a boat and two men at a berth at the figure in chalk on a slate at an end stall, and that the bag stands at five hundred and thirty-two pence at the close of the said day, being three marks four shillings and fourpence, and five hundred and thirty-two plus one hundred and sixty-eight minus one hundred and sixty-eight is five hundred and thirty-two; and that the cistern was charged at about eight in the morning and walked on by none of it at about half past nine, being the eighty-fourth time, and the top name on that rota board is still off it; and that the said day was not a wage Tuesday and was not a wipe; and that of the ten days of this stretch the twenty-third, the twenty-seventh and the thirtieth are the only three on which a charge is placed on that cistern and that seven days of the ten put none.*

*Second column, not checkable from a mole, entered on the said Thursday at about half past nine in the evening in that shed, and no name is against it: that the reason a boat of about nineteen tons went up that foul at about two o'clock in the morning is that a warp round a bollard parted and that she had been in nobody's hand since about nine o'clock on the Monday, and that this yard did not put her there and did not leave her there and has asked her master nothing about it; and that the reason nine men were on that deck from about a quarter to seven is that a mast up is a lever and that the flood was four foot more than the water she was lying in, and that nine men were on her for about fifty minutes and none of the nine was paid for it, because a morning on a wreck is not a day's work at a berth and is not a caulker's day and this harbor has no figure for a morning on a wreck; and that the reason the packing of that garboard was done from the inside is that the outside was under five foot of water with her over the way she was lying, and that the woman who prices things asked how long it would hold and was told until the flood, in those words, in her hearing, at about eleven o'clock; and that the reason no figure is entered against nine men and fifty minutes and a piece of coping is that a figure against it would be this yard putting a price on five men's morning and on a mole's coping out of a woman's slate, and that a coping is mended by a mason in his own time and this yard is not a mason; and that the reason that mast came down across that mole's rail onto the stones and not onto a cart is in her master's own mouth and is that there was no cart on that mole at half past six in the morning and that a baulk of oak under nine hundredweight of it is what is under it now; and that the reason a piece of that coping was not mended on the said day is that a coping is mended by a mason in his own time and that this yard is not a mason and has not pretended to be one since May; and that the reason the man who went into that boat to pack the seam was not paid for it is that the yard's own figures carry a day's work with a boat and two men and a caulker's day and one mark and eightpence for each, and that neither of those two things is what he did, and that the length of three-inch stuff and the iron and the oakum and the canvas and the nails were bought at the caulker's figure and are not a caulker's day; and that the reason no name is against any of it is that this yard has got no figure for a morning on a wreck and is not going to invent one, and that the standing list entered in full on the fourteenth of August past is not entered in full on the said Thursday and stands, and no part of it was paid, reduced, advanced or improved on that day.*
