# Chapter 404

*Friday the Twenty-Seventh of August, a Caulker of About Twenty-Three Asked for by Name on Wednesday and Paid in Coin at About Four in the Afternoon on the Flat of the Bench Stone at One Hundred and Sixty-Eight Pence Out of That Yard's Own Figures, a Man of About Thirty-Eight With a Boat of About Thirteen Tons and No Papers Out All Day on the Sixth Clause Again and a Loaf and a Half and a Candle and a Pot at Forty-Four Pence, the Second Block of a Return Counted on the Flat of a Hand on the Same Day, and the Third Day of a Refusal on a Door in a Corridor on That Hill*

Friday the twenty-seventh of August, in that shed, at the office on the west basin, and on the flat of the bench stone at that mole head.

**The said Friday was not a wage Tuesday and was not a rota day and was not a wipe, and the wall behind the reeve's bench came down and went up whole at the angle it has been at since the twelfth and nobody put it back. One hundred and sixty-eight pence came into that yard on the said day, being a day's work with a boat and two men at a berth at the figure in chalk on a slate at an end stall, and one hundred and sixty-eight pence went out of it in coin on the flat of the bench stone at about four in the afternoon to a caulker of about twenty-three, at that same figure, out of that yard's own money, and forty-four pence, being three shillings and eightpence, went out of it at about half past ten in the morning for a loaf and a half and a candle and a pot, and there are two hundred and eighty-four pence in the bag, being one mark ten shillings and fourpence, and the figure that bag is short of the wage of four marks is three hundred and fifty-six pence, being two marks three shillings, and it is not going to be paid and nobody is to write one word under it saying anybody is going to. Nothing has been in a tin in that shed since the twenty-eighth day of April past, and not one penny of the whole of the said day came out of the owner of any boat, out of the man with two papers, or out of the Crown.**

**And the far bench was occupied on the said Friday from about ten in the morning until about four in the afternoon and was not approached on it, and the holder of that writ did not go over to him, and nothing was put on that bench and read as a gift, and neither brother has said why. Mara Venn was in that harbor and going nowhere, of no office, not on the crew, not restored, not forgiven, forty marks first after food, neither reduced nor paid, and she was at the head of the second lay at about ten in the morning and did not go up that hill. Tobias Wren was on the crown of that mole from ten until four, being sixty-one, with eleven pieces of a boat behind him and a boat's hook in his right hand, and he lay along the sixth piece with the hook under her and got her up about a foot and let her back down, and he was still at it at four and the tide was under the eleventh piece by then. The notice of the seventeenth of May past is on its wall in a different street for one hundred and two days and has not been touched. The bellwright of that harbor has not been asked for one hundred and four days, the chest is shut, the eight marks and a day of a boat have not been argued, and no digit of anything he ever said is on a page.**

---

He had asked him on the Wednesday, at about ten past five in the morning, in that shed, and he had used his name.

"Isaac Fell. Friday the twenty-seventh. One day. The other end of that garboard on the second lay, the one that has been open since June and that nobody wanted. One hundred and sixty-eight pence out of my own figures, in coin, on the flat of that stone at about four in the afternoon, and not out of the owner of anything."

Isaac Fell had a maul in his hand and he did not put it down.

"You have not asked me for that end since the twelfth of July," he said.

"No."

"And you are asking me on a Wednesday for a Friday."

"That is the way it works and it is the way you asked for it."

"I did not ask for anything," said Isaac Fell. "I told you in June that the day you do not ask is the day there is not one, and you wrote that down and you have not acted on it since and neither have I, and here we are on a Wednesday with a Friday on it." He turned the maul over. "I will do Friday. I will not do Saturday, and I have told you why and you have written it down, and I am not going to say it again to you or to anybody."

"Noted," said Calder, "and the Saturday is not in it and will not be in it."

The caulker was paid on the flat of the bench stone at about four in the afternoon on the Friday, in coin, out of that yard's own figures, at one hundred and sixty-eight pence, and he counted it on the stone with his own thumb and put it in his own pocket, and nobody thanked him.

"There is one thing I am going to say and then it is done," he said. "You asked me for the twenty-first of August and you paid me for it and it was a Saturday, and the Wednesday and the Thursday either side of it, the nineteenth and the twentieth, you did not ask me for, and I did those two days for nothing on my own account because I have nothing else to do on them, and I have not put those two days on anything."

"I know you have not."

"Then they are not in that bag either," said Isaac Fell, "and you will not put them in, and I am going back to my own bench now because there is a coal fire in this harbor for about four men in it and I am one of the four."

---

At about half past ten in the morning Calder gave a man of about thirty-eight forty-four pence out of the bag on the quay at the second lay, being three shillings and eightpence, for a loaf and a half and a candle and a pot, and the man took it and did not say anything and went out on the sixth clause at about half past ten and was not back until about six in the evening, and that was the second time in three days and the fourth time since the first of June, and it came out of that yard's own figures and not one penny of it out of the owner of a boat of about thirteen tons, because a boat of about thirteen tons with no papers on her has no owner standing at a counter paying for anything.

---

Peat Marrow was at that board on the Friday at about half past two with his hand over the small of his back, and he did the second block.

He did it slower than he did the first one, because on the first one he had not known what he was doing and on the second one he did. He counted the lines with his thumb and he counted them a second time without the thumb, and he counted the figures out loud in tens, and at the end of it he had twenty-eight lines with a figure on them out of the thirty-one days of that month, and the three strokes were the fourth, the nineteenth and the twenty-sixth, and the sum of the twenty-eight figures was ninety-six, and the small figure in the clerk's hand at the head of that block was twenty-eight, and it was right.

He did not write either number on his hand that day, because he had said on the Thursday that he would say it to the holder of that writ on a day, and this was not that day, and he had said Friday and Friday was the day he would do it and not the day he would say it, and he is a man who has been in that room enough times to know the difference between the two.

He put the pencil behind his ear and went out sideways and the clerk of about sixty, who had not looked at him at any hour of the said day and had not been asked anything by anybody in that harbor that year, went on with his list, which is a list and is not a schedule and binds nobody.

---

At about half past five Calder was in that shed with the leaf open on the bench stone and the fourth block of that sheet in his own head and the first two of it in a man's handwriting on a man's hand.

"Saturday," he said. "He will be there on Saturday for the third month and I have not asked him to and he has not told me he is coming, and I am not going to ask him, and I am not going to be standing in that office at half past two on a Saturday watching him do it."

"Then you will not get it on the Saturday," said Nessa.

"I will get it on the Wednesday, or the Monday, or whenever he decides to say it, and if he never says it then there is no figure and that is the end of it, and there is no version of this where I stand in that office and watch a man with a bad back do arithmetic at a wall for me."

"Put that down as the reason," said Nessa, "because that is the reason, and because the first column is for things a man on a mole can take with a pencil, and if I put a number in the first column on a Monday that a man counted on a Thursday in a room I was not in, then the first column is not a first column any more and it is a column of things somebody in this yard was told by somebody else, and that is the second column and it has always been the second column and it is not going to stop being the second column because we are impatient."

---

At about ten to six a woman of about fifty-eight came down the front street with her slate under her arm and stopped in the doorway of that shed and put the slate on the bench stone face up, which she does not do.

"There is a figure on that slate," she said, "in chalk, with the day of the month beside it, and I put it there on the twenty-fifth of July and it cannot be taken off it again and this yard did not put it there and cannot take it off it, and it is a mark and eightpence and it is what a day at that berth is, and it is on my slate and not on yours."

"I know what is on your slate."

"Then you know there is nothing on it about Friday and there is nothing on it about the twenty-seventh and there is not going to be." She put her thumb on the chalk. "And I will tell you the other half of it while I have got my hand on it, and then I am going to go home. Six of the men who came to my stall this morning asked me what it costs to be in a boat of yours for a day, and I told six of them the figure on this slate and I told them what it buys, and one of the six said he would not do it for that, and I said good, and he is right and I am not going to argue with him about it in my own market."

"Who was the one."

"He is fifty-one and he has a boat called the *Anna Ray* and he is owed nineteen winters and he was in a market in July and said that an open road is not a freedom, and he has not said a word to me since and he did not say a word to you."

"He is not asked for anything," Calder said, "and he is not going to be, and I have not got a figure for him and I am not going to get one."

"I know you have not," said the woman of about fifty-eight. "That is why I am telling you before somebody else does."

She picked the slate up and went out into a market that had about nine people left in it.

**One hundred and sixty-eight pence came into that yard on the said Friday and three hundred and thirty-six pence went out of it, being one hundred and sixty-eight pence in coin on the flat of the bench stone at about four in the afternoon to a caulker of about twenty-three who had been asked for that day by name on the Wednesday, at the same figure, out of that yard's own money, and forty-four pence, being three shillings and eightpence, for a loaf and a half and a candle and a pot for a man of about thirty-eight who was out all day on the sixth clause of a notice on his own account. The bag is one mark ten shillings and fourpence after a day that put three hundred and thirty-six pence out of it against a hundred and sixty-eight that came in, and it is short of that wage by two marks three shillings. A caulker said on that mole that the nineteenth and the twentieth of August were not in that bag and were not going in and were not on anything. A man of about fifty-three with a bad back counted the second month of a return at a wall in the office on the west basin and did not write either figure on his own hand, and said nothing to anybody in that room. A woman of about fifty-eight put a slate on a bench stone face up and said that six men asked her what it costs to be in a boat of that yard's for a day and that one of them said he would not do it for that and that she said good to him, and that the man who said it is fifty-one and has a boat called the *Anna Ray* of about fifty tons and is owed nineteen winters and is not going to be asked for anything. The far bench was occupied from ten until four and was not approached, and neither brother went near the other at any hour of the said day.**

*First column, entered on Friday the twenty-seventh day of August next at about half past nine in the evening in that shed: that the said Friday was not a rota day and not a wage Tuesday and not a wipe; and that one hundred and sixty-eight pence came into that yard, being a day's work with a boat and two men at a berth at the figure in chalk on a slate at an end stall; and that forty-four pence, being three shillings and eightpence, went out of that yard at about half past ten in the morning for a loaf and a half and a candle and a pot to a man of about thirty-eight with a boat of about thirteen tons and no papers on her, who went out on the sixth clause of a notice at about half past ten and was not back until about six in the evening, and that the fourth such time since the first of June was on that day; and that a caulker of about twenty-three was asked for the said Friday by name at about ten past five in the morning on the Wednesday, in that shed, out loud, being the other end of a garboard on the second lay that has been open since June and that nobody wanted, at one hundred and sixty-eight pence out of that yard's own figures in coin on the flat of the bench stone at about four in the afternoon; and that he was paid on the said day on the said stone and counted it there with his own thumb and put it in his own pocket and was not thanked; and that the said caulker said that the nineteenth and the twentieth days of August past were not paid and are not on anything and are not going in the bag, and that the holder wrote that down; and that a man of about fifty-three of that harbor counted the second block of a return in the office on the west basin at about half past two in the afternoon, and that twenty-eight of the thirty-one lines in that block carried a figure and three were struck across, and that the sum of the twenty-eight figures was ninety-six, and that the small figure in the clerk of about sixty's hand at the head of that block was twenty-eight and was right; and that the said man wrote neither figure on his own hand on that day because he had said on the Thursday that he would say it on a day and the Friday was the day he would do it and not the day he would say it, and that he set his own count of the twenty-eight lines against the small figure at the head of that block and that the small figure was right; and that a woman of about fifty-eight with an end stall put her slate on the flat of the bench stone in that shed face up at about ten to six in the evening and said that six of the men who came to her stall that morning asked her what it costs to be in a boat of that yard's for a day, and that she told all six the figure on her slate and what it buys, and that one of the six said he would not do it for that, and that she said good to him, and that the said man is fifty-one and has a boat called the *Anna Ray* of about fifty tons and is owed nineteen winters and was not asked for anything on the said day and is not going to be.*
*Second column, not checkable from a mole, entered on the said Friday at about half past nine in the evening in that shed, and no name is against it: that a day of a man that is asked for by name on a day and paid for at an hour on a stone is a day and a wage and an hour and on nothing else, and is not a name on a board and is not a place on a list, and not one penny of either day came out of the owner of any boat or out of the man who wants the thing looked at; and that two days of that caulker done on his own account in the same month at no figure are not owed to this yard and are not going to be asked for and are not going to be paid and are not going in any column of anything; and that the reason against the counting line is that the holder is not going to stand in that office and watch a man with a bad back do arithmetic at a wall, and that a number that a man on a mole cannot take with a pencil out of a room he was not in is not a first-column figure, and that the first column is for things a stranger can check and the second column is for things a stranger cannot, and that they are not going to change their jobs because nine people in this harbor are waiting for a number; and that a woman with a slate who tells six men what a day costs and says good to the seventh is doing her own trade in her own market and has told this yard in advance so that it could not be said afterwards that it was sprung on her; and that a man of fifty-one with a boat called the *Anna Ray* of about fifty tons and nineteen winters against him saying no to a mark and eightpence in his own market is a man saying no to a price, and he has a boat and a market and a slate-woman and nothing else that has been asked of him this month, and there is no name against him in this yard's leaf and there is no figure against him anywhere; and that the standing list of the fourteenth of August past is not re-entered in full on the said Friday and stands whole, and that a man of fifty-one saying no to a mark and eightpence in his own market is a man saying no to a price and is not on that list and has nothing against him in it, and that no part of the list moved on that day.*
