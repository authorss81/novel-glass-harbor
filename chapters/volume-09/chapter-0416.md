# Chapter 416

*Wednesday the Eighth Day of September, a Man of About Forty-Six Walking Round That Harbor on His Own Two Feet in Daylight and Talking to Eleven People One at a Time in Their Own Doors and Not Showing Any of Them the Four Lines and Not Reading Them and Saying Out Loud That He Was Not Going To, Forty-Four Pence Going Out of That Yard at About Half Past Ten in the Morning for a Loaf and a Half and a Candle and a Pot, a Man of About Thirty-Eight With a Boat of About Thirteen Tons and No Papers Out All Day on the Sixth Clause of a Notice in a Different Street, and a Man of About Forty-One Coming Up a Stair at Four o'Clock in the Afternoon to Ask Whether Anybody Had Answered a Paper He Had Put Up on a Door on the Thirty-First of August*

Wednesday the eighth day of September, in that shed, in Fish Street, in a market, and on that mole head.

**The said Wednesday was not a wage Tuesday and was not a rota day and was not a wipe, and the wall behind the reeve's bench came down and went up whole at the angle it has been at since the twelfth and nobody put it back. Forty-four pence went out of that yard on the said day at about half past ten in the morning, being three shillings and eightpence, for a loaf and a half, a candle and a pot, and nothing came into it, and there are four hundred and eight pence in the bag, being two marks seven shillings and fourpence, and the figure that bag is short of the wage of four marks is two hundred and thirty-two pence, being one mark six shillings, and it is not going to be paid and nobody is to write one word under it saying anybody is going to. That loaf and a half, that candle and that pot went to a man of about thirty-eight with a boat of about thirteen tons and no papers on her, who was out all day on the sixth clause of a notice in a different street, and not one penny of that day came out of the owner of that boat, and he is not going to be asked for anything and is not going to be thanked.**

**And the far bench was occupied on the said Wednesday from about ten in the morning until about four in the afternoon and was not approached on it, and the holder of that writ did not go over to him, and nothing was put on that bench and read as a gift, and neither brother has said why. Mara Venn was in that harbor and going nowhere, of no office, not on the crew, not restored, not forgiven, forty marks first after food, neither reduced nor paid, and she was in that shed from about ten in the morning and put forty pence of her own into the bowl on the step at a quarter past one, out of her own hand and not out of the yard's bag, and did not say why and was not asked. Tobias Wren was on the crown of that mole from ten until four, being sixty-one, with eleven pieces of a boat behind him and a boat's hook in his right hand, and on the said day he got the second piece out from behind the ninth one, where it had been since some time in July, and set it against the parapet and looked at it and set it back. The notice of the seventeenth of May past is on its wall in a different street for one hundred and fourteen days and has not been touched. The bellwright of that harbor has not been asked for one hundred and sixteen days, the chest is shut, and the eight marks and a day of a boat have not been argued The three nots standing against the eighth of June past are a tenth week in which nobody in that crew has said one word about them, and the hand of a boy of twenty-two is still at the head of the third column with a name beside it that has not been said out loud since the thirteenth of May past, and that name is not on any page of this harbor.. The two objects of nobody's on that crown are four hundred and fifty-four days old and neither has moved. The bench under that pump is bare for the two hundred and twentieth night. Two lights at about a mile off that point have not moved in thirty-eight days. A man of about fifty-one has said nothing to anybody about a page of six names for fifty-six days. A nine-line sheet with a seal on it has been in that front street for twenty-five days and has been answered by nobody. The paper at the top left of that door in Fish Street is eight days old.**

---

Hobb Lee started at about nine in the morning and finished at about four in the afternoon and did eleven conversations and they were not all the same length.

The first was with a man of about fifty-four who takes fish into the first stall of that market and has taken them in for thirty-one years, standing at the back of that stall with his hands wet.

"Mr Lee."

"I am not going to ask you for your name and you are not going to tell me anything," said Hobb Lee. "There is a paper at the top left of a door at the top of Fish Street. It went up on the thirty-first of August and it has four lines on it. I am not going to read them to you and I am not going to show them to you and I am not going to tell you what is on them. I am telling you that the paper is there so that you know it is there, and if you ever want to read it, that is your business and your eyes and no one else's. And I am going to be at the end of that table on a Sunday at two o'clock reading a book out loud, and if you come you will hear it, and it costs nothing, and there is a bowl on the step and you may put a penny in it or you may not. And the notice on the wall in the other street is a different paper on a different wall and I have not got anything to do with that one either."

"Mr Lee, do you know what is on it."

"I do not know what is on it, because I have not read it," said Hobb Lee, "and I have not read it on purpose, and I am not going to read it to you, and if you ask me that question on a Sunday I am going to say the same thing."

---

The fifth was with a woman of about thirty-one with a barrow of fish and a cold, at the head of a slip, at about half past eleven.

She listened to the whole of it and then said the thing that stopped him, which was: "Are you telling me this because you know what is on it."

"No," said Hobb Lee. "I am telling you this because I do not know what is on it, and because I read a thing out loud in that room on Sunday to about twenty people, and eleven of them cannot read, and every one of those eleven walked past that door this week, and I have got a bad conscience about a book, not about a paper. I have thought about it since the Friday. I have not got a bad conscience about that paper and I have not read it and I have not got anybody to read it to me."

"Then why are you telling me at all."

"Because in nine days I am going to stand at the end of that table and read out loud in a room, and you are one of the eleven, and if I do not say this to you then you will come on a Sunday and find out what that room is from me and from nobody else, and I would rather you heard it from me standing in the street in daylight than found it out in a room with twenty people in it."

---

The eleventh was at about four in the afternoon at the head of a slip with a man of about sixty-two who has sat on the same bollard every day since he came off the water in June, and who has not got up off it while Hobb Lee was talking to him, and who said, at the end of it, in the only complete sentence he said all day:

"That is the first anybody has told me about anything in this harbor in about a month, and I have not been in a room in a month, and I will come on Sunday if the door is not shut."

"The door is not shut on a Sunday," said Hobb Lee. "It is never shut on a Sunday. Come at two."

He went back up Fish Street at about a quarter past four and put the volume down on the table and told Calder Marr that he had had eleven conversations and that four of them had gone on longer than a minute and that the shortest one had been about forty seconds, and that no one of the eleven had asked him to read the paper and that one of them had asked him whether he knew what was on it and that he had said he did not know and had not read it, and that eleven people in that harbor now know that there is a paper on that door and eleven of them will be at that table on Sunday and eleven of them will not know one word of what is on it, and that this was the arrangement and that he had thought about nothing else for two days.

---

The man of about thirty-four came up those stairs at about four o'clock in the afternoon and stood at the top and asked whether anybody had answered his paper.

Hobb Lee said no.

"I have been in this street for a week," said the man of about thirty-four, "and I have not seen one person at that door."

"Nobody has. Nobody has read it. There is a cross in chalk under it made by a boy of about nineteen on the Saturday night because a man in a taproom told him it was a lie, and I have found out that much, and I have not been able to find out who put the chalk there, and neither have you."

"So it stands."

"It stands."

"Does it stand because nobody has read it, or because nobody who has read it is saying anything."

Hobb Lee looked at him for longer than he had looked at him before.

"I am going to tell you the second one," he said, "and then I am not going to say anything else about it, and you did not ask me for anything, and you did not ask me to do anything about that paper, and I have not told you where it came from or who put it up. The person who put that paper up came to this room at about half past four this afternoon and asked me the same question you have just asked and I told him the same answer, and I told him what I am telling you, and then I told him what I am about to tell you and not before, because he put it up and he has been carrying it for eight days and he is entitled to hear it first."

"And what is it."

"That somebody in this harbor has been eleven times to a room over a joiner's shop on a Sunday since the twentieth of April and has gone down those stairs without leaving a mark, and that there is a working man of about forty-six at the end of that table who reads a book out loud to people who cannot read, and that neither of those two things has ever once been written down in this harbor by anybody, and that a man who has been twice to a room and come away and told nobody, and a woman who has been four times to a room and told nobody, are not in a position to go to a man in a taproom and tell him a paper on a door is a lie. That is what I said to him, and that is all I said to him, and I would say the same to you in the same words, and you can do what you like with it."

The man of about thirty-four went down the stairs and did not say one word, and he has not been up those stairs since, and the paper at the top left of that door is on that door and will be on that door on the twentieth day of September next and on every day after that, and nobody in that harbor has answered it and nobody in that harbor was asked to.

---

The caulker was asked for Friday the tenth of September at about five o'clock on the Wednesday afternoon, by name, out loud, for a seam on a pot-lid stock on the first lay that is wanted for a pot and not for anything else, and he said he would do it and would want it said to him on the Thursday, and the holder of that writ said it to him on the Thursday, and the money for that day was in that yard's bag on the Friday afternoon at about four o'clock, and it is the same arrangement as the tenth of August past, and the seventeenth, the twenty-first, the twenty-seventh and the thirty-first, and there are days in that calendar that nobody asked him for and that are not in the bag and are not going in.

---

**Forty-four pence went out of that yard on the said Wednesday at about half past ten in the morning for a loaf and a half, a candle and a pot, and nothing came into it, and the bag is two marks seven shillings and fourpence, and it is short of that wage by one mark six shillings. A man of about forty-six walked round that harbor on his own two feet between about nine in the morning and about four in the afternoon on the said Wednesday and had eleven conversations with eleven people, one at a time, in the street and in their own doors and in daylight, and told every one of them that there is a paper with four lines on it at the top left of the door of a room over a joiner's shop at the top of Fish Street, and told not one of them what is on it, and showed none of them the paper, and read it to none of them, and told one of them that he had not read it himself and was not going to, and told all eleven that a volume is read out loud at that table on a Sunday at two o'clock and that there is a bowl on the step and they may put a penny in it or not, and that four of the eleven conversations went on longer than a minute and the shortest was about forty seconds, and that not one of the eleven asked him to read that paper. A man of about thirty-four came up those stairs at about four in the afternoon and asked whether anybody had answered that paper and was told no, and was then told in the man's own mouth that two people in that harbor had been in that room and had told nobody and were not in a position to go to a man in a taproom, and went down the stairs and did not say one word. A caulker of about twenty-three was asked for Friday the tenth by name at about five o'clock on the said day and said he would do it and would want it said to him on the Thursday. The far bench was occupied from ten until four and was not approached, and neither brother went near the other at any hour of the said day.**

*First column, entered on Wednesday the eighth day of September next at about half past nine in the evening in that shed: that forty-four pence went out of that yard at about half past ten in the morning on the said day for a loaf and a half, a candle and a pot, out of that yard's own bag, and that it went to a man of about thirty-eight with a boat of about thirteen tons and no papers on her who was out all day on the sixth clause of a notice in a different street; and that a man of about forty-six walked on his own two feet from about nine in the morning to about four in the afternoon on the said Wednesday and spoke to eleven people, one at a time, in the street and in their own doors, being eleven people of this harbor who cannot read a word; and that to each of the eleven he said that there is a paper with four lines on it at the top left of the door of a room over a joiner's shop at the top of Fish Street, that the paper went up on the thirty-first of August past, that a volume is read out loud at the long table in that room on a Sunday at two o'clock, that there is a bowl on the top stone of that step and they may put a penny in it or not, and that the notice on the wall in the other street is a different paper on a different wall; and that he showed none of the eleven the paper and read it to none of them and told one of them that he had not read it himself and was not going to; and that four of the eleven conversations went on longer than a minute and the shortest was about forty seconds, and that not one of the eleven asked him to read that paper; and that the eleventh of the eleven was a man of about sixty-two who has sat on the same bollard at the head of a slip every day since he came off the water in June, and that he said that this was the first anybody had told him about anything in that harbor in about a month and that he would come on Sunday if the door were not shut; and that a man of about thirty-four came up the stairs of that room at about four in the afternoon and asked whether anybody had answered the paper at the top left of that door and was told that nobody had; and that there is a mark in chalk about two inches across on that door under that paper, made on the Saturday night of the fourth of September by a boy of about nineteen who had been told in a taproom that the paper was a lie, and that the man who made that mark has not been found; and that a caulker of about twenty-three was asked for Friday the tenth of September next by name at about five in the afternoon and said he would do it and would want it said to him on the Thursday.*

*Second column, not checkable from a mole, entered on the said Wednesday at about half past nine in the evening in that shed, and no name is against it: that the reason a man of about forty-six told eleven people on the said Wednesday that there is a paper on that door, and did not tell them what is on it, is in his own mouth twice over, being that he had been on the wrong side of a paper he could not read at nine years old in this harbor and had fifty-one years of not knowing, and that he had a bad conscience about a book and not about a paper; and that a woman of about thirty-one with a barrow of fish asked him at the head of a slip on the said day whether he knew what was on that paper and was told that he did not know and had not read it and was not going to; and that a man of about thirty-four who had been in that room in June was told, and told after the man who put the paper up had been told, that two people in that harbor had been in that room on Sundays and had gone away and told nobody, and that a man who has been twice to a room and told nobody is not in a position to go into a taproom and say a paper on a door is a lie, and that this was said once and not said again; and that forty pence went into the bowl on the step at a quarter past one on the said day out of the hand of a woman of no office and out of her own money and not out of the yard's bag, and that she did not say why and was not asked; and that the standing list entered in full on the fourteenth of August past is not entered in full on the said Wednesday and stands, and no part of it was paid, reduced, advanced or improved on that day.*
