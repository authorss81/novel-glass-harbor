# Chapter 415

*Tuesday the Seventh Day of September, the Forty-Sixth Wage Tuesday and the Column Not Moved on It and Forty-Five Weeks Standing Fallen Due at the Open of the Ten Days From the Twenty-Fourth of August and Forty-Six Standing at the Close of Them, One Hundred and Sixty-Eight Pence of a Day With a Boat and Two Men at a Berth Coming In, a Man of About Thirty-Four Who Was in That Room in June Asking a Man of About Forty-Six to Read Four Lines Off a Door Out Loud in a Room on a Sunday and Being Refused by Him in His Own Mouth With His Own Reason, and a Woman of About Fifty-Eight Who Counted Six in Her Own Trade and a Man of About Twenty-Six Who Counted Five on a Quay*

Tuesday the seventh day of September, in that shed, in Fish Street, and on that mole head.

**The said Tuesday was a wage Tuesday and was not a rota day and was not a wipe, and the wall behind the reeve's bench came down and went up whole at the angle it has been at since the twelfth and nobody put it back. One hundred and sixty-eight pence came into that yard on the said day, being a day's work with a boat and two men at a berth at the figure in chalk on a slate at an end stall, being one mark and eightpence, and nothing went out of it, and there are four hundred and fifty-two pence in the bag, being two marks and eleven shillings, and the figure that bag is short of the wage of four marks is one hundred and eighty-eight pence, being one mark two shillings and fourpence, and it is not going to be paid and nobody is to write one word under it saying anybody is going to.**

**And the far bench was occupied on the said Tuesday from about ten in the morning until about four in the afternoon and was not approached on it, and the holder of that writ did not go over to him, and nothing was put on that bench and read as a gift, and neither brother has said why. Mara Venn was in that harbor and going nowhere, of no office, not on the crew, not restored, not forgiven, forty marks first after food, neither reduced nor paid, and she was not in that shed on the said day and was in a room over a fish market for four hours doing nothing that anybody has paid her for. Tobias Wren was on the crown of that mole from ten until four, being sixty-one, with eleven pieces of a boat behind him and a boat's hook in his right hand, and on the said day he was sat on the tenth piece with his back against the parapet and his legs out along the stones and the hook laid across the stones beside him, doing nothing at all for the hour between one and two, and a gull took a piece of bread off the end of the eleventh piece and he watched it go and did not move. The notice of the seventeenth of May past is on its wall in a different street for one hundred and thirteen days and has not been touched. The bellwright of that harbor has not been asked for one hundred and fifteen days, the chest is shut, and the eight marks and a day of a boat have not been argued, and no digit of anything he ever said is on a page. The three nots standing against the eighth of June past are a tenth week in which nobody in that crew has said one word about them, and the hand of a boy of twenty-two is still at the head of the third column with a name beside it that has not been said out loud since the thirteenth of May past, and that name is not on any page of this harbor. The two objects of nobody's on that crown are four hundred and fifty-three days old and neither has moved. The bench under that pump is bare for the two hundred and nineteenth night. Two lights at about a mile off that point have not moved in thirty-seven days. A man of about fifty-one has said nothing to anybody about a page of six names for fifty-five days. A nine-line sheet with a seal on it has been in that front street for twenty-four days and has been answered by nobody. The paper at the top left of that door in Fish Street is seven days old.**

---

The wage column is a column in a book in that shed and it has twenty-six lines in it and one hundred and four marks against them, and there are twenty envelopes in a chest that stands on a bench in this yard's own front room, and the column has not moved on a wage Tuesday since the fourth of April past.

Forty-four weeks stood fallen due at the open of ten days that ended on Thursday the second of September, and the forty-fifth week fell on Tuesday the thirty-first day of August, and this is the forty-sixth. The column did not move. The chest is shut. Nobody wrote one word under any of it, in the shed or in the front room or anywhere, saying anybody was going to pay it, and there is a thing in that yard's own mouth about a figure that is never going to be zero, and this is a different figure, and both of them are true and neither of them cancels the other.

Nessa Pike opened the column on the Tuesday morning at about ten, read the top line, shut it, and wrote the number of the week in the second column against the reason for the column not moving, and the number of the week is forty-six.

"You have put the week in," said Calder Marr.

"It is a week. It is a week that has come and gone and the column did not move, and the first column is a count of things a stranger can check and a week is a week." She put the lid back. "The forty-seventh is the fourteenth of September. That is next Tuesday and it is not mine either."

---

The man of about thirty-four who had been in that room in June came up the stairs at about half past three on the Tuesday afternoon and asked Hobb Lee to read the four lines off the door out loud on a Sunday, to everybody in the room, before he read the heads.

Hobb Lee put the pencil down on the table and looked at him for a while.

"No."

"You are going to read a volume out loud in that room to about twenty people on a Sunday and eleven of them cannot read a word, and there is a paper on that door with four lines on it that would take about a minute, and there is a man in this harbor who has been asked one at a time whether he was in that room on a night in August and one of them was, and nobody in this harbor knows which. A minute. That is all I am asking for."

"I am not going to read it."

"Why not."

"Because you have not asked me for a minute," said Hobb Lee. "You have asked me to stand up in a room on a Sunday and say a thing that a man put on a door about eleven people, and one minute of that is about two hours of it, and if I do it then those eleven will never hear a head-note in that room again in their lives, and they will come on a Sunday and they will sit at that table and they will be waiting for me to get to the paper on the door, and the paper on the door will be the thing that room is for. And that is not a minute, and it is not even an hour, it is the rest of it."

"They do not even know it is there."

"Then they do not know it is there," said Hobb Lee, "and I am not going to be the one who changes that, and I will tell you exactly what I am going to do about it, and I am telling you now so that nobody can say afterwards that I sat on it. On Wednesday I am going to walk round this harbor on my own two feet and I am going to tell eleven people one at a time, in the street, in their own doors, that there is a paper on that door with four lines on it, and that it is a paper and it is not the notice on the wall in the other street, and that I am going to read that volume out loud on a Sunday from that table, and that I am not going to read that paper to them and they are not to ask me to. That is what I am going to do. It is eleven conversations of about a minute each and I am going to have them in daylight and I am going to have them face to face. And I am not going to tell them what is on it, and you are not going to tell them what is on it, and the woman of about fifty-eight is not going to tell them what is on it, and nobody in this harbor is going to tell them what is on it. And if one of those eleven is the one, then in thirteen days that one will read it himself on that door, standing up, with his own eyes, in his own time, and he will not have been told by a single person in this harbor, and he will be able to do whatever it is that a man does with a piece of paper that he has read with his own eyes."

The man of about thirty-four stood at the end of that table for a bit.

"You have thought about that a great deal for a man who mends nets."

"I have thought about it since the Friday," said Hobb Lee, "and the reason I have thought about it is that I have been on the wrong side of a paper I could not read. I was nine years old and there was a notice on a door in this harbor and my mother read it to me out loud in a kitchen and I have never known since that day whether she made any of it up, and I have had thirty-seven years of not knowing, and I would not put a man of about thirty-four in a room on a Sunday in front of twenty people and not know it for the rest of his life. That is the whole of my reason and it is a selfish one and I have not got a better one."

---

The woman of about fifty-eight came down that slip at about five and put her slate on the bench stone in that shed face up, which she has done twice in nine months, and both times she did not put a hand on it.

"Read out the Sunday," she said.

"Two hours and about twenty minutes, from two o'clock, in a room that is let out and that I do not have the use of on any other day, on a day I mend nets and do not mend them, and a bowl on a step that I did not have to make and Tom Barlas has to make whether I am there or not, and a shilling I was going to have on a Sunday." She looked at her own hands. "That is the whole of what it costs, and every part of it is mine, and none of it comes out of anybody's bag and none of it comes out of the owner of any boat, and I am not going to be paid for it and I would not take it if I was offered it, and there is no figure anywhere in this harbor for what a man gives up on a Sunday because he is reading a book for people who cannot read."

Nessa Pike wrote that in the second column and wrote the reason against it, being that the figure is not a figure of money and is not a figure of hours that anybody can check, and that the reason it is in the second column is that a man on a mole cannot check what a man gave up on a Sunday, and Calder Marr did not add anything to it.

---

**One hundred and sixty-eight pence came into that yard on the said Tuesday, being a day's work with a boat and two men at a berth at the figure in chalk on a slate at an end stall, being one mark and eightpence, and nothing went out of it, and the bag is two marks and eleven shillings, and it is short of that wage by one mark two shillings and fourpence. The forty-sixth wage Tuesday fell and the column did not move on it, so that forty-five weeks stood fallen due at the open of the ten days that ended on the second of September and forty-six stand at the close of them, and the column has twenty-six lines in it and one hundred and four marks against them and twenty envelopes in a chest in a front room, and nobody wrote one word under any of it saying anybody was going to pay it. A man of about thirty-four asked a man of about forty-six to read the four lines off that door out loud in that room on a Sunday, to everybody in it, and was refused in the man's own mouth with the man's own reason, being that those eleven would never hear a head-note in that room again if they were waiting for the paper, and being that he had been on the wrong side of a paper he could not read at nine years old in this harbor and had thirty-seven years of not knowing whether his mother had made any of it up. He said out loud on the said day that on the Wednesday he was going to tell eleven people one at a time, in daylight and face to face, that there is a paper on that door and that he was not going to read it to them. A woman of about fifty-eight read the Sunday out of a slate and did not touch it. The far bench was occupied from ten until four and was not approached, and neither brother went near the other at any hour of the said day.**

*First column, entered on Tuesday the seventh day of September next at about half past nine in the evening in that shed: that the said Tuesday was the forty-sixth wage Tuesday and that the column did not move on it, and that forty-four weeks stood fallen due at the close of the twenty-third of August past, that the forty-fifth fell on the thirty-first of August and that the forty-sixth fell on the said day, and that the forty-seventh falls on Tuesday the fourteenth of September next; and that the column has twenty-six lines in it and one hundred and four marks against them and twenty envelopes in a chest in a front room and has not moved since the fourth of April past; and that one hundred and sixty-eight pence came into that yard on the said day at the figure in chalk on a slate at an end stall; and that a man of about thirty-four asked a man of about forty-six to read the four lines at the top left of the door of a room over a joiner's shop out loud in that room on a Sunday to everybody in it, and was refused; and that a room over a joiner's shop at the top of Fish Street was let out on Sunday the fifth of September last from about one o'clock with a bowl on the top stone of its step from two until six, and that a man of about forty-six read the head-notes of a public volume out loud at that table for about two hours and twenty minutes and wrote his own name and the hour under every head that he read, and that a woman of about fifty-eight refused to have the figure of lamp oil entered in that volume on that Sunday and her no was entered in the same hand and the same size as every other line with her name and the hour against it; and that a woman of about sixty-one of the third lay asked at the end of that table on that Sunday whether that book holds a thing a person made themselves, and was told that it does not, and that she has one and that it is about eleven inches by eight and that it is in a box in her back room; and that the said Tuesday is the second day of that ten and the said Sunday was the first.*

*Second column, not checkable from a mole, entered on the said Tuesday at about half past nine in the evening in that shed, and no name is against it: that the number of the week against the reason the column did not move is forty-six, and that a week is a week that came and gone, and that the first column is a count of things a stranger can check and a week is a week; and that the reason a man of about forty-six refused to read a paper on a door out loud in a room in front of about twenty people is in his own mouth and is that those eleven would never hear a head-note in that room again if they were sitting at that table waiting for the paper, and that he had been on the wrong side of a paper he could not read at nine years old in this harbor and had thirty-seven years of not knowing, and that he said that was a selfish reason and that he had not got a better one; and that a man of about forty-six told a man of about thirty-four on the said Tuesday that on the Wednesday he was going to tell eleven people one at a time in daylight and face to face that there is a paper on that door with four lines on it, that it is a paper and not the notice on the wall in the other street, that a volume is going to be read out loud on a Sunday from that table, and that he was not going to read that paper to them and they were not to ask him to; and that the cost of a Sunday of reading is two hours and about twenty minutes, a shilling not earned, and a bowl on a step that a man has to make whether anybody is in the room or not, and that the whole of it is that man's own and that none of it comes out of anybody's bag, and that there is no figure in this harbor for what a man gives up on a Sunday because he is reading a book for people who cannot read, and that that is the reason it is in this column and not in the first; and that the standing list entered in full on the fourteenth of August past is not entered in full on the said Tuesday and stands, and no part of it was paid, reduced, advanced or improved on that day.*
