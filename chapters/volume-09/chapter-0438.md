# Chapter 438

*Thursday the Thirtieth Day of September, the Eighty-Sixth Time the Cistern Was Charged at About Eight in the Morning and Walked on by None of It at About Half Past Nine and the Third and Last Charge in These Ten Days, a Second Man Put in That Bell One at a Time With a Reason Written Down Against It Before He Went Down and a Tender of About Forty Tons and Fifteen Fathoms of Chain and a Weight on the Tail of It, a Cutoff Pulled at Nine Minutes to Three in the Afternoon, a Word for the Thing Asked Out Loud by a Man of About Forty-Six and Given an Answer in Nine Words, Not One Penny Charged to the Owner of Any of the Seven Boats on Any of the Three Days, One Hundred and Sixty-Eight Pence Coming In at About Two in the Afternoon, and the Bag at Three Hundred and Sixty-Four Pence*

Thursday the thirtieth day of September, on a bank about four hundred yards down that foul, at the head of the third lay, in that shed, and on that mole head.

**The said Thursday was a rota day and was not a wage Tuesday and was not a wipe, and the cistern at the back of that market was charged at about eight in the morning and walked on by none of it at about half past nine, being the eighty-sixth time, and the top name on that rota board is still off it, and that is the third and last charge on that cistern in these ten days and the other seven of them put none. One hundred and sixty-eight pence came into that yard at about two in the afternoon for a day's work with a boat and two men at a berth at the figure in chalk on a slate at an end stall, and there are three hundred and sixty-four pence in the bag at the close of the said day, being two marks three shillings and eightpence, and the figure that bag is short of the wage of four marks is two hundred and seventy-six pence, being one mark nine shillings and eightpence, and it is not going to be paid and nobody is to write one word under it saying anybody is going to.**

**And the far bench was occupied from about ten in the morning until about four in the afternoon and was not approached, and the holder of that writ did not go over to him, and nothing was put on that bench and read as a gift, and neither brother has said why. Mara Venn was in that harbor and going nowhere, of no office, not on the crew, not restored, not forgiven, forty marks first after food, neither reduced nor paid, and she was at the head of that slip from about ten in the morning until about four in the afternoon and read nothing and signed nothing. Tobias Wren was on the crown of that mole from ten until four, being sixty-one, and on the said day he stopped his hook in the crack in the stone at the corner where the steps come up, which is the piece the mast came down on on the twenty-third, and looked at the crack for about two minutes and went back to the eleventh piece. The notice of the seventeenth of May past is on its wall in a different street for one hundred and thirty-six days. The bellwright of that harbor has not been asked for one hundred and thirty-eight days, the chest is shut, and the eight marks and a day of a boat have not been argued, and no digit of anything he ever said is on a page. The three nots standing against the eighth of June past are a twelfth week in which nobody in that crew has said one word about them, and the hand of a boy of twenty-two is still at the head of the third column with a name beside it that has not been said out loud since the thirteenth of May past, and that name is not on any page of this harbor. The two objects of nobody's on that crown are four hundred and seventy-six days old. The bench under that pump is bare for the two hundred and forty-second night. Two lights at about a mile off that point have not moved in sixty days. A man of about fifty-one has said nothing to anybody about a page of six names for seventy-eight days. The sheet of six lines at the back of that end stall is on that wall for the fifteenth day and the paper of four lines in Fish Street is on that door for the thirtieth day and the nine-line sheet with a seal on it is in that front street for forty-seven days.**

---

The reason was written down before the man went down.

That had not been done before and it was done from the second day on, and the reason for it is in the ship's own mouth and is that a thing a man is put into the water to look at is the one thing about it that anybody will be asked about afterwards, and that a reason written before he goes down is worth something and a reason written after he comes up is worth nothing.

*The thirtieth day of September. A man of the third lay who crews in a boat of about fourteen tons and who has been in that boat once in nineteen years is to be put in that bell at about half past five in the morning, alone behind the first man and alone in front of the next, because two men have put their hands on about two foot of plank two foot aft of that shore and have said two different things about it, and neither of them crews in that boat and both of them have a reason to be right.*

Sedge Rundle went down at about half past five and was in there about thirty minutes.

The chain came over the rail at about nine minutes to three in the afternoon and there were about twenty-five men in that boat and they were out and on the tender's deck in about eleven minutes, and the reason for that one is in that book too and says that the water was down to about seven foot and a half over that shoe and a quarter of an inch of the last of it had gone since the day before and the bank does not care about an inch.

---

He came up and sat down on the tender's deck with his back against the steam pipe and did not say anything for about two minutes.

"I have been in that boat once, in ninety-five, and I was in her for a day and a half laying planks in her bottom and I remember where the ballast was because it was in the way," he said. "There is a piece of that bottom about two foot aft of where you are saying and it is not soft. It is as sound as anything in her. I put my whole hand flat on it and I could not get my thumb into it at all and I did not try to force it."

"That is the third account," said Nessa Pike, "of about two foot of plank, and it is entered as it was said."

"Then it is entered as it was said," he said, "and I am not going to change it because a bellwright from off the reef line has been in there and I have not. That is not how a thing works. You have got a man who says it is soft with his hand, and a man who says it is soft with his thumb and it stinks, and a man who says it is sound with his hand and a whole hand, and one of those three is wrong and I do not know which one and neither do you, and I am not going to be the one that makes it so by saying it again."

---

Hobb Lee came down that slip at about eleven in the morning in daylight with his own two feet and stood at the stern of a boat of about forty tons and looked at about thirty men on a mud bank in eight foot of water with a lamp on a man's chest and a chain going over a rail.

He said one thing and then he said nothing else for about twenty minutes.

"What is that called?" he said.

"That is seven boats on a Tuesday and a person on the water."

"That is not what I asked." He did not raise his voice. "There is a word somewhere for the man who does it. There is a word for the caulker and there is a word for the man who takes a mould and there has to be a word for the one who keeps a keel right in between tides with a chain and a boy with a weight on it."

Calder Marr took his hand off the rail.

"There is a word and I will not give it to you in a shed and you will not have it off me in a market," he said. "It is not taken by being said."

"Then I will not ask again," said Hobb Lee, "and I am not going to say it at my table either, and you can put in whatever you like about a man of about forty-six coming down a slip in daylight and asking a question and being told no, because I will not read it out and neither will anybody else in that room."

He said the last part with some heat and then he stopped, and he stood at that stern for another twenty minutes and then he went up that slip and mended a net, and the man who read the head-notes off a bound volume at the end of a long table in a room over a joiner's shop will not read anything out loud in that room that is not a head in that book, and he has said so in his own mouth before, in that market and in that shed, and he did not say it again on the said day.

---

The woman of fifty-eight with the end stall was at the head of that slip again from about two in the afternoon with a basket of tobacco and about two hundred yards of new manila in it and sold what she had and came back at about four.

She said one thing to a man of about twenty-six with a plane who was standing at her wall with his hands in his pockets.

"Three days and not one penny of it has come off anybody's boat," she said. "And there is a hole in a man's rail that I can see from here and he is not paying for it and he is not arguing about it, and I would like that written down somewhere and not on my slate, because my slate has got a figure on it and the figure has not moved since the first of June and it is not going to move because of any of this."

"It has not moved," said the woman who prices things, who was standing four feet off with the good paper under her arm.

"No," said the woman of fifty-eight. "It has not, and I did not expect it to, and I would like my tobacco paid for."

It was, and out of the holder of that writ's own hand.

On the third day of that work, not one penny of it was charged to the owner of any of the seven boats. Not one penny came out of the man with two papers on a table in an office off the west basin and not one penny came out of the Crown, and the whole of the money that moved in three days came out of one bag in one shed that has two figures in it and no third one, and it is short of the wage of four marks on every hour of every one of the ten days it has been open, and it is not going to be paid and nobody is going to write one word under it saying anybody is going to.

---

There is no flour in that market and there has been none since the spring. There has been no barrel of lamp oil in that harbor since the twenty-ninth day of August and a figure of four hundred and fifty pence stands on that board with three hundred and seventy-eight pence visible above it and both dates on it, and that figure cannot be taken off. The sheet of six lines is on the wall at the back of that stall for the fifteenth day on the outside of that figure, and it names no person and no town and no office and has a charge of one hundred and sixty-eight pence at the foot of it, and no claim has been lodged under it in this harbor. The nine-line sheet with a seal on it is in that front street for forty-seven days and is a different paper on a different wall and names no person and no harbor and puts no figure on it. The paper of four lines in Fish Street is on that door for the thirtieth day and its fourth line ended on the twentieth and has been answered by nobody. The mole-head light is out. The north berth is out. The fire party's hose is dead until the spring. The tide gang is four men short. The hard at Ashtide is unanswered since the ninth of November past. One berth on the third lay is short. Nineteen winters are disputed and unpaid. The chandler's note is at thirty-one with its three counts at twenty-two, thirteen and eleven. The delayed note stands at eleven logged occasions and two of them with no call in them. Forty-nine weeks stand fallen due with no envelope against any of them.

**A man of the third lay who crews in a boat of about fourteen tons and who has been in that boat once in nineteen years was put in a bell off the port quarter of a boat of about nineteen tons lying on a bank four hundred yards down that foul off the south end of that mole at about half past five in the morning on the said Thursday, alone, one at a time, and the reason he was put in was written down before he went down, which had not been done before and was done from that day on. He was in there about thirty minutes and came up and sat down on the deck of a steam tender of about forty tons with his back against a steam pipe and said that he had been in that boat once in ninety-five for a day and a half laying planks in her bottom and that he remembered where the ballast was, and that a piece of that bottom about two foot aft of where the shore is was not soft but as sound as anything in her, and that he put his whole hand flat on it and could not get his thumb into it at all and did not try to force it, and that he was not going to change it because somebody else had been in there and he had not. That is the third account of about two foot of plank and two men in a bell have said one thing about it and another man has said the other, and all three are in that book in the order they were said and none of them has been settled. The chain came over the rail at about nine minutes to three in the afternoon with about twenty-five men in that boat and they were out and on the tender's deck in about eleven minutes, and the reason is in that book. A man of about forty-six who mends nets on the third lay and who can read came down that slip at about eleven in the morning in daylight and asked what the man who keeps a keel right in between tides with a chain and a weight on it is called, and was told that there is a word and that it is not taken by being said and that he will not be given it in a shed and will not have it off anybody in a market; and he said in his own mouth that he would not ask again and would not say it at his table either and that he would not read it out in that room and neither would anybody else in it, and he stood at that stern for about twenty minutes and then went up that slip and mended a net. A woman of about fifty-eight with an end stall and a slate was at the head of that slip from about two in the afternoon and said that three days had gone by and not one penny of any of it had come off anybody's boat, and that there is a hole in a man's rail that she can see from where she stands and that he is not paying for it and is not arguing about it, and that she would like that written down somewhere and not on her slate because her slate has got a figure on it and the figure has not moved since the first of June and is not going to move because of any of this. One hundred and sixty-eight pence came in at about two in the afternoon at the figure in chalk on a slate at an end stall and the bag stands at three hundred and sixty-four pence, being two marks three shillings and eightpence.**

*First column, entered on Thursday the thirtieth day of September next at about half past nine in the evening in that shed: that the cistern was charged at about eight in the morning and walked on by none of it at about half past nine, being the eighty-sixth time, and that this is the third and last charge on that cistern in these ten days and that the twenty-third, the twenty-seventh and the thirtieth of September are the only three of the ten on which one was placed and the other seven put none, and that the top name on that rota board is still off it; and that a man of the third lay who crews in a boat of about fourteen tons and who has been in that boat once in nineteen years went down in a bell off the port quarter of a boat of about nineteen tons lying on a bank about four hundred yards down the foul at about half past five in the morning on the said day, alone, one at a time, and the reason he was put in was written down in that book before he went down; and that he was in that bell about thirty minutes and came up and sat down on the after deck of a steam tender of about forty tons with his back against a steam pipe and said that a piece of about two foot of that bottom two foot aft of the shore is not soft and is as sound as anything in her and that he put his whole hand flat on it and could not get his thumb into it and did not try to force it; and that this is the third account of that plank, the first being a thumb through the veneer of it about the size of a shilling at nine foot on the Friday before the twenty-third and the second being a hand flat on it at about eight foot in the dark that went in about the depth of a thumb and came back with a smell off it that was not tar and was not oil; and that the chain came over the rail at about nine minutes to three in the afternoon with about twenty-five men in that boat and they were out and on the tender's deck in about eleven minutes, being the third such cutoff in four days and the third such line written against it; and that a man of about forty-six who mends nets on the third lay came down that slip at about eleven in the morning in daylight and asked what the man who keeps a keel right in between tides with a chain and a weight on it is called and was told that there is a word and that it is not taken by being said; and that one hundred and sixty-eight pence came into that yard at about two in the afternoon for a day's work with a boat and two men at a berth at the figure in chalk on a slate at an end stall, and that one hundred and ninety-six plus one hundred and sixty-eight is three hundred and sixty-four pence at the close of the said day, being two marks three shillings and eightpence, being short of the wage of four marks by two hundred and seventy-six pence, being one mark nine shillings and eightpence; and that the said day was not a wipe and was not a wage Tuesday.*
*Second column, not checkable from a mole, entered on the said Thursday at about half past nine in the evening in that shed, and no name is against it: that the reason a reason is written down before a man is put into the water rather than after he comes up is in the ship's own mouth and is that a reason written after a man comes up is worth nothing, and that this is entered in this column and not in the first because a stranger with a leaf and a pencil can count the first column and cannot take a reason out of it; and that the reason three accounts of about two foot of plank are kept and none of them is chosen between is in the third man's own mouth and is that one of the three is wrong and that he does not know which one and neither does anybody else, and that a yard that chooses between them before somebody has a reason to choose is not keeping a record and is keeping an opinion; and that the reason no word has been given to a man of about forty-six who asked for one out loud on the said day is in the holder's own mouth and is that it is not taken by being said, and that the man who asked said in his own mouth that he would not ask again and would not say it at his table, and that this yard has entered both of those and has not improved on either; and that the reason a sheet of six lines has been on the wall at the back of that end stall for the fifteenth day and that yard has put nothing beside it and compared it with nothing is that a sheet on a wall is a sheet on a wall and a thing that is stood next to it becomes a claim about it, and that the three days of work on that bank have not put one figure against that sheet and have not taken one off it and no man on that bank went past it and stopped at it; and that the reason no figure is entered against a hole in a boat's rail is that this yard has got no figure for a boat that strikes a baulk and is not going to invent one and call it material, and that a woman with a slate has asked for a fact to be written down somewhere and not on her slate and has been refused the slate and given the second column; and that the standing list entered in full on the fourteenth of August past is not entered in full on the said Thursday and stands, and no part of it was paid, reduced, advanced or improved on that day.*
