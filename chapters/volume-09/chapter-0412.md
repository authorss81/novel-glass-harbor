# Chapter 412

*Saturday the Fourth Day of September, a Paper at the Top Left of a Door Read Out in Full in a Shed With the Hour Against It and the Drawing-Pin Under a Fold of Oiled Paper Kept There by the Man Who Put It Up, a Man of About Forty-One Called Walter Dunn Who Was on a Ladder in a Passage at Eleven o'Clock at Night on the Nineteenth of August and Found a Lamp of About Four Inches Burning in a Room With Nothing in the Room, Eleven People Asked One at a Time and One of the Eleven in That Room on That Night, One Hundred and Sixty-Eight Pence of a Day With a Boat and Two Men at a Berth Coming In at the Figure in Chalk on a Slate at an End Stall, and Nobody in That Harbor Has Answered That Paper*

Saturday the fourth day of September, in that shed, in Fish Street, and on that mole head.

**The said Saturday was not a wage Tuesday and was not a rota day and was not a wipe, and the wall behind the reeve's bench came down and went up whole at the angle it has been at since the twelfth and nobody put it back. One hundred and sixty-eight pence came into that yard on the said day, being a day's work with a boat and two men at a berth at the figure in chalk on a slate at an end stall, being one mark and eightpence, and nothing went out of it, and there are four hundred and fifty-two pence in the bag, being two marks and eleven shillings, and the figure that bag is short of the wage of four marks is one hundred and eighty-eight pence, being one mark two shillings and fourpence, and it is not going to be paid and nobody is to write one word under it saying anybody is going to.**

**And the far bench was occupied on the said Saturday from about ten in the morning until about four in the afternoon and was not approached on it, and the holder of that writ did not go over to him, and nothing was put on that bench and read as a gift, and neither brother has said why. Mara Venn was in that harbor and going nowhere, of no office, not on the crew, not restored, not forgiven, forty marks first after food, neither reduced nor paid, and she was in that shed from about four in the afternoon. Tobias Wren was on the crown of that mole from ten until four, being sixty-one, with eleven pieces of a boat behind him and a boat's hook in his right hand, and on the said day he took the ninth piece off the parapet and turned it face up to the light and looked at the grain of it for about a minute and then put it back the way it had been, which is not the way a man puts a piece back that is going to be fitted. The notice of the seventeenth of May past is on its wall in a different street for one hundred and ten days and has not been touched. The bellwright of that harbor has not been asked for one hundred and twelve days, the chest is shut, and the eight marks and a day of a boat have not been argued, and no digit of anything he ever said is on a page. The three nots standing against the eighth of June past are a tenth week in which nobody in that crew has said one word about them, and the hand of a boy of twenty-two is still at the head of the third column with a name beside it that has not been said out loud since the thirteenth of May past, and that name is not on any page of this harbor. The two objects of nobody's on that crown are four hundred and fifty days old and neither has moved. The bench under that pump is bare for the two hundred and sixteenth night. Two lights at about a mile off that point have not moved in thirty-four days. A man of about fifty-one has said nothing to anybody about a page of six names for fifty-two days. A nine-line sheet with a seal on it has been in that front street for twenty-one days and has been answered by nobody. The paper at the top left of that door in Fish Street is four days old.**

---

The four lines of that paper were in Calder Marr's coat by half past eight on the Saturday morning, folded in four, and he put them on the bench stone in that shed and read them out to nobody at about nine, word for word, and then read them out again to Nessa Pike at about ten, who wrote them onto her slate and said that a man could not check a paper he had not seen and she wanted to see it.

"That is a good thing to have said," Calder said, "and you are the only person in this harbor who has said it to me this month."

"I have been saying it since the seventh of April," said Nessa, "and you have been writing things down since the first of March."

They went up to Fish Street at about half past ten and stood in the passage at the back of the joiner's shop, which is a passage about four feet wide with a pump in it that has not been in use since anybody can remember, and at the top of the passage there is a door with a fanlight over it and one of the four panes of that fanlight is out.

"It is out at the top left," Walter Dunn said. "It has been out since the spring and there is no glazier in this harbor with the money to come and put a pane in, so it has been out all summer, and that is how I saw it."

Walter Dunn is about forty-one and he is the night man at the fish market steps and he has been the night man at the fish market steps for eleven years, and he does that work between eleven at night and five in the morning, and in the summer he sleeps from about six until about ten in the morning in a room over a chandler's store, and in this week he has been up at about two in the afternoon.

"I came up this passage at about eleven on the nineteenth of August with a shutter off my hand, because the shutter over the joiner's back door comes off its top hinge in a wind and I had promised the joiner I would look at it and I am not a man who takes a thing back after I have said I will do it." He held his hand up about shoulder high. "And there was a light coming through that fanlight onto the top of the pump, and it was not a candle, because a candle does not throw that far, and it moved twice and then it did not move at all for about four minutes, and then it went out."

"Was the door open."

"The door was shut. I put my head through the hole in the fanlight and the room was empty and the table was against the wall and the lamp was on the table and the lamp was a small one, about four inches, and there was nothing on the table and nothing on the floor and nothing on the bench at either side of the table. There is nothing in that room but a table and two benches and a lamp on a nail. I have looked at it. There is no cupboard in it and no chest and no shelves and no press and no box. If a thing of any size had been brought into that room and put down and taken out again within four minutes, two men could not have done it in that room without knocking a bench over."

"Then somebody was in there with a lamp and took the lamp away and left nothing."

"No," said Walter Dunn. "A man does not carry a lamp four inches across a market and down a slip and not spill it. That lamp is going to be in a pocket or a hand, and what was in that room was in a pocket or a hand too, and it was in there for under four minutes and it was not a thing anybody put on a table. Which means it was a person, and the person was here at night in a room that is let out on a Sunday, in a street where a lamp is showing in a window at eleven o'clock, and I am not the only man in this harbor who has stood at the top of a slip at night and wondered what that is."

Calder Marr did not say one word about what that is.

"Who put that paper up," he said.

"I did." Walter Dunn took his hands out of his pockets. "The Thursday after. It went up on the thirty-first day of August last, not the first of September, and I will correct the two days in whatever you write down, because if a man puts a day on a paper he has to have the day right. I put it up on the thirty-first of August last at about eleven in the morning, before the market filled, and I put the pin through the top left corner and folded a strip of oiled paper over the pin and stuck it, because a pin with a head on it can be taken out in about a second and I did not want it taken out in about a second."

"What is in the four lines."

"What I just read to you, and it is all of it, and there is a fifth thing I have not put on it and am not going to." He looked at the pump. "There is a lamp of about four inches, and there was a person, and I could not say which, and so I asked the people who were in the street that week. Not the eleven. Everybody that was in the street that week, in ones and twos, over four days, and I asked all of them one question, which was whether they had seen a lamp or a person in that passage, and about twenty of them had seen something and could not tell me anything, and eleven of them I asked a second question about, which was whether they had been in that room on the night of the nineteenth of August."

"You asked eleven people one at a time."

"I asked eleven people one at a time, in four days, on the quay and at the water and in their doors, and each of them was asked in front of nobody. And I wrote the eleven down on a piece of paper in my own hand with the order I asked them in and I have that paper and it is in my coat, and I am not going to show it to anybody, and I am not going to destroy it, and those are two different things and I have thought about which of them I was going to do for about a week and I have settled on not destroying it."

"You have put a paper on that door with eleven days on it in it and you have the eleven in your coat."

"I have the eleven in my coat and one of the eleven was in that room on that night and I have not said which and I am not going to say which, and that is the whole of what that paper is." He said it without any weight on it at all. "I want it understood that I have not put anybody's name on that door. I have put four lines on that door and there is a date at the end of the fourth one, and if somebody is in that list then he or she has got until the twentieth of September to be out of this harbor, and that is a thing a man can do with a piece of paper and four lines and nothing else. And if I had put the name on, then it would not be a date at the end, it would be a name, and a name on a door is a different thing entirely, and I could not get the name off again. You are the first person who has asked me about that paper and I have not said a word about it to anybody in four days, and I said I would not, and I have not, and there is a difference between me being asked a question and me offering a thing, and I have not offered a thing."

Nessa Pike wrote the day on her slate, because it was the thirty-first of August and not the first of September, and she said nothing else about it.

"Have you been asked to take it down," said Calder.

"Twice. By a woman of about fifty-eight and once by a man I did not know. And I have not taken it down and I am not going to take it down."

"Then who is the paper for."

"It is for whoever is in that list," said Walter Dunn, "and it is for the people of that street, because I have put a lamp on a wall in a street where anybody could stand on a ladder and look through a hole in a fanlight, and if I put nothing on the door then a person in that room in the dark is somebody's business, and a person in that room in the dark is my business, because I am the one standing on the ladder, and a man who has been told a thing and says nothing about it is doing a thing he is going to have to think about for the rest of his life, and I would rather be a man who wrote four lines on a door."

---

The woman of about fifty-eight came up the slip at about four in the afternoon and stood at the bench stone in that shed and read the four lines off Calder's slate, because she cannot read the paper itself and he had offered to read it to her and had not read it to her, and she had wanted the words and had wanted them off somebody else's slate.

"Eleven days," she said. "And the eleventh is Joel Harrow's aunt, and the man at the top of that passage is going to be asked by a person he has not met yet, in a week, from a table, in a room, with about twenty people in it, and he will not know that is what is happening. And the man at the top of that passage has asked eleven people one at a time and one of them was in that room and he has not said which, and that paper is the only thing on that door, and every one of the eleven can walk past that door every day of their lives and not know one word of it."

"Will you tell them."

"No," said the woman of about fifty-eight. "I have got six of them in my own trade and I could tell them in a morning and it would be the biggest thing that has happened in that market in a year, and I am not going to do it, and I will tell you why. Because that paper does not say one word about any of the six of them, and because the day I stand in that market and tell six people that there is a paper on a door with eleven names behind it, every one of the six of them will spend the rest of their lives walking round that door, and I have watched four people do that about a lamp already and I am not going to start a fifth and a sixth and a seventh and an eighth and a ninth and a tenth."

"Then who tells them."

"The man who is going to stand at the end of that table and read that volume out loud tells them," she said, "on a Sunday, and not one word before, and he will tell them that there is a paper on that door and he will not tell them what is on it, and that is a different thing from what I would be doing and it is the only version of it I would stand behind."

---

The paper was still at the top left of that door at about nine o'clock on the Saturday night, with the drawing-pin under the fold of oiled paper, and the four lines on it were the same four lines, and there was a second mark under them in chalk, about two inches across, which was not on the paper and which was made with a piece of chalk on the door itself by somebody in the Saturday night, and Calder Marr saw it on the Sunday morning and did not find out then who had made it, and the man who made that mark was not found on the Monday and was not found on the Tuesday, and what is on that door from the fourth of September is a paper with four lines and a cross in chalk and a strip of oiled paper over a pin.

**One hundred and sixty-eight pence came into that yard on the said Saturday, being a day's work with a boat and two men at a berth at the figure in chalk on a slate at an end stall, being one mark and eightpence, and nothing went out of it, and the bag is four hundred and fifty-two pence, being two marks and eleven shillings, and it is short of that wage by one hundred and eighty-eight pence, being one mark two shillings and fourpence. A man of about forty-one called Walter Dunn, who is the night man at the fish market steps and has been for eleven years, found a lamp of about four inches burning in that room over a joiner's shop on the night of the nineteenth of August past at eleven o'clock, and the lamp is four inches and the room holds a table and two benches and a lamp on a nail and nothing else, and he put four lines on the door of it on the thirty-first of August past at about eleven in the morning, before the market filled, with a drawing-pin under a fold of oiled paper, and the day on that paper is right and is the thirty-first of August and not the first of September. He asked eleven people one at a time, in ones, over four days, whether they had been in that room on that night, and one of the eleven was in that room on that night, and he has the eleven written in his own hand in his coat in the order he asked them in and has not shown it to anybody and has not destroyed it. He has been asked to take the paper down twice and has not taken it down and says he is not going to. Nobody in that harbor has answered that paper. A woman of about fifty-eight who has six of the eleven in her own trade read the four lines off a slate because she cannot read the paper, and said that she was not going to tell them, and said why, and said who was going to tell them. A man of about thirty-four who lets that room out on a Sunday had not been asked anything about any of it. The far bench was occupied from ten until four and was not approached, and neither brother went near the other at any hour of the said day.**

*First column, entered on Saturday the fourth day of September next at about half past nine in the evening in that shed: that one hundred and sixty-eight pence came into that yard on the said day at the figure in chalk on a slate at an end stall, being a day's work with a boat and two men at a berth; and that a paper with four lines on it, a drawing-pin under a fold of oiled paper, and a strip of good paper about the length of an forearm, has been at the top left of the door of a room over a joiner's shop at the top of Fish Street since the thirty-first day of August last, and the four lines are that a lamp of about four inches was found burning in the above room on the night of the nineteenth of August last at eleven o'clock and there was nothing in that room with it; that on the days following eleven people of this harbor were asked one at a time whether they had been in that room on that night and one of the eleven was in that room on that night; that the person who found the lamp will not say which one of the eleven it was, and has been asked by two persons of this street to say, and has not said; and that anybody found to have been in that room on that night is to take himself, or herself, off out of this harbor before the twentieth day of September next; and that the man who put that paper up is a man of about forty-one called Walter Dunn, who is the night man at the fish market steps and has been for eleven years, and who was on a ladder in the passage behind that shop at about eleven o'clock on the said nineteenth of August with a shutter off his hand; and that the fanlight over the door of that passage has its top left pane out and has had it out since the spring; and that that room holds a table and two benches and a lamp on a nail and has no cupboard and no chest and no shelves and no box in it; and that the four lines on that paper name no person and no harbor and put no figure on them; and that on the night of the fourth of September next there was a mark made in chalk about two inches across on the door of that room under that paper, and that the person who made that mark was not found.*

*Second column, not checkable from a mole, entered on the said Saturday at about half past nine in the evening in that shed, and no name is against it: that the man of about forty-one put that paper up on the thirty-first of August past and not on the first of September, and that he corrected the day himself before anybody had read it, and that a man who puts a day on a paper has to have the day right; and that he asked eleven people one at a time over four days and each of them in front of nobody, and that the reason he wrote the eleven down and did not show them to anybody and did not destroy them is that writing them down and destroying them are two different things and he has thought about which of the two he was going to do for about a week; and that he has not put a name on that door and that the whole of what that paper is is a date at the end of the fourth line, and that a name on a door is a different kind of thing from a date on a door and cannot be taken off again; and that he has been asked to take that paper down twice and has said twice that he is not going to; and that a woman of about fifty-eight with six of the eleven in her own trade has read the four lines off another person's slate and has said that she is not going to tell them, and has given her reason, being that the day she tells six people that there is a paper on a door with eleven names behind it, every one of the six will spend the rest of their lives walking round that door; and that a person who cannot read is owed a reading of a book and not a reading of a notice, and that a notice on a door is for the person that door belongs to and this is not that door; and that the paper at the top left of that door and the notice of the seventeenth of May past are two different papers on two different doors in two different streets and neither of them cancels the other; and that the standing list entered in full on the fourteenth of August past is not entered in full on the said Saturday and stands, and no part of it was paid, reduced, advanced or improved on that day.*
