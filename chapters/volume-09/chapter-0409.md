# Chapter 409

*Wednesday the First Day of September, a Man of About Fifty-Three With a Bad Back Who Said a Number Out Loud in a Front Street on a Day He Had Named and Was Not Thanked for It and Was Not Paid for It, Three Hundred and Ninety-One Put Into the First Column of That Yard's Own Leaf With the Five Summands and the Line Count Written Under It So That a Stranger With a Leaf and a Pencil Can Take It Again, a Caulker of About Twenty-Three on the Flat of the Bench Stone at Half Past Three That Morning and Nobody in That Harbor on It With Him, and the Eighth Day of a Refusal on a Door in a Corridor on That Hill*

Wednesday the first day of September, in that shed, in the front street, and on the mole head.

**The said Wednesday was not a wage Tuesday and was not a rota day and was not a wipe, and the wall behind the reeve's bench came down and went up whole at the angle it has been at since the twelfth and nobody put it back. Nothing came into that yard on the said day and nothing went out of it, and there are two hundred and eighty-four pence in the bag, being one mark ten shillings and fourpence, and the figure that bag is short of the wage of four marks is three hundred and fifty-six pence, being two marks three shillings, and it is not going to be paid and nobody is to write one word under it saying anybody is going to. Nothing has been in a tin in that shed since the twenty-eighth day of April past, and a caulker of about twenty-three was on the flat of the bench stone at about half past three that morning because he cannot sleep, and nobody in that harbor was on that stone with him and he was not asked for that day by anybody and did not offer it and he is not going to be asked for it by anybody in that crew.**

**And the far bench was occupied on the said Wednesday from about ten in the morning until about four in the afternoon and was not approached on it, and the holder of that writ did not go over to him, and nothing was put on that bench and read as a gift, and neither brother has said why. Mara Venn was in that harbor and going nowhere, of no office, not on the crew, not restored, not forgiven, forty marks first after food, neither reduced nor paid, and she was in that shed from about four in the afternoon and did not go up the hill. Tobias Wren was on the crown of that mole from ten until four, being sixty-one, with eleven pieces of a boat behind him and a boat's hook in his right hand, and he was not sent for and not asked and not thanked, and he is not a teacher and not a door and not a sacrifice, and he is not the man on the far bench, and neither brother went near the other at any hour of the said day. The notice of the seventeenth of May past is on its wall in a different street for one hundred and seven days and has not been touched. The bellwright of that harbor has not been asked for one hundred and nine days, the chest is shut, the eight marks and a day of a boat have not been argued, and no digit of anything he ever said is on a page. The two objects of nobody's on that crown are four hundred and forty-seven days old and neither has moved, and the bench under that pump is bare for the two hundred and thirteenth night, and two lights at about a mile off that point have not moved in thirty-one days, and a man of about fifty-one has said nothing to anybody about a page of six names for forty-nine days and has been given nothing by anybody since the thirtieth of July, and the refusal on that door is eight days old.**

---

He was standing outside the door of that shed at about ten past nine in the morning with his hand over the small of his back and a stub of pencil in his right hand, and he had been there since about a quarter to nine, and Calder Marr saw him from the window and went out.

"I have said I would say it on a day," said Peat Marrow, before Calder had got the door shut. "This is a day. I have got it on this hand and I have said it out loud to myself four times this week and I am saying it out loud to you now and then I am going to go home, and I am not going to say it to you again and I am not going to say it twice, so take it now."

"Take what."

"The return. That sheet on the wall in the room off the west basin." He turned his left hand over. The palm was grey with pencil and there were five figures on it in two columns and they were not neat. "It is ruled for every day from the first of April to the twenty-third of August. That is one hundred and forty-five lines. Twenty-nine of them are struck across and nothing came in on those days. A hundred and sixteen of them carry a figure, and that is a hundred and sixteen days in five months and a bit that something was landed and entered at this harbor, and the figure is pieces, not weight, and there is a weight column on that sheet too that nobody has ever filled in and it is not mine to fill in and I did not."

"And the pieces."

Peat Marrow went down his own palm with his own thumb, slowly, the way a man reads out of a book he has read too many times.

"April. Twenty-six days out of thirty. Seventy-one."
"May. Twenty-eight out of thirty-one. Ninety-six."
"June. Twenty-one out of thirty. Seventy-nine."
"July. Twenty-five out of thirty-one. Eighty-eight."
"August to the twenty-third. Sixteen out of twenty-three. Fifty-seven."

"Say the sum."

"Seventy-one and ninety-six is a hundred and sixty-seven, and a hundred and sixty-seven and seventy-nine is two hundred and forty-six, and two hundred and forty-six and eighty-eight is three hundred and thirty-four, and three hundred and thirty-four and fifty-seven is three hundred and ninety-one," said Peat Marrow. "Three hundred and ninety-one pieces of recovered glass landed and entered at this harbor since the first of April, counted off one line at a time with a pencil, and there is a number in a clerk's hand at the head of each of the five blocks and I have checked my count of the lines against his five numbers and his five numbers are right, and I set every count of mine against the small one at the head of that block as I came to it and I came to all five of them over six afternoons. I counted the lines of April and of May twice because I did not trust a thumb on a bad back, and I counted the lines of the other three once, and I added every one of the hundred and sixteen figures exactly once, because I am not going to do it again and I am not going to start wanting it to be a different number."

"Why once."

"Because I am not going to do it again," he said. "I did it once and I have carried it and if I do it twice I will start to want it to be a different number and that is the day a man's arithmetic stops being a man's arithmetic."

Calder Marr did not thank him. He stood in the doorway of that shed and did not say one word about it, and that is the correct thing and it is not a coldness.

"Are you going to pay me for it," said Peat Marrow.

"No."

"Good," said Peat Marrow. "If you had said yes I would have had to think about it, and I have not got a bad back that is worth a week of my thinking. I will tell you what it was worth. It was worth about three hours of standing at a wall in a room I do not work in, on six afternoons, on my own time, with a pencil that is my own, and it bought me nothing at all and I would do it again on Saturday if the board was still up, and it is up, and I am not going to be in that room on a Saturday because Saturday is when I go and look at the third lay."

He went up the front street with his hand over his back and did not look back.

---

At about half past ten Calder Marr sat down in that shed with the leaf and put the count in the first column with the method under it, so that a stranger with a leaf and a pencil could take it again without adding anything, and he wrote it in his own hand and not in hers, because it was not her figure and she was not going to have his hand on it.

He wrote the five summands and under them the total and under the total he wrote the line count, being one hundred and forty-five lines from the first of April to the twenty-third of August, one hundred and sixteen of them carrying a figure, and twenty-nine struck across, and he wrote the words *off a return in the office on the west basin, counted one line at a time with a pencil at a wall, and the five figures at the head of the five blocks in the clerk's own hand are right.*

Then he read it back to himself once and did not alter it and did not put a name against it, and the reason there is no name against it is that the man who counted it did not give his name in the room where he did it and gave it in a front street on a day he had named in advance, and that is enough for a ledger and is not enough for a list.

---

The shed at about four in the afternoon had three people in it and the leaf was on the bench stone and the lamp was not lit because the lamp on that bench stone has not been lit since the fifth of May and the lens is out of the mole-head light and a barrel of lamp oil is four hundred and fifty pence with February's three hundred and seventy-eight above it on a board in the front street, and both figures are on that board and neither of them is going anywhere.

Nessa read it out. She read it in her own left hand and she read it whole and she read it twice and she gave the hour against it, which was about ten past four, and she read the five summands and then the total and then the line count, and she read the line count twice.

"Three hundred and ninety-one," she said, "and the figure that is is five numbers added together, and any person in this harbor with a pencil can take those five off a wall in a room off the west basin and add them and get that, and it does not depend on anybody's word at all, and it is the first figure this yard has had that does not."

"What is it a figure of," said Mara Venn.

"It is a figure of pieces of recovered glass landed and entered at that harbor since the first of April, counted off a municipal return, and it is not a figure of what is in the water and it is not a figure of what is in a shed and it is not a figure of what is under this town, and it is not a figure of anything anybody owns, because there is no name on it." Nessa put her finger on the line. "And a weight column on that sheet has never been filled in by anybody, and the reason against that line is that it is not this yard's sheet and a man with a bad back was not asked to fill in a column on a sheet that is not his and did not."

"Three hundred and ninety-one," Mara Venn said. "In five months and twenty-three days, at that harbor, on that reach, with about nine hundred people going through that market on a Saturday and four berths and one of them out."

"Three hundred and ninety-one," said Calder, "and that harbor lost half of about sixty tons of flour out of a transport of about a hundred and forty tons on the twenty-first of August, and nobody has laid a claim for a pound of it and nobody has been made whole, and there is no flour in that market and there is not going to be any this month." He put his thumb on the flat of the bench stone. "And those are both of them true and neither of them cancels the other, and I am not going to be asked to choose between them and neither is anybody else in this shed."

"I have not chosen," said Mara Venn. "I have counted. There is a difference and you know there is a difference and I have watched you know it four times this week."

---

At about six in the evening Calder Marr went up the hill.

The corridor was empty and the bench at the end of it had nothing on it and the table against the wall had nothing on it. There was a paper on the third door, at the corner, with a paper under it at the corner, and the top one was the original with its nine lines and its hour and its seal line, and the one under it was four lines in a clerk's own hand and it was eight days old and it said the same four things it had said on the twenty-fifth of August.

Tomorrow was the ninth day.

He stood in that corridor and read both papers and did not knock, and went back down the hill, and the door was shut behind the papers the whole time.

A caulker of about twenty-three was on the flat of the bench stone at about half past three that morning and nobody in that harbor was on it with him. A man of about fifty-three with a bad back said a number out loud in the front street of that harbor at about ten past nine in the morning, in his own mouth, on a day he had named in advance, being three hundred and ninety-one, off a return ruled for one hundred and forty-five days from the first of April to the twenty-three of August, with a hundred and sixteen of those days carrying a figure and twenty-nine struck across, being seventy-one, ninety-six, seventy-nine, eighty-eight and fifty-seven. He was not thanked and he was not paid and he said he would do it again on a Saturday and is not going to be in that room on a Saturday. That number went into the first column of that yard's own leaf in the holder's own hand with the five summands and the line count written under it and with no name against it, and it was read out in that shed at about ten past four in the afternoon with the hour against it. The refusal on that door is eight days old and the second of September is the ninth, and the holder of that writ went up that hill at about six in the evening, read both papers, and did not knock. The far bench was occupied from ten until four and was not approached, and neither brother went near the other at any hour of the said day.**

*First column, entered on Wednesday the first day of September next at about half past nine in the evening in that shed: that the said Wednesday was not a rota day and not a wage Tuesday and not a wipe; and that a caulker of about twenty-three was on the flat of the bench stone at about half past three in the morning and nobody in that harbor was on it with him and he was not asked for that day by anybody and did not offer it; and that a man of about fifty-three with a bad back said out loud in the front street of that harbor at about ten past nine in the morning, in his own mouth and on a day he had named on the Thursday before, that a sheet nailed to a wall in a room off the west basin is ruled for every day from the first of April to the twenty-three of August, being one hundred and forty-five lines, of which twenty-nine are struck across and one hundred and sixteen carry a figure; and that the figures on those hundred and sixteen lines are pieces and not weight, and that there is a weight column on that sheet which nobody has ever filled in; and that the five summands are seventy-one for April out of twenty-six lines out of thirty days, ninety-six for May out of twenty-eight out of thirty-one, seventy-nine for June out of twenty-one out of thirty, eighty-eight for July out of twenty-five out of thirty-one, and fifty-seven for August to the twenty-third out of sixteen out of twenty-three; and that the sum of the five is three hundred and ninety-one, and that the small figures in the hand of the clerk of about sixty at the head of the five blocks are the number of lines in each block that carry a figure and all five of them are right; and that the said man counted the lines of the first and second blocks twice and the lines of the other three once, and added each of the hundred and sixteen figures exactly once, and gave the reason for not going over the figures a second time, being that he was not going to start wanting it to be a different number; and that he was not thanked and was not paid and said he would do it again on a Saturday and was not going to be in that room on a Saturday; and that the said number was put into the first column of that leaf in the holder's own hand with the five summands and the line count written under it and with no name against it, and that it was read out in that shed at about ten past four in the afternoon with the hour against it, being that a stranger with a leaf and a pencil can take the five summands off a wall in a room off the west basin and add them and get three hundred and ninety-one without depending on anybody's word; and that the holder of that writ went up that hill at about six in the evening, read the first paper and the refusal under it, and did not knock, and that the refusal is eight days old and the second of September is the ninth.*
*Second column, not checkable from a mole, entered on the said Wednesday at about half past nine in the evening in that shed, and no name is against it: that the reason against the count being a first-column figure is that it is a count of lines and a sum of figures on a sheet, and not a report of what anybody said, and that a man on a mole can take it with a pencil, and that it is the first figure this yard has had that does not depend on anybody's word; and that there is no name against it because a man who counts a thing at a wall is not a man this yard is going to put on a list, and the day a yard writes down who turns up that yard has a list, and a list is the boat; and that three hundred and ninety-one pieces landed and entered is a figure of what came ashore in five months and is not a figure of what is in the water and not a figure of what is under this town and is not a figure of anything anybody owns; and that a woman of no office put the two of those side by side in that shed, being a count and a loss of about half of sixty tons of flour on the twenty-first of August with no claim laid and nobody made whole, and that neither of them cancels the other and that no choice between them is going to be asked for; and that a man who would do it again on a Saturday and is not going to be in that room on a Saturday is a man with a life and not a hand of this yard, and that this yard does not pay him and does not thank him and has not got a figure against him and is not going to; and that the reason against the fourth column entered on the twenty-eighth of August is entered once and is not entered again; and that the standing list entered in full on the fourteenth day of August is not entered here again and stands, and the whole of it stands unpaid and unmoved and no part of it was paid, reduced, advanced or improved on the said day.*
