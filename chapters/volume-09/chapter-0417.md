# Chapter 417

*Thursday the Ninth Day of September, the Eightieth Time the Cistern Was Charged at About Eight in the Morning and Walked on by None of It at About Half Past Nine on a Rota of a Monday and a Thursday, a Man of About Forty-Six Walking Down to the Third Lay at About Two in the Afternoon and Asking a Woman of About Sixty-One One Question and Not Asking Her a Second, Nine Marks a Year for a Room With a Rope Walk in the Back of It That Has Been Nine Marks for Eleven Years, a Caulker of About Twenty-Three Told on the Thursday the Thing He Asked to Be Told on the Thursday, and a Man Who Decides Not to Count a Volume Himself and Says Out Loud Why He Is Not Going To*

Thursday the ninth day of September, in that shed, on the third lay, and on that mole head.

**The said Thursday was not a wage Tuesday and was not a wipe and was a rota day, and the wall behind the reeve's bench came down and went up whole at the angle it has been at since the twelfth and nobody put it back. The cistern at the back of that market was charged at about eight in the morning and walked on by none of it at about half past nine, being the eightieth time, and the top name on the rota board is still off it. Nothing came into that yard on the said day and nothing went out of it, and there are four hundred and eight pence in the bag, being two marks seven shillings and fourpence, and the figure that bag is short of the wage of four marks is two hundred and thirty-two pence, being one mark six shillings, and it is not going to be paid and nobody is to write one word under it saying anybody is going to.**

**And the far bench was occupied on the said Thursday from about ten in the morning until about four in the afternoon and was not approached on it, and the holder of that writ did not go over to him, and nothing was put on that bench and read as a gift, and neither brother has said why. Mara Venn was in that harbor and going nowhere, of no office, not on the crew, not restored, not forgiven, forty marks first after food, neither reduced nor paid, and she was not in that shed on the said day. Tobias Wren was on the crown of that mole from ten until four, being sixty-one, with eleven pieces of a boat behind him and a boat's hook in his right hand, and on the said day he worked at the scarf of the eleventh piece with a piece of white chalk in his left hand, and he put the chalk mark and then he rubbed it out with his thumb and left the piece where it was. The notice of the seventeenth of May past is on its wall in a different street for one hundred and fifteen days and has not been touched. The bellwright of that harbor has not been asked for one hundred and seventeen days, the chest is shut, and the eight marks and a day of a boat have not been argued, and no digit of anything he ever said is on a page. The three nots standing against the eighth of June past are a tenth week in which nobody in that crew has said one word about them, and the hand of a boy of twenty-two is still at the head of the third column with a name beside it that has not been said out loud since the thirteenth of May past, and that name is not on any page of this harbor. The two objects of nobody's on that crown are four hundred and fifty-five days old and neither has moved. The bench under that pump is bare for the two hundred and twenty-first night. Two lights at about a mile off that point have not moved in thirty-nine days. A man of about fifty-one has said nothing to anybody about a page of six names for fifty-seven days. A nine-line sheet with a seal on it has been in that front street for twenty-six days and has been answered by nobody. The paper at the top left of that door in Fish Street is nine days old.**

---

Hobb Lee went down to the third lay at about two in the afternoon. It is a long way round by the market and a short way down a slip that comes out behind the second lay, and he went the long way, because the short way takes a man past the store and he had not told himself yet that he had a reason for not going past it.

Nell Carrow's room is on that lay and there is a rope walk in the back of it, about forty feet long and about five feet wide, with a handle at one end and a drum at the other, and it does not run, and there is not another rope walk in that harbor. She was in the doorway when he came up the slip with a bad knee and a stick, at about twenty past two.

"I have come about the Sunday," he said.

"You have come once," she said. "So say it once and then go away and do not come again about it."

He said it once. He said that the room was open at one o'clock on a Sunday, and that if she brought the thing to that table she could put it on it and look at it, and that about twenty people would be in the room, and that he would not ask her one word about it, and that he would not write one word about it, and that she could take it away again when she had finished with it.

"That is the whole of what you have come to say."

"There is one thing about the stairs," he said. "Two flights. I have carried a barrow of cod up them and I am telling you now that it is not a thing a person can do with a bad knee and a box, and there is a barrow in that joiner's yard with two handles and a wheel at the front that I can bring up at half past twelve on Sunday and stand at the top of with, and I will be the man at the top of it and you will be the woman at the bottom of it, and we will carry it up between us and nobody will be in the room when we do it."

"I have carried a barrow of wet hemp up a slip for forty-two years," said Nell Carrow. "I will manage a box and a stair."

"You will manage it, and it will take you an hour and a half, and you are going to be sitting on that bench at half past two in the afternoon instead of at half past one." He put his hands in his pockets. "I am not asking you to do anything you have not already said you would do. I am telling you about a barrow, and if you want the barrow it will be at the top of those stairs at half past twelve on Sunday, and if you do not want the barrow then I will not bring it, and those are the two things and both of them are all right."

"Bring the barrow," said Nell Carrow.

She asked him one question then, and it was the only question she asked him, and it was not about the box.

"Will they be in the room when I look at it."

"About twenty of them."

"Then bring the barrow," she said, "and I will be there at one o'clock and not before, and I am not going early to be helped in, and I am not going to be thanked at the top of that stair or at the bottom of it or anywhere else, and if anybody in that room says a kind thing to me about it I will walk out, and I would rather you told them now than me telling them at the top of that stair."

"I will tell them," said Hobb Lee, "and I will tell them that you said it and I will tell them that you said it on a Thursday and not on a Sunday, and I will not soften it, because a thing that is softened in a message is a thing that has been said twice."

The room is nine marks a year, being one thousand four hundred and forty pence, and it has been nine marks for eleven years and there is a rope walk in the back of it that does not run, and nobody has ever raised it, and nobody in that yard is going to raise it, and the nine marks came out of rope in every one of those eleven years and there is no figure in that yard's own leaf against it and there is not going to be one.

Isaac Fell was told on the Thursday afternoon that he was wanted on the Friday the tenth, at about five o'clock, at the head of that slip, and he listened to it and said that was the arrangement.

"You have been asking me on a Wednesday."

"I asked you yesterday."

"You asked me yesterday and I said I would want it said to me on the Thursday," said Fell, "and I have had it said to me on the Thursday, and that is the whole of what I wanted and it costs you nothing and it is the only thing in this harbor that has cost me nothing this year."

---

At about six in the evening Calder Marr was in that shed with the volume of that harbor's own leaf in front of him and a pencil in his hand, and he put the pencil down on the bench stone about two inches from the leaf and left it there.

"I am not going to count it," he said.

Nessa Pike did not look up. "Count what."

"That book over the joiner's. I have got a pencil and I have got a Sunday coming and I have got about twenty people who are going to be in that room, and if I go in there on Saturday morning with a pencil and sit down and go down that volume and count the fourth lines, then whatever I find, I found it, and I found it because a panel with a person in it is on shelf four of a store on the second lay and I have been in nine hundred places in this harbor looking for things and I did not like what I saw, and a man with a grievance and a pencil is not a count, it is a complaint with a number at the end of it."

"It is a count. You are the man who put three hundred and ninety-one in the first column with the summands under it and a stranger can take it again without asking anybody a word, and that was a man with a grievance counting a thing that did him no good at all."

"That was a municipal return in an office off the west basin and a man of about fifty-three with a bad back who counted it on his own time in five afternoons and nobody sent him and nobody paid him and nobody thanked him, and the figure went in with no name against it and it is in no mouth and it is on no wall." He turned the leaf over. "If I count that volume I will be a man with a stake counting a thing that is against his own interest and he will be a man with a stake with the same pencil, and one of the two of us will be doing the count to get a result and the other one will be doing it because he cannot help it, and the one doing it to get a result is the one everybody will listen to."

"Then who is going to count it."

"Somebody who walks in off a street on a Sunday because there is nothing else to do, and who has a pencil, and who has not got a stake in a panel on a shelf on the second lay, and who does not know that there is a dash at the end of a third line anywhere in this harbor." He put both hands flat on the bench stone. "And I am not going to plant him. I am not going to stand in Fish Street on Saturday and ask a man to come and read that volume. If a man does not come, then nobody comes, and that is what it costs to do it properly and it is cheaper than doing it the other way. And if somebody does come, and picks the volume up off that table because it is the only thing in the room with writing on it, and reads it, and counts something with a pencil on the back of his own hand because he counts things for a living, then that is a finding and not an instruction, and there is no way anybody can get at it afterwards and say it was arranged. And I want that. I want it so badly that I have put the pencil down on this stone rather than pick it up."

"You have put it down two inches away."

"I have put it down two inches away," said Calder Marr, "and it is going to stay two inches away, and on Sunday I am going to be in that room and I am not going to watch anybody's hands, and if I see a man count something on the back of his own hand I am going to go outside and stand in that street until he has finished and I am not going to be the first person in that room to know."

Nessa Pike wrote in the second column, against a reason, that the holder of that writ did not count a public volume with a pencil in his own hand on the Saturday and gave his reason in his own mouth, being that a man with a stake is not a count and is a complaint with a number at the end of it, and that he put the pencil down two inches from that yard's own leaf and left it there.

**The cistern was charged at about eight in the morning on the said Thursday and walked on by none of it at about half past nine, being the eightieth time, and the top name on the rota board is still off it. Nothing came into that yard on the said day and nothing went out of it, and the bag is two marks seven shillings and fourpence, and it is short of that wage by one mark six shillings. A man of about forty-six walked down to the third lay on the said afternoon and asked a woman of about sixty-one one question about a Sunday, and was asked one back, and neither of them asked the other a second thing, and the woman said that she would be there at one o'clock and not before and that she is not going to be thanked at the top of that stair or at the bottom of it, and that if anybody in that room says a kind thing to her about it she will walk out, and the man said he would tell them that she had said so on a Thursday and would not soften it. Her room is nine marks a year and it has been nine marks for eleven years and there is a rope walk in the back of it that does not run, and nothing in that yard has got a figure against it. A caulker of about twenty-three was told on the said Thursday that he was wanted on the Friday, which is what he asked for. The holder of that writ said out loud in that shed on the evening of the said day that he was not going to count a public volume with a pencil in his own hand, and gave his reason, and put the pencil down on the bench stone two inches from that yard's own leaf and left it there. The far bench was occupied from ten until four and was not approached, and neither brother went near the other at any hour of the said day.**

*First column, entered on Thursday the ninth day of September next at about half past nine in the evening in that shed: that the cistern was charged at about eight in the morning and walked on by none of it at about half past nine on the said Thursday, being the eightieth time, and that the rota of that cistern is a Monday and a Thursday and that the top name on that board is still off it; and that a man of about forty-six walked down to the third lay on the said afternoon and asked a woman of about sixty-one of that lay one question, being whether she would bring a thing out of a box in her back room to a long table in a room over a joiner's shop on the Sunday the twelfth of September next at one o'clock and put it on that table and look at it with about twenty people in the room; and that he offered her a barrow and a hand at the top of two flights of stairs at half past twelve on that Sunday and did not offer again; and that she asked him one question, being whether there would be people in the room when she looked at it, and was told that there would be about twenty; and that she said that she would be there at one o'clock and not before and that she is not going early to be helped in and that she is not going to be thanked at the top of that stair or at the bottom of it or anywhere else, and that if anybody in that room says a kind thing to her about it she will walk out; and that the man of about forty-six said he would tell them so and would not soften it; and that no second question was asked her by anybody in that yard on the said day; and that her room on that lay is nine marks a year, being one thousand four hundred and forty pence, and has been nine marks for eleven years, and that there is a rope walk of about forty feet in the back of it that does not run; and that a caulker of about twenty-three was told on the said day that he was wanted on the Friday the tenth, which is what he asked to be told and when he asked to be told it; and that the holder of that writ said out loud in that shed at about six in the afternoon on the said day that he was not going to count a public volume with a pencil in his own hand and gave the reason.*

*Second column, not checkable from a mole, entered on the said Thursday at about half past nine in the evening in that shed, and no name is against it: that the reason the holder of that writ did not count the head-notes of a public volume with a pencil in his own hand is in his own mouth and is that a man with a stake is not a count and is a complaint with a number at the end of it, and that a man of about fifty-three with a bad back who counted a municipal return on his own time in five afternoons was a different thing, and that he would not stand in a street and ask a man to come and read that volume, and that if nobody comes then nobody comes; and that the reason a woman of about sixty-one of that lay is not going to be thanked at the top of a stair is in her own mouth and is that a kind thing said to a person about a thing they made with their own hands at about nineteen is a thing she would have to stand there and take in front of about twenty people; and that the two flights of stairs from the bottom of that slip to the top of that room are the whole of what a bad knee and a box have to do on the Sunday, and that a barrow with two handles is a thing a man can bring up at half past twelve and stand at the top of, and that no figure in that yard's own leaf stands against the two flights or against the barrow; and that the standing list entered in full on the fourteenth of August past is not entered in full on the said Thursday and stands, and no part of it was paid, reduced, advanced or improved on that day.*
