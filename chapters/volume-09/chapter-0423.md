# Chapter 423

*Wednesday the Fifteenth Day of September, a Sheet of Six Lines With a Date at the Head of It and a Charge at the Foot of It Going Up on a Wall at the Back of an End Stall at Ten in the Morning on the Outside of a Figure of Four Hundred and Fifty Pence That Was Not Taken Down First and Cannot Be Taken Off Again, a Man of About Thirty-Four Who Says in His Own Mouth That He Was Given the Lot at the Top of a Gangway and Was Not Asked His Name and Could Not Say That What Was On It Was a Lie, and About Thirty People Standing in Front of That Wall by Twelve o'Clock and Not One of Them Knocking It Down*

Wednesday the fifteenth day of September, in that market, in that street, in that shed, and on that mole head.

**The said Wednesday was not a wage Tuesday and was not a rota day and was not a wipe, and the wall behind the reeve's bench came down and went up whole at the angle it has been at since the twelfth and nobody put it back. Nothing came into that yard on the said day and nothing went out of it, and there are five hundred and seventy-six pence in the bag, being three marks eight shillings, and the figure that bag is short of the wage of four marks is sixty-four pence, being five shillings and fourpence, and it is not going to be paid and nobody is to write one word under it saying anybody is going to.**

**And the far bench was occupied on the said Wednesday from about ten in the morning until about four in the afternoon and was not approached on it, and the holder of that writ did not go over to him, and nothing was put on that bench and read as a gift, and neither brother has said why. Mara Venn was in that harbor and going nowhere, of no office, not on the crew, not restored, not forgiven, forty marks first after food, neither reduced nor paid, and she was in that front street at about eleven in the morning and read what is set out below off that wall with about nine people standing near her. Tobias Wren was on the crown of that mole from ten until four, being sixty-one, with eleven pieces of a boat behind him and a boat's hook in his right hand, and on the said day he was sat on the seventh piece with his back to the water and the hook across his knees and he did not touch it between ten and four. The notice of the seventeenth of May past is on its wall in a different street for one hundred and twenty-one days and has not been touched. The bellwright of that harbor has not been asked for one hundred and twenty-three days, the chest is shut, and the eight marks and a day of a boat have not been argued, and no digit of anything he ever said is on a page. The three nots standing against the eighth of June past are an eleventh week in which nobody in that crew has said one word about them, and the hand of a boy of twenty-two is still at the head of the third column with a name beside it that has not been said out loud since the thirteenth of May past, and that name is not on any page of this harbor. The two objects of nobody's on that crown are four hundred and sixty-one days old and neither has moved. The bench under that pump is bare for the two hundred and twenty-seventh night. Two lights at about a mile off that point have not moved in forty-five days. A man of about fifty-one has said nothing to anybody about a page of six names for sixty-three days. A nine-line sheet with a seal on it has been in that front street for thirty-two days and has been answered by nobody. The paper at the top left of that door in Fish Street is fifteen days old and has been answered by nobody, and the fourth line of it ends on the twentieth day of September next, which is five days off.**

---

At about ten in the morning the man of about thirty-four came back up the quay from the packet and got the sack off the stall and carried it twenty feet and put it down on the stones at the back of the end stall where the woman of about fifty-eight stands.

The wall at the back of that stall is not the wall the notice of the seventeenth of May past is on, and it is not the wall the nine-line sheet with a seal went up on in the middle of August, and it is not the wall in Fish Street. On that wall there is one thing, and it is a slate about two foot by three hanging on a nail, and there is a ruled line under every figure on it, and the top figure is four hundred and fifty pence with the first of June against it, and February's three hundred and seventy-eight pence is standing visible above it with the first of February against it, and there has been no barrel of lamp oil in this harbor since the twenty-ninth of August.

The figure was not taken down first. Nobody asked anybody to take it down.

The man of about thirty-four took one sheet out of the sack and pasted it on the wall above the slate and about four inches to the left of it, at about ten in the morning, and it is nine lines long and it is printed, and there is nothing on the back of it.

---

*Eighth day of May.*

*A piece of worked glass that has had a scene taken from a living person in it keeps what the scene was, and what the scene was stands off the piece into the air of a room the piece is in, and it settles on the people that are in that room, and it is taken home in the clothes, and it is not a thing that comes off in an afternoon at a tub.*

*It is not a fever and it is not a disease, and no physic has ever charged for it, and no man keeps it by lying down, and it will sit in a market the same as it sits in a room, because the piece does not care what it is stood in front of.*

*Therefore every person who has been in a room with such a piece, or carried one, or stood within a man's arm's length of one on a table, is a person who has had it, and is to be entered in the claim book at the counter of the harbor authority of the town where he stands, under his own name, and is not to be at work on glass in that town until he has been entered and the entry has been read out to him where he can hear it.*

*A book is not a court. A claim in a book decides nothing, and a man may say no in it and his no goes in at the same size as everybody else's, and a claim is a claim and not a verdict and not a finding out.*

*No person is named in this sheet and no town is named in this sheet, and it is put up on the outside of this figure so that any town that has had glass landed on it may know what it has in it.*

*The charge for lodging one claim is one hundred and sixty-eight pence, being one mark and eightpence, and a claim so lodged is read out in the room where the public book of that town is kept, on a Sunday, by a man at the end of the table, and a man who cannot read may be there and hear it.*

---

He looked at it the way a man looks at a thing he has done that he is not sure of, and then he looked at his own boots for a while.

"Was there anything in the sack worth money to anybody," said the woman of about fifty-eight.

"Paper."

"Then it is not my sack," she said, "and I will not have it on my counter, and if it has to stand somewhere it will stand against my own back board and not on the counter where people put their hands."

She had it carried three feet and set down against the boards at the back of her stall with a stone on it, and that is where it stood, and she has not put her hand on it since.

The man of about thirty-four stood in front of his own work for about a minute and a half and then said what he had come to say and would not say more.

"That is the whole of it," he said. "Six lines and a date and a charge. I did not write it and I am not going to say who did and you are not going to get it out of me by asking me twice. It was given to me at the top of the gangway by a man behind a counter with a book open in front of him, and he did not ask my name, and he did not ask what it was for, and he did not ask who it was for, and I have been carrying it four hundred miles."

"Did you read it."

"I read the first four lines standing at that counter and I have not been able to put them down since," said the man of about thirty-four. "I have been in this harbor nine hours. I am going out on the tide at about five and I am not going to be asked about it again by anybody in this harbor, and I am telling you that now so that nobody stands about on a quay all afternoon waiting to catch me at it."

"Can you say that it is a lie."

"No," he said. "And that is the whole of what I have got. If you want it said that it is a lie, you will have to say it yourself, and you will have to have read it, and I have not got anybody for that."

He went up the quay and the packet's hook was up before the middle of the day and she went out on the evening tide, and the sack of the rest of the lot is standing against the back of that stall with a stone on it and nobody in that harbor has opened it.

---

The woman of about fifty-eight came out of her stall at about half past ten and looked at the wall and then took hold of the slate and turned it about so that the four hundred and fifty and the three hundred and seventy-eight and the two dates were showing again.

"That figure is not coming off," she said, to nobody in particular. "It has not been on my wall since the first of June and it is not coming off for a piece of paper with a date on the top of it, and whoever printed that has put it on my wall on purpose, because there is nothing else on this wall."

She stood in front of the two of them together for a while with her hands behind her back.

"In August I wrote one word on the edge of that board and I would not rule a line under the oil figure, and the reason I gave was that a line with one word on it under a figure goes up this street in about an hour. Now there is a sheet up there telling me what is in the air of a room, and I have got no chalk answer to that and I am not going to write one, because whatever I write on this wall goes out of that wall in a morning."

She wrote nothing new in chalk. The one word she wrote on the edge of that slate is the one she wrote in August and it can be rubbed out in about a second, and she has not let anybody rub it out.

By half past eleven there were about a dozen of them and by twelve o'clock there were about thirty.

---

A man of about thirty-three who has handled glass out of wrecks for forty years and has a hand like a winch said, loud enough for the front of that crowd:

"That first part is a lie. There are men in this harbor who have had glass in their hands since they were boys and there is not one of us with anything. That is a lie and I will say so to anybody."

A woman standing beside him said: "I was in the room."

"Out of a room," he said.

"You were not in the room," said the woman. "There were about twenty people in that room on the Sunday and about eleven of them could not read a word, and I can tell you exactly how many of those twenty were standing in front of a table at about half past three, because I counted them twice going down the stair afterwards, and you were not one of them."

The man of about thirty-three stood there with his hand half raised.

"You have counted people going down a stair," he said. "That is not the same as saying a piece of glass can do a thing to a room."

"No," said the woman. "It is not. And I am not going to stand here in front of thirty people and say a thing I do not know either. You have got a thing you are sure of and I have got a thing I am sure of and neither one of us can get at the other one, and I am not going to pretend to you that mine is bigger because I am the one who was upstairs."

And there was no answer to that on that wall at twelve o'clock in the afternoon, and there was no answer to it in that market for the rest of that day, and nobody put a hand on that sheet at any hour of it.

---

Calder Marr read it twice, standing at about four feet off it with his hands at his sides.

The first time he read it as a man looking for a name and there was not one, and he went back four inches and read it again as a man looking for a town and there was not one, and then he stood in front of the top of it for a while with his mouth moving and his hands in his pockets.

The date at the head of it is the eighth of May and he read that line a good many times and did not go up the hill.

There was a paper in the inside pocket of his coat and he did not take it out, and he put his hand flat on the stones under that sheet at one point, as a man does who has been standing a long while, and then he took it off again and left no mark.

He did not tell anybody in that market that there was a paper in the inside pocket of his coat.

**A sheet of nine lines went up on a wall at the back of an end stall in that front street at about ten in the morning on the said Wednesday, on the outside of a slate that carries a figure of four hundred and fifty pence with February's three hundred and seventy-eight pence standing visible above it and both dates on it, and that figure was not taken down first and was not taken down and cannot be taken off again, and there has been no barrel of lamp oil in that harbor since the twenty-ninth day of August. The sheet has a date at the head of it and six lines and a charge at the foot of it and names no person and no town and no office. A man of about thirty-four off a screw-steam packet out of Bramble Strait pasted it up and said out loud that he did not write it, that it was given to him at the top of a gangway by a man behind a counter with a book open who did not ask his name and did not ask what it was for, that he read the first four lines standing at a counter four hundred miles from here and has not been able to put them down since, that he could not say it was a lie, and that he was not going to be asked about it a second time. About thirty people stood in front of that wall by twelve o'clock and a man of about thirty-three said the first of the six lines was a lie and a woman beside him said not from glass but from a room and said she had been in it, and he had not, and there was no answer to that on that wall that day, and nobody put a hand on the sheet. The woman of about fifty-eight turned her slate about so that the oil figure was showing again and said the figure was not coming off for a piece of paper with a date on the top of it. Nothing came into that yard on the said Wednesday and nothing went out of it, and the bag is five hundred and seventy-six pence, being three marks eight shillings, and is short of the wage of four marks by sixty-four pence, being five shillings and fourpence. The paper at the top left of that door in Fish Street is fifteen days old and its fourth line ends on the twentieth of September next and has been answered by nobody. The far bench was occupied from ten until four and was not approached, and neither brother went near the other at any hour of the said day.**

*Second column, not checkable from a mole, entered on the said Wednesday at about half past nine in the evening in that shed, and no name is against it: that the reason a man of about thirty-four could not say that the sheet on that wall was a lie is in his own mouth and is that he read the first four lines of it standing at a counter four hundred miles from that harbor and has not been able to put them down since, and that a man who has read a thing and cannot put it down is not a man who can go up a street and deny it; and that the reason the figure of four hundred and fifty pence was not taken off that slate before the sheet went up on the outside of it is that a figure put on a board cannot be taken off again, and that the wall at the back of that stall had that figure on it and nothing else on it, and that whoever printed the sheet put it there on purpose and said so on the face of the sheet in one of its six lines; and that the reason no name is against the sheet in this yard's leaf is that this yard did not put it up, this harbor's answer to it is not owed anybody and no figure has been paid to anybody to put it up, and that the man who did put it up has said that he was given it at the top of a gangway by a man behind a counter with a book open; and that the reason the first column of that leaf takes an entry against a wall in that front street and does not take the contents of the sheet is that a stranger with a pencil can see that a sheet is on a wall and cannot take anything off the inside of another person's paper without reading it, and that this yard is not going to copy a sheet into a ledger and call that a record; and that the standing list entered in full on the fourteenth of August past is not entered in full on the said Wednesday and stands, and no part of it was paid, reduced, advanced or improved on that day.*
