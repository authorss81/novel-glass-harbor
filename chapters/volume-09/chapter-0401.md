# Chapter 401

*Tuesday the Twenty-Fourth of August, the Forty-Fourth Wage Tuesday and the Column Not Moved on It and Forty-Four Weeks Standing Fallen Due With No Envelope Against Any of Them, a Knock Put on a Door in a Corridor on That Hill Where on the Last Evening of the Last Month Nobody Knocked, an Hour With a Man of About Forty of That Office in the Room the Whole of It and a Woman of No Office in It With Him, a Fragment About Nine Inches by Six Whose Edge Was Cut and Not Broken, a Seam in It That Says Two Things at Once and Will Not Say Which of the Two Is the One They Came In to Look At, Three People Going Out of a Room Carrying Something in Their Bodies and Nothing at All Going Out of That Room in Any Hand, and a Request Made in a Corridor at Ten Past Eleven in the Morning for Another Hour of the Same Thing*

Tuesday the twenty-fourth of August, in that shed, in a corridor of that office on that hill, and in a room at the back of that corridor.

**The said Tuesday was a wage Tuesday and was not a rota day and was not a wipe, and the wall behind the reeve's bench came down and went up whole at the angle it has been at since the twelfth and nobody put it back. Nothing came into that yard on the said day and nothing went out of it, and there are one hundred and sixty pence in the bag, being one mark, and the figure that bag is short of the wage of four marks is four hundred and eighty pence, being three marks, and it is not going to be paid and nobody is to write one word under it saying anybody is going to. Nothing has been in a tin in that shed since the twenty-eighth day of April past. The wage column is at twenty-six lines and one hundred and four marks with twenty envelopes on a bench in a chest, and the said Tuesday was the forty-fourth week, and it did not move on it, so that forty-four weeks stand fallen due with no envelope against any of them, and nobody in that harbor has any idea what this yard is going to do about the forty-fifth and that is the yard's own business.**

**And the far bench was occupied on the said Tuesday from about ten in the morning until about four in the afternoon and was not approached on it, and the holder of that writ did not go over to him, and nothing was put on that bench and read as a gift, and neither brother has said why. Mara Venn was in that harbor and going nowhere, of no office, not on the crew, not restored, not forgiven, forty marks first after food, neither reduced nor paid, and she was in a room on that hill from ten until eleven in the morning because a paper on a door in a corridor of that building names her as permitted at that hour, and she was not thanked for going and nobody thanked her for staying. Tobias Wren was on the crown of that mole from ten until four, being sixty-one, with eleven pieces of a boat behind him and a boat's hook in his right hand, and he was not sent for and not asked and not thanked, and he is not a teacher and not a door and not a sacrifice, and he is not the man on the far bench, and neither brother went near the other at any hour of the said day. The notice of the seventeenth of May past is on its wall in a different street for ninety-nine days and has not been touched and has not been taken down. The bellwright of that harbor has not been asked for one hundred and one days, the chest is shut, the eight marks and a day of a boat have not been argued, and no digit of anything he ever said is on a page. The two objects of nobody's on the crown of that mole are four hundred and thirty-nine days old and neither has moved, and the bench under that pump is bare for the two hundred and fifth night, and a man of about fifty-one has said nothing to anybody about a page of six names for forty-one days and has been given nothing by anybody since the thirtieth of July. Two lights at about a mile off that point have not moved in twenty-three days, counted from the hour they were first seen on the evening of the first of August past, and the light on the mole head is not lit and has not been since the seventh of May past.**

---

He went up the hill at about twenty to ten with nothing in his hands. Not the leaf. He had left the leaf in that shed under the bench stone at about ten to seven because a leaf does not go up a hill into a building that holds other people's things, and he had thought about that for a day and a half and had not said the thought out loud to anybody.

The corridor was empty. There is a bench at the end of it and there is a table against the wall and there are three doors, and two of them are open and the third one is shut, and the paper was still on the third one at the corner where it had been for a day or two, and nobody in that building had taken it down and he had not taken it down.

He read it again. Nine lines. Three names. An hour, a place and an arrival. Three lines under them. And at the foot of it the seal line, which is not the shape of any other seal line that yard has seen on the face of a paper, because the office at the end of it is not one that has ever sent this yard anything. He read the seal line through this time as well and it told him no more than it told him on Monday evening, and there is no more in it, and that is the whole of what a seal line is.

Then he knocked.

It took him a moment to do it. His hand went to the wood and stayed there about a second and a half before the knuckles did anything, and if anybody in that building had been listening through the door they would have heard both halves of that.

---

The door came in about two inches and then the rest of the way, and the man behind it was about forty and had a jacket with the elbows gone at both of them and a piece of pencil behind one ear and a flat cloth in his hand, and he did not say good morning and he did not say anything at all, and he turned and went into the room and left the door standing open behind him, which was either an instruction or an absence of one.

He was not asked anything. Nobody in that harbor had ever seen him before. He was a copying clerk of that office and that was on the paper in three lines, and everything else about him was what he did with his hands in the next fifty-eight minutes.

There are three things in that room and they are on three shelves and there is a number painted on the front edge of each shelf, and the holder of that writ wrote none of the three down and asked for none of the three, and he is not going to ask for any of them, and that is the whole of what that number is for.

The far shelf is against the far wall. The middle shelf is on the left-hand wall between the door and the far wall. The near shelf is under the window and the window has a board across the outside of it.

The clerk went to the near shelf first, not the far one, and lifted a wooden cover off a flat case and set it down on the table against the wall, and then went back and lifted the cover off the far one, and then came and stood about six feet off the case with the cloth in his hand and waited, and did not look at the door.

Calder went past the middle shelf. There is a book on the wall above it with a claim in it that has been in a book in that office since the December before last, and he did not turn his head at the shelf or at the book, and the clerk did not turn his either, and the whole hour was spent at the far one.

---

The thing on the cloth was about nine inches by six.

The first thing about it was the edge, and Calder spent the first ten minutes of the hour on the edge and was glad of it, because the edge was the plainest thing in the room.

"A break," he said, to nobody in particular, and to Mara, who was standing on his left with her hands behind her back. "You can tell it. A break goes through and it goes out in a cone and the cone has a length to it, and you can put your thumb in it and you can feel where it started. That has been cut. Somebody put an edge on it, on a wheel or on a stone, and they took their time, and the arris is still on it and there is no chipping anywhere along nine inches of it. That is a thing somebody took out of a hull and dressed on a bench and not a thing a sea did."

"A thing a sea did," Mara Venn said.

"A thing a sea did would have nine inches of chipping in it and a thumb-print of a cone in it and it would be a different colour where it was fresh," he said. "It is not. It was cut."

The clerk came at about a quarter past ten and put a lamp on the table against the wall without being asked and without speaking, and the lamp was not a flame and gave a white light, and he went back to his six feet.

Under a white light the thing is not one colour. That is the whole trouble with recovered glass and every man who has ever sold any has found it out. There is green in it that goes to nothing at about a third of the way down, and a band of it that is the colour of the sea off a hard in March, and a place in the upper corner where it is the colour of nothing anybody can name and where a man who works with it every day of his life will not look for more than about a minute at a stretch.

And across it, from about a third of the way in on the left to about a third of the way in on the right and about two thirds of the way up, there was a line.

It was not a crack. Calder had been in a bell over a wreck at the south end in June with two other men in it, and he knew what a crack in this looks like from the inside, and it does not run like that. It ran across, and it did not run across so much as it *changed*, and where it changed the two sides of it are at a slightly different depth and the white light lands on them about a hair's breadth apart, and the difference in the way the two edges of the light are printed is visible from about a foot off it and from any angle at all, and that is the whole of what there is to see at first.

He put his thumb on the cloth on either side of it and did not touch the thing itself, because he had been told nothing about touching and had decided on his own account not to.

"There is a seam," he said.

Mara Venn did not move her head. "Say the rest of it."

"There are two," he said. "That is what a seam is where glass has been made and worked and worked again. It is not damage. It is not a fault. It is where one maker's pressure met another maker's pressure and neither of them won all the way, and the line is the place where they agreed to differ. It is the only honest mark in recovered work and it is worth more to me than a name."

"And which one of the two is the one we came in to look at."

"I cannot tell you," Calder said. "And I am not going to guess in this room, and if I did guess you would carry it out of here and put it in a column and there would be a figure on a leaf in this harbor that is a man's opinion with a Tuesday on it."

"Then what can you tell me."

"That two people made it and that they did not make it at the same time and that they were not in the same room and that they were both frightened, and I cannot tell you which of the two was in the room this thing was cut in and which of the two was somewhere else entirely while it was being cut."

Mara Venn was quiet for about four seconds, which on that room was a long time.

"That is not an answer," she said.

"It is the only one I have," he said, "and I would rather bring you that than bring you a guess, and both of those are true and neither of them cancels the other."

---

At about twenty to eleven the clerk said the hour, in his own mouth, in five words, and did not raise his voice.

Calder took his hands off the cloth and stepped back and the clerk came in and put the cover on and lifted the case and carried it the four paces to the far shelf and set it down on the same shelf, on the same place on the shelf, under the number that was painted on the front edge of it in a hand that had nothing to do with the hand that painted the numbers on the other two.

Nothing went out of that room in anybody's hand. Calder went in with his hands empty and came out with his hands empty. Mara Venn went in with her hands empty and came out with her hands empty. The paper came off the door and went into Calder's own coat in the corridor, and it is a copy of a paper and not the paper, and that is what a copy is.

---

And then, in the corridor, at about ten past eleven, with the door shut and the clerk inside it doing whatever a clerk does at ten past eleven on a Tuesday:

"I want an hour again," Calder said. "The same hour. The same thing out of the three. The same three lines on the paper."

Drury had come out of the room behind him without being called and was standing at the table against the wall with the flat cloth over his arm.

"I will write on the door," he said.

"That is a refusal."

"It is a refusal," said Saul Drury, "and it is not mine and I am not going to tell you whose it is, because you have not asked me and I am not going to be asked. I will write it on the door and you will come and read it, and it will be there in the morning, and it will be in my hand, and you will know my hand because you watched me rule a line at ten past ten this morning with the same pencil that is behind my ear."

He went into the room and shut the door.

Calder stood in that corridor and read the paper he had in his coat a second time and thought about a man with a bad back at a door and a pencil behind his ear, and about a woman of fifty-eight with a slate in a market, and about a woman of no office standing in a room with her hands behind her back asking him for an answer he had not got, and he thought about how many things in this harbor were being counted by one person standing up.

---

At about four in the afternoon he was on the crown of that mole and Mara Venn came up the slip and stood four feet off the flat of the bench stone and neither of them sat down.

"I have got something," she said, and she said it in her own mouth and she did not ask him whether he had.

"Yes," Calder said.

"I am not going to tell you what it is, and you are not going to tell me what yours is, and I want that understood before either of us starts."

"That is understood."

"And it is not the forty marks and it is not going to reduce the forty marks by a penny and it is not going to be paid, and I am not going to be thanked for it and I am not asking you to thank me." She was looking at the water and not at him. "And I am not going up that hill again on a Thursday until there is a paper on that door that says a different hour than the one you asked for, and if there is not one by the end of the month then that is the end of me going, and you can have that in your own head and you cannot have it in a column."

"Nobody is going to put that in a column."

"You did not hear me say so," said Mara Venn.

---

**Nothing came into that yard on the said Tuesday and nothing went out of it, and there are one hundred and sixty pence in the bag, being one mark, and the figure that bag is short of the wage of four marks is four hundred and eighty pence, being three marks, and it is not going to be paid and nobody is to write one word under it saying anybody is going to pay it. The far bench was occupied from ten until four and was not approached, and the holder of that writ did not go over to him, and neither brother went near the other at any hour of the said day. A door on that hill was opened by a man of about forty who is a copying clerk of that office and who is doing a job, and he was not asked anything about the office he works in and was not thanked, and nobody in that harbor had ever seen him until that morning. A fragment about nine inches by six was looked at for the better part of an hour under a white light that a clerk put down without being asked, and it has an edge that was cut and not broken and a seam in it, and the seam says that two people made it and that the two of them were both frightened, and it does not say which of the two was in the room it was cut in. It went back on the far shelf under the number it was on. Nothing went out of that room in anybody's hand, and three people came out of it carrying something that was not in their hands, and a request for another hour of the same thing was made in a corridor at ten past eleven in the morning and was refused in a clerk's own mouth in nine words.**

*First column, entered on Tuesday the twenty-fourth day of August next at about half past nine in the evening in that shed: that the said Tuesday was the forty-fourth wage Tuesday and the column did not move on it, and that forty-four weeks stand fallen due with no envelope against any of them; and that the holder of that writ went up that hill at about twenty to ten in the morning with his hands empty and knocked on a shut door in a corridor, having not knocked on it on the Monday evening of the twenty-third of August when he stood in that same corridor and read nine lines twice; and that the door was opened by a man of about forty who is a copying clerk of that office and who said nothing at all and left the door standing open; and that the hour named on the paper ran from ten in the morning to the hour, being about twenty to eleven, and that a clerk of that office was in that room the whole of it; and that there are three things in that room on three shelves and that the one on the far shelf is the one the paper named and that it is about nine inches by six and that its edge over nine inches is cut and not broken and that the arris is still on it and that there is no chipping anywhere along it; and that it has a line across it at about a third of the way up which is a seam and not a crack, and that a seam is where one maker's pressure met another maker's pressure and neither won all the way; and that the holder said in that room that there are two and that he cannot say which of the two was in the room it was cut in and that he refused to guess in that room, and that the woman of no office said that that was not an answer and that the holder said that it was the only one he had; and that the line of the seam runs from about a third of the way in on the left to about a third of the way in on the right and about two thirds of the way up; and that the holder went past the middle shelf and did not turn his head at it or at the book above it, and that the clerk did not turn his either, and that the whole hour was spent at the far one; and that the thing was put back on the far shelf on the same place on that shelf under the number painted on the front edge of it; and that the woman of no office came up the slip to that mole at about four in the afternoon and said in her own mouth that she had got something and did not ask him whether he had and was not going to tell him what it was, and that the holder said yes; and that a request for another hour of the same thing was made in that corridor at about ten past eleven in the morning and that a copying clerk of that office said in nine words that he would write it on the door.*

*Second column, not checkable from a mole, entered on the said Tuesday at about half past nine in the evening in that shed, and no name is against it: that a seam is a difference and not a truth and does not settle an argument and does not name anybody and convicted nobody in that room and settled nothing in that room, and that the holder said in his own mouth in that corridor that he would rather bring a woman a thing he cannot do than a guess, and that a guess made in a room with a clerk in it comes out of that room as a fact because it is in the mouth of two people who were both there; and that an hour named on a paper is the end of an hour and not the middle of one, and that the holder found it out inside it, and that the whole of what that party won on the said morning was a door and an hour and a look at one thing out of three, and that a stable copy marked with where a thing came from cannot be made in an hour, and that the man who runs that bench in that building knows that and did not say it; and that three people came out of that room carrying something in their bodies and that nothing went out of it in anybody's hand, and that both of those are facts and neither of them is a record and neither of them can be put on a leaf where a stranger with a pencil could take it, and that a thing that cannot be taken by a stranger is in this column and not in the first one; and that a request is a request and a refusal is a refusal and that a clerk who writes a refusal on a door in his own hand is a working man doing a job and not a face and not a courier and is not to be asked anything about the office he works in; and that the woman of no office said on that mole that the thing she is carrying is not the forty marks and is not going to reduce the forty marks and is not going to be thanked for and was not asked for; and that the standing list entered in full on the fourteenth day of August is not entered here again and stands, and the whole of it stands unpaid and unmoved and no part of it was paid, reduced, advanced or improved on the said day.*
