# Chapter 443

*Tuesday the Fifth Day of October, the Fiftieth Wage Tuesday, and the Wage Column at Twenty-Six Lines and One Hundred and Four Marks With Twenty Envelopes in a Chest in a Front Room Did Not Move on It and Fifty Weeks Stand Fallen Due With No Envelope Against Any of Them at the Close of the Said Day, Four People in a Room at the Back of a Corridor in a Building on a Hill Under a Paper on a Door That Names Three of Them, a Bellwright of About Thirty-Four With a Piece of Worked Lead About the Size of a Man's Palm and a Knife and a Straight Edge and a Pair of Hands, a Seam in a Fragment About Nine Inches by Six That the Cut Will Not Carry and That Is the Reason the Cut Is a Route and Not the Thing, a Clerk of About Forty With a Pencil Behind One Ear and a Flat Cloth in His Hand Going Back to a Table at Eleven o'Clock Because the Hour Named on That Paper Is the End of It and Not the Middle of It, One Thing Out of Three and Nothing Going Out of That Room in Any Hand, One Hundred and Sixty-Eight Pence Coming Into That Yard at About Ten in the Morning and One Hundred and Sixty-Eight Going Out of It at About Half Past Eleven in the Morning, and the Bag at One Hundred and Ninety-Six Pence*

Tuesday the fifth day of October, in that shed, in a corridor of that office on that hill, and in a room at the back of that corridor.

**The said Tuesday was the fiftieth wage Tuesday and was not a rota day and was not a wipe, and no charge was placed on that cistern on it. One hundred and sixty-eight pence came into that yard at about ten in the morning for a day's work with a boat and two men at a berth at the figure in chalk on a slate at an end stall, and one hundred and sixty-eight pence went out of it at about half past eleven in the morning for oakum and a can of pitch and forty feet of inch-and-a-half stuff, and there are twenty-eight pence in the bag at the close of the said day, being two shillings and fourpence, and the figure that bag is short of the wage of four marks is six hundred and twelve pence, being three marks eleven shillings. The wage column is at twenty-six lines and one hundred and four marks with twenty envelopes in a chest in a front room and it did not move on the fiftieth, and forty-nine weeks stood fallen due with no envelope against any of them at the open of this day and fifty stand at the close of it, and nobody is to write one word under any of it saying anybody is going to pay it. Nothing has been in a tin in that shed since the twenty-eighth day of April past.**

**And the far bench was occupied from about ten in the morning until about four in the afternoon for the third day of ten and was not approached, and the holder of that writ did not go over to him, and nothing was put on that bench and read as a gift, and neither brother has said why. Mara Venn was in that harbor and going nowhere, of no office, not on the crew, not restored, not forgiven, forty marks first after food, neither reduced nor paid, and on the said day she was in a room at the back of a corridor in a building on that hill from about ten in the morning until about eleven, because a paper on a door in that corridor names her as permitted at that hour. Tobias Wren was on the crown of that mole from ten until four, being sixty-one, and on the said day he took the eleventh piece up off that stone and turned it over twice and set it down nine inches from where it had been. The notice of the seventeenth of May past is on its wall in a different street for one hundred and forty-one days. The bellwright of that harbor has not been asked for one hundred and forty-three days and the eight marks and a day of a boat have not been argued, and no digit of anything he ever said is on a page, and the bellwright of about thirty-four who was in that room on that hill on the said morning is not that bellwright and is in a different place and is not asked one question about him and does not ask him one. The three nots standing against the eighth of June past are a thirteenth week, and the hand of a boy of twenty-two is at the head of the third column with a name beside it that is not on any page of this harbor. The two objects of nobody's on that crown are four hundred and eighty-one days old. The bench under that pump is bare for the two hundred and forty-seventh night. Two lights off that point have not moved in sixty-five days. A man of about fifty-one has said nothing to anybody about a page of six names for eighty-three days. The sheet of six lines at the back of that end stall is on that wall for the twentieth day and the paper of four lines at the top left of that door in Fish Street is on that door for the thirty-fifth day and has been answered by nobody. The nine-line sheet with a seal on it is in that front street for the fifty-second day.**

---

He went up the hill at about twenty to ten with nothing in his hands, and the second paper was still pinned under the first at the same corner of the same shut door, and the first paper had been at that corner since the twenty-second day of August, and he read the foot of it again standing in the corridor because he had read it seven times and he read it an eighth.

Nine lines. Three names. An hour, a place and an arrival. Three lines under them. And at the foot of it the seal line, which is not the shape of any other seal line that yard has seen on the face of a paper, and he read it and it told him the same it had told him on the twenty-fourth day of August, and there is no more in a seal line than the office at the end of it, and that a copying clerk of that office had put a figure of seven on that door in September and had put his own name and the word clerk under it, and that the hour named on the second paper on that door is ten in the morning on the fifth day of October, being a Tuesday, and that the room was entered at that hour on the said day with a paper on that door and a fourth stool at that bench.

He knocked.

The door came in and the man behind it was about forty with a jacket gone at both elbows and a piece of pencil behind one ear and a flat cloth in his hand, and he did not say good morning, and he turned and went into the room and left the door standing open behind him.

Mara Venn was in the corridor and she had a piece of pencil of her own and a slate under her arm, and neither of them said anything on the way up the steps behind him.

---

There were three things in that room.

The middle one was on a bench under a cloth, and the cloth came off it at about ten o'clock, and the holder of that writ had seen it once before on the twenty-fourth day of August for one hour and had not touched it then and did not touch it now.

It was a fragment about nine inches by six. Its edge was cut and not broken, and that was the whole of what a cut edge tells a man, which is that somebody with a wheel took it out of something larger on purpose. It had a seam in it, and the seam was not a join and not a crack and not a scratch; it was a place about three inches long where the pressure on one side of the glass was different from the pressure on the other side, and two people's hands had been on it, and the office at the end of the first paper had put it there and had not said which of the two was the one it was for.

"There is your hour," said the copying clerk. "I have got my stool and I have got my book and I have got a clean cloth, and the three of you have got until eleven, and I am in this room the whole of it, and the paper says the hour named on it is the end of it and not the middle of it, and that is the only sentence on that door that has cost anybody anything to write down."

"There are four people in this room and the paper names three of them."

"There are four people in this room and the paper names three of them, and I have put a fourth stool at that bench and I have not written the fourth one in my book, and I am not going to, and if you want to make it an irregularity then it is an irregularity and I will write that down at the end of the day in a book that is not this one, and I will not have written anything about her at all."

Mina Kett, of about thirty-four, a bellwright of Tidewatch on the western reef line, put her box down on the floor of that room at about ten o'clock and opened the lid of it and took out a piece of worked lead about the size of a man's palm and about a quarter of an inch thick, and a knife with a blade about three inches long, and a straight edge about a foot long, and a small hammer, and about forty small pieces of lead in the shape of little notches.

She had paid her own passage down that coast and she had her own hours and her own figure and she was not asked one question about that harbor's own bellwright, and nobody thanked her for being in that room, and she was asked one question, which was whether she could cut a lead.

"I can cut a lead," she said. "That is nine years of it and I will do it in this room in about forty minutes and I will do it badly at the ends because I cannot see a line through my own thumb. What I cannot do is copy that."

"Why not."

"Because that is nine inches by six and it has a whole scene in it and I am not a painter and there is no painter in this coast. A scene is not a cut. A cut is one line and a mark at the end of it and it is the same line in a hundred years, and a scene is nine inches of somebody being afraid and you cannot cut that with a knife without deciding what they were afraid of, and I am not going to decide what a woman of about nineteen was afraid of on a Thursday in the spring in a room I have not been in."

The room was quiet for about as long as it takes to say that.

"Then what are you going to cut," said the holder of that writ.

"The way in."

---

She did not touch the fragment for the first twenty minutes.

She stood at that bench and she looked at it, and once she put her hand flat on the cloth beside it and once she took her hand away, and at about twenty past ten she said one sentence about a place in it.

"There is a change in it about here," she said, with the flat of her finger against the air two inches above the glass and not touching it. "Not a crack. Somebody worked on one side of this for longer than somebody worked on the other side, and where that changes over is about the length of my hand, and it is not a join and it is not a repair. I can feel where that is with my hand on the cloth and I could not tell you what it means and neither could anybody else who is not a fool."

"Is that the seam."

"That is the seam. And I am telling you now, in front of three of you and a clerk, that my cut is not going to carry it."

"Why not."

"Because a cut is a line, and a line cannot carry a place where the pressure changed, because a place is not a line. If I cut that place in, then I will have cut an opinion into a piece of lead and the lead will go on saying it for two hundred years after everybody who could tell you it was an opinion is dead. If I cut round it, then there is a hole in the lead about the size of my thumb and a man with a pencil in a hundred years can see that somebody left a place out on purpose and can go and ask what was there." She put the knife down flat on the bench. "So the cut carries the way in and it does not carry that, and that is the reason your copy is a route and not a transcript, and it is going to be a route and not a transcript whether you like it or not, and if you had come up here wanting a transcript then you have come up an hour early and the clerk's seven is the right number."

The copying clerk said nothing at all to that and wrote in his book with a flat rule, and at the end of the morning when the three of them were on the steps outside he said one thing to the holder of that writ and then went back to his table.

"Seven is the right number for a bench," he said. "It is not the right number for a knife, and I put the seven on a door and I have not been asked about the seven and I am not going to be. She has been in my room for an hour and she has not asked me a question about this building and I have not asked her one about where she is from, and if anybody wants to know what I did this morning, they can read my book, and my book says that a thing was cut at that bench at ten in the morning and that a mark was cut with it, and my book does not have her name in it and it is not going to."

---

She cut it in about thirty-five minutes and she was right that she could not see a line through her own thumb, and the two ends of the cut on the lead were thicker than the middle by about the width of a hair, and she said that was how it is and that a man in a hundred years would be able to see it and would be able to tell from it which end of the cut was made first.

The line was about four inches long and it went nine notches into the lead, one notch for each sounding, and the notches went deeper on the second, the fifth and the eighth than they did on the others, and she said that was because those were the three shallow ones and that a cut that did not say which were the shallow ones was a picture of something rather than a way in.

Then she cut the marks, and the marks were not figures.

There was a letter at the head of the lead, cut deep and square, that was the office's own mark and was not a number. There was a hand, cut flat, about the size of a thumbnail, which was the way that office has cut a hand on the foot of a paper for longer than anybody in that building has been alive. There was an arm with a notch in it and a hole through the head, cut small and off to one side, which was her own, and which is on about forty pieces of lead hanging on a nail in the door frame of a workshop at Tidewatch, and a woman of about sixty-one there copies a cut onto a paper for a penny to anybody who walks in, and that mark is the mark of a person and not of a building and not of an office.

And there was no figure and no number of any kind cut into that piece of lead at all.

She put the knife down and the straight edge and looked at the holder of that writ and then at the clerk at his table.

"He will ask you why," she said. "So I will tell you now, in front of the clerk, so that you can look at him while I say it." She nodded at the man at the table. "Tell them."

"A number cut into a thing is a thing that can be asked after," said the copying clerk, without looking up. "I have three things in this room under three numbers and I am not putting one of them on a piece of lead that is going to sit in a drawer for a hundred years. You would not do it either, and I have watched you leave a hole in that line rather than cut something into it that you did not know, and a person who will not cut a place she does not know is not going to cut a number on top of one she has been told about. Write the marks and leave the figures out. When somebody wants the figures they can come and stand in this room and ask me for them, and I will tell them whether I am allowed to say them, and that is a different thing and a better one."

The fragment went back onto the shelf under the same number at about twenty to eleven, and the cloth went over it, and the third of the three was not looked at and was not named, and the claim on it in a book in that building has been in that book since the December before last and is not withdrawn and no request for its withdrawal was made by anybody in that room or in that harbor or by anybody at all.

At eleven o'clock the three people who were named on that paper came out of that room, and the fourth person who was not named on that paper came out of it behind them, and the door was shut, and nothing went out of that room in any hand, including a piece of worked lead about the size of a man's palm with a line cut in it and a hand cut on it and an office's mark cut at the head of it and no figure on it at all.

The lead is in a drawer in that office on that hill. The account of it is not in that building.

**THE THING THAT IS IN THAT DRAWER IS A MADE THING AND NOT A TRANSCRIPT, AND A STRANGER WITH A PENCIL CAN TAKE IT UP AND FIND THAT IT SAYS WHAT IT SAYS ABOUT ITSELF: IT CARRIES A LINE AND NINE SOUNDINGS AND IT CARRIES THREE OF THOSE NINE DEEPER THAN THE OTHER SIX, AND IT CARRIES A THUMB-SIZED PLACE LEFT OUT ON PURPOSE, AND IT CARRIES AN OFFICE'S MARK AND A HAND AND A PERSON'S OWN MARK CUT SMALL AND OFF TO ONE SIDE, AND IT CARRIES NO FIGURE AND NO NUMBER, AND EVERY ONE OF THOSE SIX THINGS IS TRUE OF THE LEAD ITSELF AND NOT OF ANYBODY'S WORD ABOUT IT. THE ORIGINAL IS ON ITS SHELF UNDER THE SAME NUMBER AND THE THIRD OF THE THREE WAS NOT LOOKED AT AND ITS CLAIM IS NOT WITHDRAWN AND NOBODY HAS ASKED FOR IT TO BE WITHDRAWN. A CLERK OF ABOUT FORTY WROTE A FIGURE OF SEVEN ON A PIECE OF PAPER AT A DOOR ON THE SECOND DAY OF SEPTEMBER AND WAS NOT ASKED ABOUT IT AND WAS NOT THANKED FOR IT, AND A BELLWRIGHT OF ABOUT THIRTY-FOUR CUT A LINE IN LEAD IN FORTY MINUTES IN THAT ROOM ON THE FIFTH DAY OF OCTOBER AND IS PAID AT THE FIGURE IN CHALK AND HER OWN FIGURE IS FIVE SHILLINGS, BEING SIXTY PENCE, AND SHE SAID THAT OUT LOUD ONCE ON THE TWENTY-SIXTH DAY OF SEPTEMBER AND DID NOT SAY IT AGAIN, AND NOBODY THANKED HER. A FIGURE OF FOUR HUNDRED AND FIFTY PENCE IS STILL ON A BOARD AT AN END STALL AND DID NOT COME DOWN, AND THE FIFTIETH WAGE TUESDAY FELL ON THAT DAY AND THE COLUMN DID NOT MOVE ON IT, AND THERE IS NO FLOUR IN THAT MARKET.**

and that one hundred and sixty-eight pence came into that yard at about ten in the morning and one hundred and sixty-eight pence went out of it at about half past eleven in the morning, and that twenty-eight plus one hundred and sixty-eight less one hundred and sixty-eight is twenty-eight pence at the close of the said day, being two shillings and fourpence, being short of the wage of four marks by six hundred and twelve pence, being three marks eleven shillings; and that a room at the back of a corridor in that office on that hill was entered at about ten in the morning on the said day under a paper on that door which names two as permitted and one as present for the whole of the hour, and that four people were in that room, and that a clerk of about forty of that office was in it the whole of the hour and went back to his table at eleven; and that one thing out of three was under a cloth on a bench in that room and came off the cloth at about ten o'clock, being a fragment about nine inches by six whose edge was cut and not broken and in which there is a seam about three inches long where the pressure on one side is not the pressure on the other, and that the third of the three was not looked at and was not named, and that the claim standing on the third in a book in that building was not withdrawn and no request for its withdrawal was made by anybody; and that the two objects of nobody's on that crown are four hundred and eighty-one days old, the bench under that pump is bare for the two hundred and forty-seventh night, and two lights off that point have not moved in sixty-five days.*
*Second column, not checkable from a mole, entered on the said Tuesday at about half past nine in the evening in that shed, and no name is against it: that the reason a cut is not a transcript is in a bellwright's own mouth and is that a scene is not a line and that a line cannot carry a place where the pressure changed, and that a cut which carried that place would be an opinion cut into a thing that would go on saying it for two hundred years, and that the place was left out of the cut on purpose and is about the size of a thumb and can be seen; and that the reason no figure and no number was cut into that piece of lead is in a copying clerk's own mouth at about half past ten in that room and is that a number cut into a thing is a thing that can be asked after, and that he put no figure on it and was not asked about it and was not thanked for it; and that the reason four people were in that room when a paper on that door names three of them is in the clerk's own mouth and is that the door carries a figure of seven hours at that bench and a pair of hands, and that the door names a party and does not say a hand may not come into the room, and that the clerk put a fourth stool at that bench and did not write the fourth one in his book; and that the reason a bellwright of about thirty-four was asked one question in that room is that she was asked whether she could cut a lead and that is a thing a man may be asked, and that she was not asked whether she would come and was not asked whether she would stay, and that she was not thanked for being in that room and did not ask to be; and that the reason no figure is set against that lead and that line and those nine notches is that it is in a drawer in a building on a hill and that a figure on a thing a man in this harbor cannot see is a price put on it by a woman with a slate; and that the standing list entered in full on the fourteenth of August past is not entered in full on the said Tuesday and stands, and no part of it was paid, reduced, advanced or improved on that day.*
