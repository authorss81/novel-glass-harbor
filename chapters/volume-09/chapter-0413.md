# Chapter 413

*Sunday the Fifth Day of September, a Bowl Carried Down to the Step of a Room over a Joiner's Shop at Two o'Clock and Taken In Again at Six With Whatever Is in It, About Twenty People in That Room of Whom Eleven Cannot Read, a Man of About Forty-Six Reading Every Head-Note Out Loud off the Long Table and Writing His Own Name and the Hour Under Each One in His Own Hand on the Day, a Woman of About Fifty-Eight Who Would Not Have the Figure of Lamp Oil Entered in That Volume and Whose No Was Entered at the Same Size as Every Other Line on the Page With Her Name and the Hour Against It, and a Woman of About Sixty-One of the Third Lay Called Nell Carrow Who Came Up Those Stairs With a Stick and Asked One Question at the End of That Table*

Sunday the fifth day of September, in that room, in that shed, and on that mole head.

**The said Sunday was not a wage Tuesday and was not a rota day and was not a wipe, and the wall behind the reeve's bench came down and went up whole at the angle it has been at since the twelfth and nobody put it back. Nothing came into that yard on the said day and nothing went out of it, and there are four hundred and fifty-two pence in the bag, being two marks and eleven shillings, and the figure that bag is short of the wage of four marks is one hundred and eighty-eight pence, being one mark two shillings and fourpence, and it is not going to be paid and nobody is to write one word under it saying anybody is going to.**

**And the far bench was occupied on the said Sunday from about ten in the morning until about four in the afternoon and was not approached on it, and the holder of that writ did not go over to him, and nothing was put on that bench and read as a gift, and neither brother has said why. Mara Venn was in that harbor and going nowhere, of no office, not on the crew, not restored, not forgiven, forty marks first after food, neither reduced nor paid, and she was not in that room on the said day and did not come up that slip at all, and a man of about twenty-three was on the flat of the bench stone in that shed at about half past three that morning because he cannot sleep, and nobody in that harbor was on that stone with him. Tobias Wren was on the crown of that mole from ten until four, being sixty-one, with eleven pieces of a boat behind him and a boat's hook in his right hand, and on the said day he was sat on the third piece with the hook across his knees and a cup of something in his left hand, and he drank it and looked at the water for an hour and put the cup down behind the parapet where it would not be in the way of anybody's boots. The notice of the seventeenth of May past is on its wall in a different street for one hundred and eleven days and has not been touched. The bellwright of that harbor has not been asked for one hundred and thirteen days, the chest is shut, and the eight marks and a day of a boat have not been argued The three nots standing against the eighth of June past are a tenth week in which nobody in that crew has said one word about them, and the hand of a boy of twenty-two is still at the head of the third column with a name beside it that has not been said out loud since the thirteenth of May past, and that name is not on any page of this harbor.. The two objects of nobody's on that crown are four hundred and fifty-one days old and neither has moved. The bench under that pump is bare for the two hundred and seventeenth night. Two lights at about a mile off that point have not moved in thirty-five days. A man of about fifty-one has said nothing to anybody about a page of six names for fifty-three days. A nine-line sheet with a seal on it has been in that front street for twenty-two days and has been answered by nobody. The paper at the top left of that door in Fish Street is five days old, and the chalk cross under it is one day old, and about twenty people went up those stairs on the said day.**

---

Tom Barlas carried the bowl down to the step at two o'clock and put it on the top stone, which is the stone a man has to step on to get in, and went back inside and did not stand at the window. He was in the doorway twice in the hour, both times to move a bench, and both times he went past a person on the step without looking at his face, and that is the correct way and he knows it is the correct way and he said so to nobody.

About twenty people went up. Four came down. Of the twenty, eleven could not read, and they were not asked to prove it and nobody in that room knew which eleven they were except the two people who had counted, and the woman of about fifty-eight counted six in her own trade and had counted them on a Thursday in August and has said since that she will count them again before she dies.

Hobb Lee stood at the end of the long table with the volume open in front of him and a stub of pencil in his right hand, and he did not read the whole thing, because there is no use in a man reading a lamp twice in one hour, and he read the heads and he read them in the order they were in, and under each head that he read he wrote one line in his own hand, being his own name and the hour, and the hour was the hour at which he had finished that head and not the hour at which he had begun it, and the twenty people in that room could hear the pencil.

"Fourteenth. The lamp, off a hull out of the second lay on the fourteenth of March last. Where it came from: the fourteenth of March, the second lay, out of a hull with her stem opened. Who stands where it is: a room over the fish market, and a man is paid a shilling a week to wipe it and his name is a matter for him. What it does not show: it shows nothing of the room it stood in, and a man who wants to know that should go and ask the room, and the room is not here."

He wrote under it. *Hobb Lee. Four minutes past two in the afternoon.*

And the ninth of June.

"Nineteenth. Two boats of a man of about forty-four of the second lay, of nine and eleven tons, and he would not have them entered. He came on the seventh of June and said no, and I wrote no, and I wrote his reason, and that is all there is to write. Where they came from is his own business and he has not given it to me and I did not ask him for it. Who stands where they are: the second lay, both of them, and he stands aboard them. What it does not show: it does not show whether that man is afraid of somebody or of something, and he is not obliged to tell this room that, and he has not, and he is not in this room and he was not asked to come."

*Twenty-one minutes past two in the afternoon. Hobb Lee.*

A man of about thirty-four near the door said, at that, that he had been in that room in June when that was written and had not known there was a no in it, and Hobb Lee said, without looking up, that the no was in it in June and would have been in it in June for anybody who had opened it, and that a man is not on a list because he has a no against him, and that there was no list and there was not going to be one, and that the man of about forty-four did not know he was in it either until a man read it out in a room in front of twenty people on a Sunday, and that this was the first time that anybody had read that entry out loud, and that if the man of about forty-four wanted it off the page he could come up the stairs on a Sunday and say so, and that Hobb Lee would enter that in the same four lines with the same size hand and the hour, and that was that, and he went on reading.

---

At about half past three in the afternoon the woman of about fifty-eight stood up at her end of the table, which is not where a person stands at her end stall, and said that she had something to refuse.

"Go on," said Hobb Lee, and he put the pencil down flat on the page.

"A barrel of lamp oil," she said. "Four hundred and fifty pence, being thirty-seven shillings and sixpence, quoted the first of June, with February's three hundred and seventy-eight pence, being thirty-one shillings and sixpence, standing visible above it, and both dates on the board, and a figure put on a board cannot be taken off again. I will not have that figure entered in that volume."

Somebody at the back said why not.

"Because a figure on a board is a figure in a place where a man has to come to it," she said. "He walks up my stall, he reads it, he goes away, and he does not have to write anything and he does not have to have a name against it and he cannot carry it off with him unless he takes a piece of paper out of his pocket and writes it down, and a man who does that is a man who is going to be asked about it, and a man who is going to be asked about it is a man who knows what it costs to be in this market. And if that figure is in that book, then it is in a building that anybody can walk into on a Sunday and sit down in, and it is read out loud, and every person in this harbor who cannot read will be told a number for oil out loud in a room, and it will be in a man of about forty-six's mouth, and he did not put it there and he cannot take it out, and the next week the next barrel is going to be a pound higher and the week after that it is going to be higher again, and it will be because a man read it out in a room and forty people heard it and one of them was standing at a counter and told another one what he had heard."

Nessa Pike, who was on the stairs, said, from the step, that the woman of about fifty-eight was right, and that a price in a book stops being a price and becomes a thing people say they heard.

"It would be the best talking in that harbor," said the woman of about fifty-eight, "and there is no flour in that market and I have not sold a sack of it since the spring and I would be the woman who put the talking about oil into the mouths of eleven people who cannot read."

Hobb Lee wrote it. It took him a while and he did it the same size as everything else in the volume and he did not write it small and he did not put it at the back.

*What it is: a figure on a board at an end stall. Where it came from: the first of June. Who stands where it is: the end stall, and the figure is on a board and February's figure stands above it with both dates. What it does not show: it does not show that a figure read out in a room is a different kind of figure from a figure on a board, and it does not show that she was asked on this day and said no, and that no was given and written at the same size as every other line on this page.*

*Three twenty-six minutes in the afternoon. Hobb Lee.*

"Read that out," she said.

He read it out. All four lines, and the hour, and his own name, and she stood at her end of the table and listened to it and nobody said one word, and then she said that was all right and went down the stairs, and she was not thanked by anybody in that room and did not ask to be, and it is in that volume in the same hand and the same size as the twenty-two pieces of glass out of wrecks.

---

At about ten to four in the afternoon a woman of about sixty-one came up those stairs one step at a time with a stick in her right hand and a bad knee that she does not explain, and stood at the end of the long table and looked at the volume for a while without touching it.

Her name is Nell Carrow. She is of the third lay and she makes rope, and she has had a room on that lay for eleven years with a rope walk in the back of it, and there is not another rope walk in that harbor and there has not been for a long time, and she walks on it herself because there is nobody to walk it for her. She was born in that room.

"You are reading them out," she said.

"I am."

"Then I will wait."

She waited about twenty minutes with her hand on the end of the table and the stick against her leg, and she did not interrupt, and when the twenty people had gone down the stairs and Tom Barlas had come in to move the benches she said, in her own mouth, the one question she came up those stairs to ask.

"Does that book hold a thing a person made themselves."

Hobb Lee put the pencil down.

"It holds a thing out of a wreck, a thing off a vessel, a thing off a building, and a lamp and a door plate and a box of tally sticks, and a whole bell," he said. "It holds whatever this harbor is holding that anybody wants to say where it came from. It does not hold a thing a person made with their own hands, because a thing a person made with their own hands is not recovered, and that book is a book of what this harbor recovered, and there is a difference between the two and it is not a small one and I am not going to pretend it is a small one."

"Then it is the wrong book for me," said Nell Carrow, "and I have been in the wrong book for about forty-two years, and I have not been in this harbor's book at all, and I did not know that until this afternoon and I would not have said anything about it if you had not been reading out in this room and I had not been stood at the end of your table."

She did not say what the thing was. She stood there with her hand on the end of the table and she said four more sentences, and they were these.

"I have got one. It is about eleven inches by eight and it is in a box in my back room and it has been in that box since the year I was nineteen. I have not looked at it in a long while. I will not have it entered in that book if the book is a book of what you recovered, and I am not asking you to change what the book is. But I would like to look at it in a room, with people in the room, because it is a thing that is in my back room and nobody in this harbor has seen it and I have not had it out in the open air since I was a girl, and that is not a good thing to have been doing for forty-two years and I have only worked it out this afternoon."

Hobb Lee said that the room was let out on a Sunday and was shut on a week day and that the volume was on that table on a Sunday and that anybody who put something on that table on a Sunday could look at it, and that the room was open at one o'clock and that he would be there, and that anybody who brought a thing to that table on a Sunday could take it away again when they had finished with it and that he would not ask what was on it and would not write one word about it.

"That is a yes," said Nell Carrow.

"That is a yes," said Hobb Lee, "and I am going to be here at one o'clock and I have not asked you a single thing about that box and I am not going to, and if you come at one o'clock with it I will put the benches back the way they were for a market and there will be about twenty people in here, because it is a Sunday and it is a Sunday the whole afternoon, and if you would rather have the room with four people in it than twenty then say so now and I will shut the door and put a chair against it and take the money out of the bowl and put it back on the step, and that is as easy for me as the other thing."

"Twenty," said Nell Carrow. "I have had forty-two years of nobody."

She went down the stairs one step at a time. Nobody in that room thanked her, because she had not done anything for anybody, and the whole of what happened to her on that Sunday was that she asked a question at the end of a table and a man gave her an answer, and the answer cost him one sentence, and no name of hers went into that volume that day, and she went down those stairs one step at a time.

A room over a joiner's shop at the top of Fish Street was let out on the said Sunday from one o'clock, a bowl stood on the top stone of its step from two o'clock until six, and about twenty people went up those stairs and four came down, and eleven of the twenty could not read and were not asked to prove it. A man of about forty-six read the head-notes of that volume out loud in that room from about two o'clock and wrote his own name and the hour at which he finished each head under each head in his own hand, and the hour at which he finished the nineteenth entry was twenty-one minutes past two in the afternoon. A refusal of a man of about forty-four of the second lay, entered on the ninth of June, was read out loud in that room for the first time, with his name on it and his reason on it and four lines under the head. A woman of about fifty-eight refused, at about half past three in the afternoon and out loud, to have the figure of lamp oil entered in that volume, and her no was entered on the said day in the same hand and the same size as every other line on the page with her name and the hour against it, and it was read out in that room, and she was not thanked in that room. A woman of about sixty-one of the third lay called Nell Carrow, who makes rope and has a room with a rope walk in the back of it that she has had for eleven years, came up those stairs at ten to four in the afternoon and asked one question at the end of that table, and the question was whether that book holds a thing a person made themselves, and the answer was that it does not, and she said that she has got one and that it is about eleven inches by eight and has been in a box in her back room since the year she was nineteen and that she would like to look at it in a room with people in it, and the man of about forty-six said that the room was open at one o'clock on a Sunday and that anybody who brought a thing to that table on a Sunday could take it away again, and that about twenty people would be in the room. The far bench was occupied from ten until four and was not approached, and neither brother went near the other at any hour of the said day.**
