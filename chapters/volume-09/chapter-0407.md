# Chapter 407

*Monday the Thirtieth of August, the Sixty-Ninth Time the Wall Behind the Reeve's Bench Came Down Bare and Went Up at the Angle It Has Been At Since the Twelfth and Nobody Put It Back, the Seventy-Seventh Time the Cistern Was Charged and Walked on None of It, a Man of About Fifty-One on the Crown of That Mole with His Page and His Stone and No Figure and Forty-Seven Days of Silence and Nothing Given to Him by Anybody, a Copyist of About Fifty-Nine at Her Counter in a Room off the Market Who Has Not Once in Twenty-Two Years Asked What She Was Copying, the Last Block of a Return Counted at a Wall and Not Said to Anybody, and the Sixth Day of a Refusal on a Door*

Monday the thirtieth of August, in that shed, at the office on the west basin, in a room off the fish market, and on the crown of that mole.

**The said Monday was a wipe and was not a wage Tuesday and was not a rota day, and the wall behind the reeve's bench came down bare at about eight in the morning for the sixty-ninth time and went up at the angle it has been at since the twelfth with nobody putting it back. It was the day the cistern at the back of that market was charged at about eight and walked on by none of it at about half past nine, being the seventy-seventh time, and the top name on the rota board is still off it. Nothing came into that yard on the said day and nothing went out of it, and there are two hundred and eighty-four pence in the bag, being one mark ten shillings and fourpence, and the figure that bag is short of the wage of four marks is three hundred and fifty-six pence, being two marks three shillings, and it is not going to be paid and nobody is to write one word under it saying anybody is going to. Nothing has been in a tin in that shed since the twenty-eighth day of April past, and there is no flour in that market and there is not going to be any this month.**

**And the far bench was occupied on the said Monday from about ten in the morning until about four in the afternoon and was not approached on it, and the holder of that writ did not go over to him, and nothing was put on that bench and read as a gift, and neither brother has said why. Mara Venn was in that harbor and going nowhere, of no office, not on the crew, not restored, not forgiven, forty marks first after food, neither reduced nor paid, and she did not come up that hill on the said day. Tobias Wren was on the crown of that mole from ten until four, being sixty-one, with eleven pieces of a boat behind him and a boat's hook in his right hand, and he was not sent for and not asked and not thanked, and he is not a teacher and not a door and not a sacrifice, and he is not the man on the far bench, and neither brother went near the other at any hour of the said day. The notice of the seventeenth of May past is on its wall in a different street for one hundred and five days and has not been touched and has not been taken down. The bellwright of that harbor has not been asked for one hundred and seven days, the chest is shut, the eight marks and a day of a boat have not been argued, and no digit of anything he ever said is on a page.**

---

The copyist's counter is in a room off the fish market, up four steps and along, and it has been in the same place for twenty-two years and the woman behind it is about fifty-nine and has never once asked what she was copying.

Calder went in on the Monday morning because he wanted a sheet of good paper and there is no other sheet of good paper in that town.

She was copying. She was copying a list and the list went down the page for about eleven inches and each line of it was the same length, and she was going down it at the speed of a woman who has stopped reading what she is writing and is doing the thing her hands do, which is faster and which is not the same thing.

"I want a sheet," Calder said. "The best you have got."

"That one," she said, without stopping, and went on writing.

He put a penny on the counter out of his own hand and not out of the bag, and she put the penny in a box and took a sheet out of a drawer and squared it and pushed it across, and neither of them said anything for about ten seconds.

Then she said, "You are the one that reads out a leaf."

"I am."

"You have come in here four times since the middle of June and you have never once asked me what I am copying," she said, "and I have told you about that on purpose, because in about two months somebody is going to come in here and ask me, and I want you to have heard me say this in your own hearing before that happens and not afterwards."

"What are you copying."

"I am copying a list of glass," said the woman of about fifty-nine. "I do not know whose. It comes in on a Monday and it goes out on a Thursday and it is about eleven inches long and every line of it is the same length and there is a seal on the top of it that I have not seen on anything else in twenty-two years. I have never asked. I am not going to ask. I have got a reason and the reason is that a copyist who stops copying is a copyist who has admitted that she copied something, and a trade can be put in a schedule and an admission cannot, and I have a man to feed and he does not care what is on the page."

"Was that a warning?"

"It was a thing said out loud on a Monday morning in a room with four steps and a window," she said, "and if anybody wants to make it a warning they can come and make it one and I will deny it and I will be believed, because I have been believed for twenty-two years and I have never once been wrong in this room."

She went back to the list. He took the sheet and went down the four steps and out into a market where about nine hundred people were doing what nine hundred people do in a fish market on a Monday.

---

The man of about fifty-one was on the crown of that mole at about eleven in the morning with a page under a stone and a stone in his own pocket.

The page is a sheet of paper about the size of a fish plate and it is four feet off the two objects of nobody's, which are under two tarpaulins about nine feet apart, and one of the tarpaulins has had a corner off it since the seventeenth of June past and has not been lashed back, and the two of them were four hundred and thirty-eight days old on the twenty-third of August and are four hundred and forty-five days old today, and neither has moved.

He had put the stone there himself. He put it there on the thirtieth of July past because a page blows away.

He has said nothing to anybody about that page for forty-seven days. He wrote six names on it and one boat, and there is no figure on it anywhere, and the price on it is a boat, and nobody in that harbor has offered him a boat or a wage or a berth or a word, and nobody has asked him a question about it, and Calder Marr did not ask him a question about it and did not stand near him at more than the distance two men stand at on a mole.

The two of them were on that crown at the same time for about four minutes. The man of about fifty-one worked at the edge of the two tarpaulins with a piece of chalk and did not look across. Calder went to the far end of the crown and looked at the water and came back down the slip.

Nobody gave that man anything on the said day. Nobody has given that man anything since the thirtieth of July.

---

Peat Marrow was at the wall at about half past two on the same afternoon and did the fifth block, which is the last block, and which is ruled for every day of August from the first to the twenty-third and stops there.

There are twenty-three lines in it. Sixteen of them carry a figure and seven are struck across. The sum of the sixteen figures is fifty-seven. The small figure in the clerk of about sixty's hand at the head of that block is sixteen, and it is right.

That is the whole of the sheet. Five blocks. The first of April to the twenty-third of August. One hundred and forty-five lines. One hundred and sixteen of them carry a figure. One hundred and forty-five lines less one hundred and sixteen is twenty-nine lines that are struck across, being a day that went by and nothing came in on it.

He stood at that wall for a while after he had done it, with the pencil behind his ear, and then he put his left hand flat on the wall under the fifth block and looked at the two figures he had on that palm from the Thursday of the twenty-sixth of August, and he did not go out sideways. He stood there until the clerk of about sixty looked up from his list, which is a list and is not a schedule and binds nobody in that harbor.

"Do you want the rest of it in a hand," said the clerk of about sixty.

Peat Marrow turned round and looked at him.

"You have not asked me my name," he said.

"No."

"Then I will not give it to you in this room," said Peat Marrow, "and if you had asked me I would have given it to you, and I would have thought less of you."

The clerk of about sixty put his thumb on the edge of the list and said nothing else at all, and Peat Marrow went out sideways with two figures on the flat of his own left hand and did not say them to anybody in that town on that day.

---

At about half past five Calder Marr was on the flat of the bench stone and did the arithmetic, on the back of the good sheet from the copyist, and it took him about four minutes and he got it wrong once and did it again.

Twenty-six lines out of April, and the figures on them: seventy-one.
Twenty-eight out of May: ninety-six.
Twenty-one out of June: seventy-nine.
Twenty-five out of July: eighty-eight.
Sixteen out of twenty-three days of August: fifty-seven.

Seventy-one and ninety-six is a hundred and sixty-seven. A hundred and sixty-seven and seventy-nine is two hundred and forty-six. Two hundred and forty-six and eighty-eight is three hundred and thirty-four. Three hundred and thirty-four and fifty-seven is three hundred and ninety-one.

He wrote it on the back of the good sheet in a figure that anybody with a pencil could take, with the five summands under it, and then he did it a second time on his own knee without the sheet and got the same number, and then he went over the five summands once more against what Peat Marrow had written on the flat of his own left hand on the Thursday and against what Peat Marrow had said out loud to him in the front street on that Thursday, and they are the same five numbers.

Three hundred and ninety-one.

He looked at it for a while on the back of a sheet of paper a copyist of about fifty-nine sold him for a penny out of her own drawer, on a mole head at half past five in the evening with the light out, and then he folded the sheet in four and put it inside his coat, against the other paper, which is a copy of a paper on a door in a corridor on that hill and is not the paper.

And then he sat down on the flat of the bench stone, which is the same stone a caulker of about twenty-three stands on at half past three every morning he cannot sleep, and there was nobody on it, and nobody had been asked for that night, and he was not going to ask anybody for anything on that stone for the next three nights because the second of September was on a door in a corridor on that hill and there was nothing he could do to it before then and a man who goes and knocks on a door every second day is a man asking for a favour twice.

**The wall behind the reeve's bench came down bare for the sixty-ninth time on the said Monday and went up at the angle it has been at since the twelfth and nobody put it back, and the cistern was charged for the seventy-seventh time and walked on by none of it and the top name on the rota board is still off it. Nothing came into that yard on the said day and nothing went out of it, and there are two hundred and eighty-four pence in the bag, being one mark ten shillings and fourpence, and the figure that bag is short of the wage of four marks is three hundred and fifty-six pence, being two marks three shillings. A copyist of about fifty-nine, twenty-two years in a room off that market, said out loud on the said morning that she has never asked what she is copying and is not going to and gave the reason, which is that a copyist who stops copying is a copyist who has admitted she copied something. A man of about fifty-one stood on the crown of that mole for about four minutes at about eleven in the morning and worked at the edge of the two objects of nobody's with a piece of chalk and did not look across, and has said nothing to anybody about that page for forty-seven days, and the two objects are four hundred and forty-five days old and neither has moved. A man of about fifty-three with a bad back finished the last block of a return at a wall at about half past two in the afternoon and did not say the figures to anybody in that town. The holder of that writ did the sum on the back of a sheet at about half past five in the evening and got three hundred and ninety-one, and folded the sheet in four and put it inside his coat, and sat down on the flat of the bench stone and was not going to ask anybody for anything on that stone for the next three nights. The far bench was occupied from ten until four and was not approached, and neither brother went near the other at any hour of the said day.**

*First column, entered on Monday the thirtieth day of August next at about half past nine in the evening in that shed: that the said Monday was the sixty-ninth time the wall behind the reeve's bench came down bare and it went up at the angle it has been at since the twelfth and nobody put it back; and that the cistern was charged for the seventy-seventh time at about eight in the morning and walked on by none of it at about half past nine, and that the top name on the rota board is still off it; and that a copyist of about fifty-nine at a counter in a room off that market sold the holder of that writ one sheet of good paper for a penny out of his own hand and not out of the bag, and that she said in her own mouth that she has been in that room twenty-two years and has never once asked what she was copying and is not going to, and that she is copying a list of glass of about eleven inches with every line of it the same length and a seal on the top of it that she has not seen on anything else in twenty-two years, and that she gave her reason, being that a copyist who stops copying is a copyist who has admitted she copied something; and that the said man was on the crown of that mole at about eleven in the morning with his page under a stone and a stone in his own pocket and worked at the edge of the two objects of nobody's with a piece of chalk and did not look across, and that the two objects are four hundred and forty-five days old on the said day and are about nine feet apart under two tarpaulins and one of the tarpaulins has had a corner off it since the seventeenth of June past and has not been lashed back, and that the said man has said nothing to anybody about that page for forty-seven days and has been given nothing by anybody since the thirtieth of July; and that a man of about fifty-three counted the fifth and last block of that return at about half past two in the afternoon, and that it is ruled for every day of August from the first to the twenty-third, and that sixteen of those twenty-three lines carry a figure and seven are struck across, and that the sum of the sixteen figures is fifty-seven, and that the small figure in the clerk of about sixty's hand at the head of that block is sixteen and is right; and that the said man stood at that wall after he had done it and a clerk of about sixty looked up from his list and asked him whether he wanted the rest of it in a hand, and that the said man said that the clerk had not asked him his name and that he would not give it in that room and would have given it if he had asked; and that the holder of that writ did the sum of the five blocks on the back of that sheet at about half past five in the evening, with the five summands written under it so that a stranger with a pencil can take it again, being seventy-one and ninety-six and seventy-nine and eighty-eight and fifty-seven, which is three hundred and ninety-one, and that he took it twice, the second time without the sheet; and that the five summands are five numbers a man added once each, and that he set his own count of the lines in each block against the small figure the clerk of about sixty had written at the head of that block as he came to it, and that all five of the clerk's small figures were right when he came to them, and that the two line-counts of the first and second blocks were taken twice and the other three were taken once.*
*Second column, not checkable from a mole, entered on the said Monday at about half past nine in the evening in that shed, and no name is against it: that the reason against the line of the sum is that the holder of that writ is not going to enter it in the first column on the Monday, because a man with a bad back said two of his figures out loud in a front street on the Thursday and three of them to nobody at all, and until that man says them on a day and a person hears them and can be asked about them afterwards, the number is one man's arithmetic at a wall and the first column is not the place for one man's arithmetic at a wall; and that a copyist who has never asked what she is copying is a working woman with a trade and a reason and not a witness and not a leak on purpose and is not to be asked again by anybody in this yard; and that a man of about fifty-one with a page and a stone and forty-seven days of silence is not on a list and is not on a board and is not in a return and has had nothing from this yard and is not going to have anything from this yard, and that the page on that mole is what a boat looks like when nobody is maintaining anything; and that a man who goes and knocks on a door every second day is a man asking for a favour twice, and that the holder is not going to do that, and that the second of September is on a door in a corridor on that hill and there is nothing he can do to it before then; and that the reason against the fourth column entered on the twenty-eighth of August is entered once and is not entered again; and that the standing list entered in full on the fourteenth day of August is not entered here again and stands, and the whole of it stands unpaid and unmoved and no part of it was paid, reduced, advanced or improved on the said day.*
