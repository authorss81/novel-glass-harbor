# Chapter 433

*Saturday the Twenty-Fifth Day of September, Eleven Masters Asked One at a Time in the Same Words and Not One of Them Asked Twice, a Page With a Name and an Hour Against Each of Eleven Lines and Ten of the Eleven Said Yes and One Said No With a Reason in His Own Mouth and Was Not Asked Again on Any of the Six Days After It and Was Not Given Another Job and Was Not Thanked, a Boat of About Fourteen Tons Out of Lowfen Among the Seven and a Man of About Sixty There Who Puts a Bundle of Hazel Out on the Point of the Shoal Every Spring, About Thirty Men in Seven Boats and a Woman of About Fifty-Eight With a Slate Who Says That the Figure on That Page Is Her Figure and That She Is Not Going to Be Told What to Do With It, One Hundred and Sixty-Eight Pence Coming in at the Figure in Chalk on a Slate at an End Stall, and the Bag at Five Hundred and Thirty-Two Pence*

Saturday the twenty-fifth day of September, at the head of the third lay, at the second lay, at the first lay, in that shed, and on that mole head.

**The said Saturday was not a rota day and was not a wage Tuesday and was not a wipe, and no charge was placed on that cistern on it. One hundred and sixty-eight pence came into that yard at about eleven in the morning for a day's work with a boat and two men at a berth at the figure in chalk on a slate at an end stall, and there are five hundred and thirty-two pence in the bag at the close of the said day, being three marks four shillings and fourpence, and the figure that bag is short of the wage of four marks is one hundred and eight pence, being nine shillings, and it is not going to be paid and nobody is to write one word under it saying anybody is going to.**

**And the far bench was occupied from ten until four and was not approached, and the holder of that writ did not go over to him, and nothing was put on that bench and read as a gift, and neither brother has said why. Mara Venn was in that harbor and going nowhere, of no office, not on the crew, not restored, not forgiven, forty marks first after food, neither reduced nor paid, and she was not on that walk of eleven doors and was not asked for a line on that page. Tobias Wren was on the crown of that mole from ten until four, being sixty-one, and on the said day he stopped the hook in a crack in the stone about a foot from the eleventh piece and stood with the hook in that crack and did not use it. The notice of the seventeenth of May past is on its wall in a different street for one hundred and thirty-one days. The bellwright of that harbor has not been asked for one hundred and thirty-three days, the chest is shut, and the eight marks and a day of a boat have not been argued, and no digit of anything he ever said is on a page. The three nots standing against the eighth of June past are an eleventh week in which nobody in that crew has said one word about them, and the hand of a boy of twenty-two is still at the head of the third column with a name beside it that has not been said out loud since the thirteenth of May past, and that name is not on any page of this harbor. The two objects of nobody's on that crown are four hundred and seventy-one days old. The bench under that pump is bare for the two hundred and thirty-seventh night. Two lights at about a mile off that point have not moved in fifty-five days. A man of about fifty-one has said nothing to anybody about a page of six names for seventy-three days. The sheet of six lines at the back of that end stall is on that wall for the tenth day and the paper of four lines in Fish Street is on that door for the twenty-fifth day and the nine-line sheet with a seal on it is in that front street for forty-two days.**

---

The words were written at the head of the page before anybody was asked, and they were the same eleven times, and they were read out of the page and not out of a memory.

*There is a boat of about nineteen tons lying on a bank four hundred yards down the foul off the south end with her garboard open and soft plank nine feet aft of it, and I want her lifted off that bank and a new piece fitted to her keel, and there is about a foot of water on the flood and about two hours of it in twenty-four and it has to be somebody's day. So on the Tuesday I am asking seven boats of the seven boats in this harbor to go out in company and keep her in company and go home in company, and there is to be one person on the water from about seven in the morning until the last of the ebb, and that person is paid a day at the figure in chalk on a slate at an end stall out of this yard's own figures and not out of your boat and not out of you. You may say no. If you say no I will not come back and ask you again, and I will not ask you for anything else this month, and nobody will come to your door about it.*

The page had a line for each of the eleven masters of that harbor who had a boat in it, and a name and an hour against each line, and the page was that yard's own and was in the ship's left hand and it is not the leaf with the four columns on it and no part of that leaf is on it.

---

Ormond Kettle, of about forty-four, master of the *Sorrel*, of about nineteen tons, lying on the hard at the third lay with her heel off and her garboard open since June, said yes at about nine in the morning at the head of that slip, and said the reason, which was that her heel is off and that he has a mould problem and that he has been waiting eleven weeks for anybody to tell him it was a mould problem.

Ham Birt, of about fifty, master of a boat of about twenty-two tons of the second lay, said yes at about ten and gave the reason that he can pull and that there is nothing about pulling that he cannot do, and he asked one question back, which was who pays for the damage, and was told that he would be told in a week what the answer was.

Silas Pride, of about fifty-two, master of a boat of about nine tons of the first lay, said yes at about half past ten and said that he had been in eleven of these things since June and that he had never once been asked whether he could be in one.

Alban Pree, of about sixty, of the first lay, forty-one years on that water, said yes at about eleven and said out loud, in front of his own mate, that he had been told in July that he would get nothing for that and that he has got nothing, and that he was saying yes anyway and was not going to be thanked for it and did not want to be.

Sedge Rundle, of about forty-six, out of Lowfen, master of a boat of about fourteen tons, said yes at about half past eleven at the head of that slip, and nobody had told him to come in and he had been in the harbor since the Tuesday.

Jens Ower, of about twenty-nine, of no lay, master of a lighter of about eleven tons he gave up a road of about eleven tons for in that market a fortnight ago in front of nine people, said yes at about two in the afternoon and said that he had had nothing since and was going to have nothing until the season turns, and that he was not saying it to anybody to be let off and was not saying it to be thanked.

Jos Lund, master of the *Alewife*, of about forty-four, said yes at about three in the afternoon on the bank, in the rain, on his own boat's ground, and the reason he gave was that it was his boat and that he had put nine men on a deck at a quarter to seven in the morning for nothing and that he wanted the day of it to be somebody's day.

Elias Voke, fifty-one, herring skipper of the *Anna Ray*, nineteen winters disputed and unpaid, said yes at about four in the afternoon and gave no reason at all and said that if anybody wanted one they could ask the nineteen winters.

Iver Sallow, of about thirty-one, master of the *Ashtide Rose*, said yes at about half past four in the afternoon and said that he had a chart of about nine years standing in a bag for nine years and had never once been asked what it was for.

A man of about twenty-nine of the third lay who asked his own master's leave first and was told to go on with it said yes at about five and did not give a reason.

Nessa Pike, of that second lay, master of a boat of about thirteen tons of her own, said yes at about twenty past five in the evening on her own deck and gave her hours, which were from about seven in the morning until the last of the ebb, and said that her hours were her hours and that they went into that day at the figure and that the bill for the whole of it came to her own table and not to anybody else's.

---

Rowan Teel, of about fifty-one, of the second lay, whose right forearm is set out of his own hand and has been since March and who has not been to a bonesetter on his own account, said no at about a quarter to six in the evening on the second lay.

"I have got one arm that closes and one that does not," he said. "And you are going to want about eight men who can pull on a line, and I would be the ninth, and I would be in the way and I would be slow at the one minute of a hundred and five that there is. So the answer is no and it is not for a reason that would make you ask me again, and I would rather say it now at a quarter to six than have somebody come to my door about it on the Tuesday."

He was not asked again on the twenty-seventh, or the twenty-eighth, or the twenty-ninth, or the thirtieth, or the first or the second of October. On each of those six days he was at his own bench in the front room of a shop in the second lay from about eight in the morning until about six, planing and fitting, and nobody came in there with a piece of paper, and at about half past ten on the Tuesday the twenty-eighth a man of about nineteen came past that shop and said good morning to him and did not stop, and that was the whole of it.

---

Sedge Rundle of Lowfen sat on the bollard at the head of that slip until about half past six and would not go home to his lodging, and the woman who prices things put one question to him about how he finds the channel in a place that has no soundings on any paper in that harbor, and he gave the answer out loud and it is on the page because he said it and because somebody in that shed wrote it down.

"I go by three things and none of them is on a paper," he said. "A double post at the top of the flood with the top one white and the bottom one tarred, and that is a woman called Nera Sedge who put it up in the year her husband died and it is hers and not the town's. And a raft of sticks a man's length across on the inside of the point, and a man of about sixty there puts a bundle of hazel out on it every spring and takes it down in the autumn and nobody pays him for it and nobody has ever asked him why. And a bell on a frame at the low end. And that bell is rung by whoever wants to come through, and if it is not rung then somebody is in the channel and you wait, and I have waited two hours on the strength of a bell not being rung and I have never once been wrong about it."

"And who makes the marks?"

"Somebody makes the marks. It is not my business and I have never asked, and I have got a boat of about fourteen tons and I have got the three of them in my head and that is the whole of what I steer by, and I would not put a chart of that ground on paper for anything, because in ten years the reeds will have moved the point and the paper would be a lie with my name on it."

Nobody wrote any of that on a sheet and the woman who prices things wrote it in the second column with a reason against it and no name against it.

---

Seven boats went on that page as seven boats. The eleven lines carry a name, an hour, a boat and a yes or a no, and there is no word at the head of that page above the eleven sentences, and none was asked for in that shed on the said day between about nine in the morning and about half past five in the evening. What is true is that about thirty men in seven boats agreed to be on that water on the Tuesday and not one of the seven owners was asked for a penny and not one of the thirty men was put on a wage out of any of them.

A woman of about fifty-eight with the end stall stood at the head of that slip with her slate against her hip while that page was being read out and she said one thing.

"That is my figure," she said. "One hundred and sixty-eight pence for a day's work with a boat and two men. I wrote it up in chalk on the first of June after the oil went and I have not been asked what it is for and I am not going to be, and if somebody in this harbor is going to stand on a bank four times at a quarter to four in the morning then they are standing on my figure and that is all right with me, and I am not going to be told what to do with it and neither is that slate."

---

There is no flour in that market and there has been none since the spring. A barrel of lamp oil at four hundred and fifty pence is on that board with February's three hundred and seventy-eight pence standing visible above it and both dates on it, and there has been no barrel of that oil in that harbor since the twenty-ninth day of August. The sheet of six lines is on the wall at the back of that end stall for the tenth day on the outside of that figure, and it names no person and no town and has a charge of one hundred and sixty-eight pence at the foot of it, and no claim has been lodged under it in this harbor and no penny of it has been paid. The nine-line sheet with a seal on it is in the front street for forty-two days and is a different paper. The paper of four lines in Fish Street is on that door for the twenty-fifth day. The mole-head light is out. The north berth is out. One berth on the third lay is short. The tide gang is four men short. The hard at Ashtide is unanswered. The chandler's note is at thirty-one with its three counts at twenty-two, thirteen and eleven and it is not re-written. Forty-eight weeks stand fallen due with no envelope against any of them.

---

**Eleven masters of that harbor were asked one at a time between about nine in the morning and about twenty past five in the evening on the said Saturday, in the same eleven sentences written at the head of a page before anybody was asked and read out of the page and not out of a memory, and every line of that page carries a name and an hour against it. Ten said yes and one said no. The one is a man of about fifty-one of the second lay whose right forearm is set out of his own hand and who gave his reason in his own mouth and said that he would rather say no at a quarter to six than have somebody come to his door about it on the Tuesday, and he was not asked again on any of the six days after it and was at his own bench in the front room of a shop in the second lay from about eight until about six on each of them and nobody came in there with a piece of paper. Seven boats and about thirty men are in it. One of the seven is a boat of about fourteen tons out of Lowfen whose master of about forty-six said out loud that he finds that channel by three things and none of them is on a paper, being a double post with the top one white, a raft of sticks a man's length across that a man of about sixty there puts out every spring and takes down in the autumn for nothing, and a bell on a frame at the low end which is rung by whoever wants to come through and which, when it is not rung, means somebody is in the channel and a boat waits; and who said that he would not put a chart of that ground on paper for anything because in ten years the reeds will have moved the point and the paper would be a lie with his name on it. One of the seven is a boat of about thirteen tons of the second lay belonging to the woman of that yard who prices things, who gave her hours and said that the bill for the whole of it comes to her own table and not to anybody else's. A woman of about fifty-eight with the end stall stood at the head of that slip and said that the figure on that page is her figure and that she wrote it up in chalk on the first of June and is not going to be told what to do with it. One hundred and sixty-eight pence came in at about eleven in the morning at the figure in chalk on a slate at an end stall and the bag stands at five hundred and thirty-two pence, being three marks four shillings and fourpence.**

*First column, entered on Saturday the twenty-fifth day of September next at about half past nine in the evening in that shed: that eleven masters of that harbor were asked one at a time on the said Saturday between about nine in the morning and about twenty past five in the evening, in the same eleven sentences, written at the head of a page before anybody was asked and read out of the page; and that a line for each of the eleven carries a name and an hour against it, and that ten of the eleven said yes and one said no, and that the one who said no is a man of about fifty-one of the second lay whose right forearm is set out of his own hand, and that his reason was given in his own mouth and was that he would be the ninth man on a line of eight and that he would be in the way and slow at the one minute of a hundred and five that there is; and that he was not asked again on the twenty-seventh, the twenty-eighth, the twenty-ninth or the thirtieth of September or on the first or the second of October, and was at his own bench in the front room of a shop in the second lay from about eight in the morning until about six on each of those six days, and that nobody came in there with a piece of paper; and that seven boats and about thirty men were named in it, being a boat of about nineteen tons of the third lay on the hard with her heel off, a herring boat of the third lay, a boat of about nine tons of the first lay, a boat of about fourteen tons out of Lowfen, a lighter of about eleven tons, a boat of about thirteen tons of the second lay belonging to the woman who prices things, and the boat on the bank itself, which is the *Alewife* of about nineteen tons; and that a man of about forty-six out of Lowfen said out loud that he finds that channel by three things and none of them is on a paper; and that one hundred and sixty-eight pence came into that yard at about eleven in the morning for a day's work with a boat and two men at a berth at the figure in chalk on a slate at an end stall, and that three hundred and sixty-four plus one hundred and sixty-eight is five hundred and thirty-two pence at the close of the said day, being three marks four shillings and fourpence, and short of the wage of four marks by one hundred and eight pence, being nine shillings; and that no charge was placed on that cistern on the said day and the said day was not a wipe and was not a wage Tuesday.*
*Second column, not checkable from a mole, entered on the said Saturday at about half past nine in the evening in that shed, and no name is against it: that the reason the three things a man out of Lowfen steers by are entered in this column and not in the first is that they are not a count of anything anybody can check from a mole, being a post with the top one white and a raft of sticks and a bell, and that they are on the coast and not in this harbor and no stranger can go and stand at them without being taken out; and that the reason no sheet carries those three things is that the man who said them out loud said in the same breath that a chart of that ground would in ten years be a lie with his name on it, and this yard is not going to be the reason a thing is a lie; and that the reason the bill for the whole of that Tuesday is to come to the woman's own table is in her own mouth and is that her hours are her hours and go into that day at the figure, and that the woman of fifty-eight with the end stall says in her own mouth that the figure on that page is her figure and that she wrote it up in chalk on the first of June and is not going to be told what to do with it; and that the reason the eleven sentences were written at the head of that page before anybody was asked rather than said from a door is in the ship's own mouth and is that a man who is asked a thing from a door is being asked a thing he cannot get out of, and that this yard has one page and eleven lines on it and has not got a second page; and that the reason the hours are on that page and not only the yes or the no is that a year from now somebody will want to know whether a man was asked at nine in the morning or at four in the afternoon and the only place that is written is the hour; and that the reason no figure is set against a man of about fifty-one whose right forearm is set out of his own hand is that a man is not a figure and that what that page says about him is a name, an hour, a boat and a no; and that the reason no name is against the man of about fifty-one on the crown of that mole is that he was not asked anything on the said day and nothing was taken to him, and the standing list entered in full on the fourteenth of August past is not entered in full on the said Saturday and stands, and no part of it was paid, reduced, advanced or improved on that day.*
