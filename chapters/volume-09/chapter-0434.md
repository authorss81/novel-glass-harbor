# Chapter 434

*Sunday the Twenty-Sixth Day of September, a Twelfth Week Beginning With the Three Nots of the Eighth of June Past Standing Unasked in It, a Room over a Joiner's Shop at the Top of Fish Street Let Out for the Fourth Time With a Bowl on the Step at Two o'Clock and a Man of About Forty-Six Reading Heads off a Long Table With His Own Name and the Hour Under Each, a Bellwright of About Thirty-Four off the Western Reef Line Coming Down off a Coast at About Half Past Ten in the Morning With Her Own Tools in a Box and a Paper of Lead Cuts in the Lid of It, Five Shillings Named Against One Hundred and Sixty-Eight Pence Said Out Loud Once, and a Mould Taken off a Keel on a Hard With Battens and a Pitch, Two Baulks of Oak Planed at One Hundred and Sixty-Eight Pence, and the Bag at Three Hundred and Sixty-Four Pence*

Sunday the twenty-sixth day of September, at the top of Fish Street, at the third lay, in that shed, and on that mole head.

**The said Sunday was not a rota day and was not a wage Tuesday and was not a wipe, and no charge was placed on that cistern on it. Two hundred and sixty-eight pence did not come in on it. One hundred and sixty-eight pence went out of that yard at about half past eleven in the morning for two baulks of oak planed on a bench in that yard, and there are three hundred and sixty-four pence in the bag at the close of the said day, being two marks three shillings and eightpence, and the figure that bag is short of the wage of four marks is two hundred and seventy-six pence, being one mark nine shillings and eightpence, and it is not going to be paid and nobody is to write one word under it saying anybody is going to. A twelfth week began on the said day and the three nots standing against the eighth of June past are in the first day of it, and nobody in that crew has said one word about them and nobody is going to.**

**And the far bench was occupied from about ten in the morning until about four in the afternoon and was not approached, and the holder of that writ did not go over to him, and nothing was put on that bench and read as a gift, and neither brother has said why. Mara Venn was in that harbor and going nowhere, of no office, not on the crew, not restored, not forgiven, forty marks first after food, neither reduced nor paid, and she was in that room over a joiner's shop for about fifty minutes and read nothing. Tobias Wren was on the crown of that mole from ten until four, being sixty-one, and on the said day he sat on the second piece for the whole of the four hours and did nothing to it and neither brother went near the other at any hour of the day. The notice of the seventeenth of May past is on its wall in a different street for one hundred and thirty-two days. The bellwright of that harbor has not been asked for one hundred and thirty-four days, the chest is shut, and the eight marks and a day of a boat have not been argued, and no digit of anything he ever said is on a page. The hand of a boy of twenty-two is still at the head of the third column with a name beside it that has not been said out loud since the thirteenth of May past, and that name is not on any page of this harbor. The two objects of nobody's on that crown are four hundred and seventy-two days old. The bench under that pump is bare for the two hundred and thirty-eighth night. Two lights at about a mile off that point have not moved in fifty-six days. A man of about fifty-one has said nothing to anybody about a page of six names for seventy-four days. The sheet of six lines at the back of that end stall is on that wall for the eleventh day and the paper of four lines in Fish Street is on that door for the twenty-sixth day and the nine-line sheet with a seal on it is in that front street for forty-three days.**

---

She came down off that coast on a boat that was not hers, at about half past ten in the morning, with a box of tools in one hand and her own bag in the other, and she paid the man for the passage out of her own hand and would not say what it was and nobody asked.

Mina Kett, of about thirty-four, a bellwright, of Tidewatch on the western reef line, where she has a workshop with a door and a nail in the door frame and a bench that she has had since she was twenty-six.

She came into that shed with the box on the bench and put her hand flat on the lid and then took it off again.

"You want a mould off a keel," she said. "You want it to a pitch. You cannot get a light on the keel so you want somebody who has taken one off a hull that was still in the water when it was done."

"Yes."

"Then that is what I do and it is the same work and it is called something else here." She opened the box. "Show me your boat on the hard."

---

The *Sorrel* is of about nineteen tons and she has been on that hard since June with her heel off and her garboard open, and her keel is up out of the bilge water where a man can stand on her floors and put both hands on it.

Mina Kett did not measure anything. She had about fourteen battens of three-eighth inch deal in a bundle, and she soaked the ends of them in the bucket and then wrung them and left them, and while they were wet she shaved one end of each with a drawknife to a bevel that she got by holding two of them against the light and looking at the line between them, and she did that eleven times until she had eleven battens that would all lie against one another and no daylight between them anywhere along their length.

Then she got on that boat's floors with her left knee in the bilge water and put the eleven battens down the length of that keel, one under the other, and worked them down the curve with a wooden mallet and a piece of belaying pin, and she worked from the garboard forward to the stem and back again three times, and she did not stop to look at anything else, and nobody on that hard talked.

It took her about two hours and ten minutes and it is a thing that cannot be done properly by anybody who has not done it.

When she came off she had eleven battens with one edge bent to the curve of a keel about nineteen feet long, and she stood them against the end wall of that shed in the order she had taken them off, and numbered them on the ends in pencil with her own marks and not with figures, because a figure is for a thing that can be measured twice by two people and a bend in a piece of deal is not that.

"Your new piece goes on to that," she said. "It is not a drawing. It is a bit of that keel. You take it to the saw and take it off and then you fair the ends of the new one to the ends of that one and it will not be a guess. It is still a guess. It is a guess with a curve in it instead of a straight one."

---

They paid her a day at the figure in chalk on a slate at an end stall.

One hundred and sixty-eight pence, being one mark and eightpence, out of that yard's own figures, and it goes in on the ledger on the Tuesday with everything else, and the wood for two baulks of oak went out of that same bag on the Sunday morning at that same figure, which is a caulker's figure and was used for wood because there is no other figure in this yard's book and the book has two.

She was told what it was going to be and she said it out loud, in that shed, in front of the two people in it, and she said it once.

"That is a caulker's figure," she said. "That is what this harbor pays a man for putting oakum in a seam in a day. I am not a caulker and I have not been paid a caulker's figure in nine years and I am not going to be one. A bellwright off that line gets five shillings, being sixty pence, and I have been getting five shillings for nine years and I have never asked anybody for more than that because I have never been in a room where I could. And I am telling you once so that it is in somebody's head, and I am not going to tell you again and I am not going to be thanked for it, and I am going to be here on Tuesday and you may pay me that figure if it is the figure you have."

Nobody thanked her and she did not wait to be thanked and she ate a piece of bread and cheese off the corner of that bench at about half past one and sat down on the bottom step of that boat's ladder while she did it.

---

At about half past two in the afternoon the room over the joiner's shop at the top of Fish Street was let out.

A bowl was put on the top stone of the step at two o'clock and about twenty people went up those stairs over the next half hour and three came down, and eleven of them could not read.

Hobb Lee, of about forty-six, a net-mender of the third lay who has mended nets since he was nine and who can read, stood at the end of the long table and read the head-notes off the bound volume out loud in that room and wrote his own name and the hour at which he finished each one under it in his own hand on the day, on the page, with a stub of pencil.

There was no claim in any of them and no claim on that table and nobody asked him to read one.

The woman of about fifty-eight came up those stairs with her slate and stood at the end of that table for about twenty minutes and did not ask for anything to be read and said one thing out loud to about twenty people, which was that the sheet on the wall in the front street is not on that table and is not in that book and she was not going to ask anybody to read it out in that room.

At the end of it Hobb Lee shut the volume and squared it to the edge of the table and put the bowl on the step again and stood at the head of his own stairs and let everybody out, and the room was empty in about ten minutes.

---

At about four in the afternoon Mina Kett opened the box again and took out of the lid of it a sheet of paper with about forty small pieces of lead on it, and each one of them was cut in a different shape, and about half of them had notches cut into one arm and a hole punched through the head.

"There is a nail in the door frame of my workshop at Tidewatch with about forty of them on it," she said, "and there is a woman there of about sixty-one who copies a cut onto a piece of paper for a penny, and any person on that coast can walk into that workshop and pay the penny and be told what marks that cut stands for. That is the whole of it. There is no office, there is no paper with a seal on it, there is no fee but the penny and it goes to the woman and not to anybody, and nobody has ever been refused at that door in the twenty-six years she has had it."

"And these are not for this coast."

"No. They are not for this coast and I brought them because you are going to ask me for them in about a week and I would rather you had them than that you asked a stranger and got told a half of one." She put the sheet down flat on the bench. "That cut with the hole through the head and two notches on the left arm is not for your water and it is not for yours, and I am not going to say which marks it stands for, and nobody on that coast would tell you either unless you paid the penny, and if you paid the penny you would be told and then you would have something in your hand that you did not earn by being in it."

She left the sheet on the bench and did not leave the paper.

"Take that to your woman who prices things," she said, "and do not let anybody put it on a wall."

---

There is no flour in that market and there has been none since the spring. There has been no barrel of lamp oil in that harbor since the twenty-ninth day of August. A figure of four hundred and fifty pence is on the board at an end stall with three hundred and seventy-eight pence standing visible above it and both dates on that board, and it cannot be taken off. The sheet of six lines is on the wall at the back of that stall for the eleventh day on the outside of that figure, and no claim has been lodged under it and nobody has paid a penny of the one hundred and sixty-eight pence at the foot of it. The nine-line sheet with a seal on it is in the front street for forty-three days and is a different paper on a different wall. The paper of four lines in Fish Street is on that door for the twenty-sixth day and has been answered by nobody. The mole-head light is out. The north berth is out. The fire party's hose is dead until the spring. The tide gang is four men short. The hard at Ashtide is unanswered since the ninth of November past. The chandler's note is at thirty-one and is not re-written. Forty-eight weeks stand fallen due with no envelope against any of them and the forty-ninth falls on Tuesday the twenty-seventh day of this month.

**A bellwright of about thirty-four named Mina Kett came down off the western reef line at about half past ten in the morning on the said Sunday and stood on the floors of a boat of about nineteen tons that has been on that hard since June with her heel off, and took eleven battens off that keel to a pitch in about two hours and ten minutes with a drawknife and a mallet and a piece of belaying pin and with nobody talking, and stood the eleven up against a wall in the order she had taken them off and numbered the ends in pencil with her own marks and not with figures, and said out loud that a new piece goes on to that and that it is not a drawing and is a bit of that keel, and that it is still a guess and it is a guess with a curve in it instead of a straight one. She was told she would be paid a day at the figure in chalk on a slate at an end stall, being one hundred and sixty-eight pence, being one mark and eightpence, and she said out loud once in that shed that that is a caulker's figure and that she is not a caulker, that a bellwright off that line gets five shillings, being sixty pence, and that she has been getting five shillings for nine years and has never asked anybody for more, and that she was saying it once so that it would be in somebody's head and was not going to say it again and was not going to be thanked for it. A room over a joiner's shop at the top of Fish Street was let out at two o'clock and about twenty people went up and three came down and eleven of the twenty could not read, and a man of about forty-six read the head-notes off the long table out loud and wrote his own name and the hour under each in his own hand, and there was no claim in any of them and nobody asked him to read one. About forty small pieces of lead were put on the bench out of the lid of her box at about four in the afternoon, cut in different shapes, about half of them notched on one arm with a hole through the head, and she said there is a nail in the door frame of her workshop at Tidewatch with about forty of them on it and a woman of about sixty-one there who copies a cut onto a paper for a penny to any person who walks in, and that nobody has ever been refused at that door in twenty-six years, and that these are not for this coast and she is not going to say which marks they stand for, and that the sheet was to go to the woman who prices things and was not to be put on a wall. Two baulks of oak planed went out of that yard at about half past eleven in the morning at one hundred and sixty-eight pence and the bag stands at three hundred and sixty-four pence, being two marks three shillings and eightpence.**

*First column, entered on Sunday the twenty-sixth day of September next at about half past nine in the evening in that shed: that a twelfth week began on the said day, and that the three nots standing against the eighth of June past stand in the first day of that week unasked, and that nobody in that crew has said one word about them on any day of that week or any other; and that a bellwright of about thirty-four named Mina Kett came down off the western reef line at about half past ten in the morning with her own tools in a box in one hand and her own bag in the other and paid her own passage out of her own hand; and that she took eleven battens off the keel of a boat of about nineteen tons lying on the hard at the third lay with her heel off since June, in about two hours and ten minutes, and that the eleven stand against the end wall of that shed in the order they were taken off with her own pencil marks on the ends and no figure on any of them; and that one hundred and sixty-eight pence went out of that yard at about half past eleven in the morning for two baulks of oak planed on a bench in that yard, and that five hundred and thirty-two minus one hundred and sixty-eight is three hundred and sixty-four pence at the close of the said day, being two marks three shillings and eightpence, being short of the wage of four marks by two hundred and seventy-six pence, being one mark nine shillings and eightpence; and that a room over a joiner's shop at the top of Fish Street was let out at two o'clock on the said day and about twenty people went up those stairs and three came down and eleven of the twenty could not read, and that a man of about forty-six read the head-notes off the long table out loud from two o'clock until about twenty past three and wrote his own name and the hour under each in his own hand, and that there was no claim in any of them and no claim on that table; and that about twenty-eight days stand between the eleventh week and a thirteenth week and no week turns on a day that is not a week; and that no charge was placed on that cistern on the said day and the said day was not a wipe and was not a wage Tuesday.*
*Second column, not checkable from a mole, entered on the said Sunday at about half past nine in the evening in that shed, and no name is against it: that the reason a bellwright of about thirty-four says out loud once that the figure on a slate at an end stall is a caulker's figure and is not hers is in her own mouth and is that she has been getting five shillings for nine years and has never been in a room where she could ask for more, and that she told this yard once and did not tell it again on any of the six days after it, and that nobody in that yard thanked her for saying it; and that the reason no figure is entered against the eleven battens and the two hours and ten minutes is that a bend in a piece of deal is not a figure of anything anybody can check from a mole and that a stranger with a pencil cannot take a curve; and that the reason a woman who came four hundred miles down that coast was not put on a sheet of anything in this harbor is in the holder's own mouth and is that a tradesperson who comes into a yard for a day and is not asked to be anything is a tradesperson, and that the moment a name goes on a page about her she has been asked to be something and she was not; and that the reason her own figure of five shillings was written down by a woman with a slate who was not her and not that yard's is in that woman's own mouth and is that a figure that somebody else has said out loud once and will not say again is a figure that would otherwise be gone in a month, and that this yard's book has no column for what a person says about what she is paid and the second column is where it goes; and that the reason about forty pieces of lead are on that bench and are entered here and not in the first column is that they are not for this coast, and that the reason they are not on a wall is in her own mouth and is that nobody is to put them on a wall; and that the reason about twenty people went up those stairs at two o'clock on the said Sunday is in that room's own record and is that it is let out on a Sunday and anybody may come and there is a bowl on the step and nobody is at the door to ask who, and that this yard asked nobody in there and was in there for about fifty minutes and read nothing; and that the reason the woman of fifty-eight with the end stall was in that room over a joiner's shop on the said Sunday and asked for nothing is in her own mouth and is that the sheet on the wall in the front street is not on that table and is not in that book; and that the reason no name is against the man of about fifty-one on the crown of that mole is that he was not asked anything on the said day and nothing was taken to him, and the standing list entered in full on the fourteenth of August past is not entered in full on the said Sunday and stands, and no part of it was paid, reduced, advanced or improved on that day.*
