# Chapter 402

*Wednesday the Twenty-Fifth of August, a Refusal Pinned at the Corner of the Same Door in the Hand of the Man Who Opened It and Nine Days of It Running to the Second of September, a Woman of No Office on a Mole at About Four in the Afternoon With the One Thing She Came Down Off That Hill to Say and One Thing She Did Not Say, a Yard That Went Back to Work at About Eight in the Morning With Nothing Came Into It and Nothing Went Out of It, and a Man of About Thirty-Eight With a Boat of About Thirteen Tons and No Papers Who Used the Sixth Clause of a Notice on Its Own Account for the Second Time in Six Weeks and Was Not Thanked*

Wednesday the twenty-fifth of August, in that shed, in a corridor of that office on that hill, and on that mole.

**The said Wednesday was not a wage Tuesday and was not a rota day and was not a wipe, and the wall behind the reeve's bench came down and went up whole at the angle it has been at since the twelfth and nobody put it back. Nothing came into that yard on the said day and nothing went out of it, and there are one hundred and sixty pence in the bag, being one mark, and the figure that bag is short of the wage of four marks is four hundred and eighty pence, being three marks, and it is not going to be paid and nobody is to write one word under it saying anybody is going to. Nothing has been in a tin in that shed since the twenty-eighth day of April past, and about five hundred people walked into this harbor on that Tuesday and came out of it and about one mark and eightpence a head of what they bought went across four counters, and a barrel of lamp oil is four hundred and fifty pence on a board in the front street with February's three hundred and seventy-eight standing above it and both dates on the board, and nobody in that street said a figure of oil out loud and no man went to that counter.**

**And the far bench was occupied on the said Wednesday from about ten in the morning until about four in the afternoon and was not approached on it, and the holder of that writ did not go over to him, and nothing was put on that bench and read as a gift, and neither brother has said why. Mara Venn was in that harbor and going nowhere, of no office, not on the crew, not restored, not forgiven, forty marks first after food, neither reduced nor paid, and she did not go up that hill on the said day and was not asked to and did not say why. Tobias Wren was on the crown of that mole from ten until four, being sixty-one, with eleven pieces of a boat behind him and a boat's hook in his right hand, and he got a third of the way along the eleven and back again and then sat down on the mole stones at the head of the slip and had his dinner out of a tin, and nobody went over to him and he did not look up at anybody. The notice of the seventeenth of May past is on its wall in a different street for one hundred days and has not been touched and has not been taken down, and two older naval notices are further along the same wall. The bellwright of that harbor has not been asked for one hundred and two days, the chest is shut, the eight marks and a day of a boat have not been argued, and no digit of anything he ever said is on a page.**

---

He went up the hill at about ten to ten again and the corridor was empty again and the bench at the end of it had nothing on it and the table against the wall had nothing on it.

There was a second piece of paper on that door. It was pinned at the same corner and it was about the size of a hand and it was underneath the first one and not on top of it, so that the first one was still legible and the second one was not, and a man who wanted to know whether those two people were permitted had to look at the top one, which is what the first one was for.

He read it. He read it four times because he wanted to be sure of the hand.

It was Drury's. He knew it because at about ten past ten on the previous morning the same man had picked up a flat rule and drawn one line across a sheet on the table against the wall, in one movement, without a ruler's wobble, and had then gone back and stood six feet off and had not looked at the line again.

The refusal said, in his own mouth, in four lines:

*A second hour is asked for on this door. It is refused. A second paper will be on this door on the second day of September and not on any day before it.*

There was no seal at the foot of it and there was no office at the foot of it and there was nobody's name at the foot of it but the clerk's, which was not a seal and was a working man's name on a piece of paper he had written himself and would have to unpick himself if the answer came back different.

Calder stood in that corridor for about as long as it takes to read four lines twice, and then he went down the hill.

---

The shed was open at eight and he was in it at about ten past eight and he did the ordinary thing, which is to sit with the leaf open on the bench stone and a chart weighted at the corners and get no further with it than he had got with it the day before, and at about half past eleven Nessa came in and put her own slate down on the bench stone and looked at the chart for about a minute and a half without saying anything, which is her way of saying what she is going to say.

"You want to know what it cost," she said.

"I did not say a word about it."

"You went up that hill and came down it and you have not sat down since and you have turned that leaf round to the clean page twice." She put her thumb on the edge of the chart. "An hour and a half of a man's day, and about ten of a mile on a hill there is no other way up, and a night's sleep, and whatever came off that thing came off you and it is not a thing anybody in this yard can price because there is no rate for it. That is the bill. I am not going to put a figure on the last line of it and neither are you."

"Then put the rest of it in."

"The rest of it is an hour and a half and a hill, and I will put that in with the hour against it, and the rest is going in the second column with the reason against the line, and the reason is that a man of about fifty-three with a bad back is standing at a board on that west basin twice a week and putting his own hand against a figure nobody has asked him to put a hand against, and that is the same bill twice over in one week and this yard did not order either half of it."

"Where do you have a man of about fifty-three with a bad back?"

"I have not got him," said Nessa. "I have got a bad back and a board and a Tuesday, and you will find out the rest of it yourself or you will not."

---

At about four in the afternoon Mara Venn was on the flat of the bench stone with her back to the light and she had not been up that hill and she was not going to.

"I said I would not go up on a Thursday unless the paper changed," she said. "I have come down off that hill and I have not been up it today and you can see that for yourself and I have not come to be asked about it."

"Then what have you come for."

She was quiet for a while, and a cart went up the front street with a bad axle on it, and neither of them watched it go.

"There is a thing about it that is not the thing I said on Monday," she said, "and I have been carrying it since about eleven o'clock yesterday morning and I have not been able to put it down and set it on a table and look at it, and that is the part I did not say on Monday. On Monday I said I had got something. I did not say that it is not sitting still."

"That is not a different thing."

"It is the same thing with the end of it cut off," she said, "and I want you to have both halves of it, and I am not going to say the other half because I do not know whether mine is the one with the light in it or the one without, and if I say it out loud and I am wrong then it will be a thing two people in this harbor are carrying in the wrong order for the rest of the year."

"Say it to me anyway and I will carry it in the wrong order with you."

She looked at him for the first time since she had come up the slip.

"No," she said. "That is the one thing I am not going to do, and you will not talk me out of it, and if you ask me again I will stop coming up that hill altogether and I have told you why once and I am not going to tell you twice."

"Then I will not ask."

"And you are not going to put it in that ledger either, and I know what you are and I know you have got a leaf with four columns on it and a hand that does not stop."

"The second column," Calder said, "with the reason against the line. And the reason is that a man on a mole cannot check it and that a figure nobody can check is a figure a stranger cannot take, and that is the whole of the rule and you knew it before I did."

"Yes," said Mara Venn, "and I am not thanking you for it, and it is not the forty marks, and the light is out and it has been out since the seventh of May and I have not said one word about the light and neither are you."

---

At about half past five he was at the head of the second lay.

The man of about thirty-eight with a boat of about thirteen tons and no papers on her was lying under her counter with a light in a tin hooked over the frames, caulking a seam of his own that nobody had asked him to caulk and that nobody was paying him for, and he had his own stove going under his own counter with about two inches of coal in it, out of his own hand, at about four in the afternoon, in a harbor where a barrel of lamp oil is four hundred and fifty pence and has been since the first of June.

He came up out from under the counter when he heard the step and he did not say good evening and Calder did not say good evening, and neither of them said anything about the seam and neither of them said anything about the coal.

"You went up that hill this morning," the man said.

"I did."

"I saw you go at about ten to ten and I saw you come down at about twenty past eleven and I counted the hours because there is nothing else to do under that counter," he said. "I want to say one thing and then I am going back under there. I went out this morning on the sixth clause of that notice on my own account and I did not tell you and I did not ask you and I am not going to tell you why. I have been out on it twice since the first of June and both times I went on my own book and my own time. That is all I have got and it is not enough to get anybody to sea, and I am not going to ask you for anything and you are not going to thank me for it, and if I ever do ask you for something you can say no."

"I will not thank you for it," Calder said.

"I know you will not," said the man of about thirty-eight, and went back under the counter.

---

**Nothing came into that yard on the said Wednesday and nothing went out of it, and the bag has not moved since the Tuesday and is still one mark against a wage of four, and it is not going to be paid and nobody is going to write one word under it saying anybody is going to pay it. A second paper was pinned under the first one at the corner of a shut door in a corridor on that hill, and it is a refusal in four lines in the hand of the man who opened that door yesterday morning, and it says that a second paper will be on that door on the second day of September and not on any day before it, and there is no seal at the foot of it and no office at the foot of it and only a clerk's own name at the foot of it. The woman of no office came up that mole at about four in the afternoon and said that the thing she is carrying is not sitting still and that she would not say the other half of it and that he was not going to ask her, and that if he asked her again she would stop coming up that hill, and the holder of that writ did not ask her again. The far bench was occupied from ten until four and was not approached. A man of about thirty-eight with a boat of about thirteen tons and no papers on her went out on the sixth clause of a notice on his own account for the second time in six weeks, told nobody, asked nobody, was thanked by nobody, and said so out loud on the second lay at about half past five in the evening and went back under his own counter.**

*First column, entered on Wednesday the twenty-fifth day of August next at about half past nine in the evening in that shed: that the said Wednesday was not a rota day and not a wage Tuesday and not a wipe, and that the wall came down and went up whole at the angle it has been at since the twelfth; and that a second paper was pinned at the corner of the shut door in that corridor underneath the first one and not on top of it, and that it carried four lines and a clerk's own name and no seal and no office; and that the holder read it four times and identified the hand from a line the same man had ruled on a sheet in that room with a flat rule at about ten past ten on the previous morning; and that the four lines were that a second hour is asked for on that door, that it is refused, and that a second paper will be on that door on the second day of September and not on any day before it, and that the refusal begins on the said day and stands nine days; and that the holder went down that hill at about half past ten in the morning and did not go up it again on that day; and that the woman of no office did not go up that hill on the said day and came up the slip to that mole at about four in the afternoon and said that the thing she is carrying is not sitting still, and that she would not say the other half of it, and that if she was asked again she would stop coming up that hill; and that the holder said he would not ask her again; and that a man of about thirty-eight with a boat of about thirteen tons and no papers on her went out on the sixth clause of a notice on the morning of the said day on his own account and came back on the evening's, and said so out loud on the second lay at about half past five in the evening, and that he has been out on it twice since the first of June and that both times he went on his own book and his own time, and that he asked for nothing and was thanked for nothing.*

*Second column, not checkable from a mole, entered on the said Wednesday at about half past nine in the evening in that shed, and no name is against it: that a man on a mole cannot check what came off a thing in a room and that the whole of it is in this column and none of it is in the first, and that the reason against the line is that a figure a stranger cannot take is not a figure; and that the ship's hand put an hour and a half and a hill into the first column with the hour against it and put the rest of the bill into this one with the reason that there is no rate in this harbor for a night's sleep and that she is not going to invent one and neither is he; and that a thing that is not sitting still after a day and a half is a real thing that this yard's own ledger has been carrying since a bell went down over a wreck at the south end in June, and that about a third of what came off that wreck came off a wreck, and that the holder knows that and has not got a use for the knowing; and that a man of about thirty-eight going out on a clause on his own book and his own time is a man of that harbor with a boat of about thirteen tons and no papers on her, and that the sixth clause of that notice has been used by that harbor four times since June and once by seven boats at once, and that a boat of thirteen tons with no papers is a boat and not an instrument; and that a refusal written by a clerk in his own hand on a door is a working man's work and not a decision and not a face, and that the reason a copyist's counter is the whole of a leak is that a trade can be put in a schedule and a refusal cannot; and that the standing list of the fourteenth of August past is carried whole and untouched and is not set out a second time on the said Wednesday, and that a man going out on a clause of a notice on his own book and a woman of no office carrying a thing she will not name are not items on it and are not going on it, and that nothing in it moved on that day.*
