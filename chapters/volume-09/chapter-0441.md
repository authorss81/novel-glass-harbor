# Chapter 441

*Sunday the Third Day of October, a Day on Which No Charge Was Placed on That Cistern and on Which That Wall Came Down and Went Up Whole, the Last Day of a Twelfth Week in Which Nobody in That Crew Has Said One Word About Three Nots, Six Things Named Out Loud in a Shed as What Any Account of Anything in This Harbor Has to Carry and the Refusals Named Among the Six, a Woman of No Office Told in That Shed at About Four in the Afternoon That Her Name Is Going on a Public Sheet for the First Time Since She Came to That Harbor and Her Not Asking for It to Be Taken Off, One Hundred and Sixty-Eight Pence Coming Into That Yard at About Ten in the Morning and One Hundred and Sixty-Eight Going Out of It at About Four in the Afternoon, a Room over a Joiner's Shop at the Top of Fish Street Let Out for the Sixth Time on a Sunday With a Bowl on the Step at Two o'Clock, and a Boat of About Nineteen Tons Still on a Bank About Four Hundred Yards Down That Foul in Nine Foot of Water With Eleven Feet of Caulked Joint in Her That Has Been Wet Once*

Sunday the third day of October, in that shed, at the head of the third lay, in a room over a joiner's shop at the top of Fish Street, and on that bank about four hundred yards down that foul.

**The said Sunday was not a rota day and was not a wage Tuesday and was not a wipe, and no charge was placed on that cistern on it, and the wall behind the reeve's bench came down and went up whole at the angle it has been at since the twelfth and nobody put it back. One hundred and sixty-eight pence came into that yard at about ten in the morning for a day's work with a boat and two men at a berth at the figure in chalk on a slate at an end stall, and one hundred and sixty-eight pence went out of it at about four in the afternoon for a length of three-inch stuff, iron straps, canvas and nails, and there are one hundred and ninety-six pence in the bag at the close of the said day, being one mark and three shillings, and the figure that bag is short of the wage of four marks is four hundred and forty-four pence, being two marks ten shillings and fourpence, and it is not going to be paid and nobody is to write one word under it saying anybody is going to. Nothing has been in a tin in that shed since the twenty-eighth day of April past, and not one penny of the whole of it comes out of the owner of any boat, out of the man with two papers on a table in an office off the west basin, or out of the Crown.**

**And the far bench was occupied from about ten in the morning until about four in the afternoon for the first day of ten and was not approached, and the holder of that writ did not go over to him, and nothing was put on that bench and read as a gift, and neither brother has said why. Mara Venn was in that harbor and going nowhere, of no office, not on the crew, not restored, not forgiven, forty marks first after food, neither reduced nor paid. Tobias Wren was on the crown of that mole from ten until four, being sixty-one, with eleven pieces of a boat behind him and a boat's hook in his right hand, and on the said day he did not move the eleventh piece and looked at the two objects of nobody's about nine feet off him for about an hour and then at the water. The notice of the seventeenth of May past is on its wall in a different street for one hundred and thirty-nine days and has not been touched. The bellwright of that harbor has not been asked for one hundred and forty-one days, the chest is shut, and the eight marks and a day of a boat have not been argued, and no digit of anything he ever said is on a page, and a bellwright of about thirty-four off the western reef line is in that harbor and is not asked one question about him and does not ask him one. The three nots standing against the eighth of June past are a twelfth week and this is the last day of it, and in that week nobody in that crew has said one word about them, and the hand of a boy of twenty-two is still at the head of the third column with a name beside it that has not been said out loud since the thirteenth of May past, and that name is not on any page of this harbor. The two objects of nobody's on that crown are four hundred and seventy-nine days old and neither has moved. The bench under that pump is bare for the two hundred and forty-fifth night. Two lights at about a mile off that point have not moved in sixty-three days, counted from the hour they were first seen on the evening of the first of August past. A man of about fifty-one has said nothing to anybody about a page of six names for eighty-one days. The sheet of six lines at the back of that end stall is on that wall for the eighteenth day and the paper of four lines at the top left of that door in Fish Street is on that door for the thirty-third day, its fourth line ended on the twentieth day of September past, it has been answered by nobody, and the eleven people it names have been told nothing further by anybody in that harbor. The nine-line sheet with a seal on it is in that front street for the fiftieth day.**

---

The boat of about nineteen tons was still on the bank four hundred yards down that foul, in nine foot of water at the top of the tide and about seven foot and a half at the bottom of it, with her port bilge up and a new piece on her keel and about eleven feet of caulked joint in her that had been let into water once.

Nessa Pike stood in the bow of a boat of about eleven tons with her hands on the gunwale and looked at the top of her for a while before she said anything.

"She is not coming off this tide and she is not coming off the next one either, and if anybody in this harbor tells a boat's owner that she comes off on Tuesday they are telling him a thing they have not got."

"Why not."

"Because that joint is caulked oakum and tar and it has been in the water once, and it goes soft the second time it soaks and it sets hard the second time it dries, and a joint that has done one of each is not a joint yet." She put her thumb on the gunwale and took it off again. "She has to be wet twice and dry once between and that is about four days of tide whatever the weather does, and the weather is not going to do anything for us this week. She is coming off on the Monday and she is coming off because the joint will have been wet twice by then, and I will put that in the second column in a reason against the line and not in the first, because a man on a mole cannot check what a joint feels like."

The smaller steam tender of about forty tons lay off that bank with fifteen fathoms of chain off her quarter round a spurle and back with a weight on the tail of it, and Halvard Ness was on her after deck in oilskins at about half past seven in the morning, and he had a kettle on her galley stove and he had made it for about nine men and there were nine men in it, and none of them is paid by that tender and none of them is paid by this yard today, and both of those things are true and the second one is the reason he can pull the chain.

He had not pulled it. He said at about eight o'clock, to nobody in particular, that a chain weight is a thing for a man who has a reason, and that he had not got one this morning, and that if a man wanted a reason he was to go and get one from whoever had sent him.

---

At about eleven in the morning the holder of that writ put six things on the bench stone in that shed and said them out loud, and two people were in that shed and one of them wrote them down on the slate with them and the other one did not write anything because she was not going to.

"This is what an account has to carry," he said, "and I am saying it now while the three of us are standing up, because in three days it is going to be on a piece of paper and I will not be able to change a word of it after that. It carries what the thing is. It carries what the thing is not. It carries where the thing came from. It carries what the thing does not show. It carries the refusals. And it carries the people."

"That is five."

"That is five, and the sixth is on the wall and I will read it to you. The sixth is the seam, and the sixth is a thing we have not got a copy of and are not going to get one of."

Nessa Pike looked up from the slate.

"The refusals are on it too and are not smaller than the rest," she said, "and if that sixth thing is on the same page then somebody is going to come along in about six weeks and put their thumb on the refusals and say that the sixth thing is the same size as the other five and that is not a thing this page can be asked to carry. It goes on its own page or it goes nowhere."

"It goes on its own page."

"Then say what you are going to do about the first thing, because a thing that is what a thing is, written by you, is a thing that cannot be argued with, and there are going to be people in that room who cannot read it and they are going to be told it by a man with a thing in his own hand, and that is not the same as a page."

"I know it is not the same as a page."

"Say what you are going to do about it."

"I am going to say on the face of it that the person who reads it out is named and the hour is under it, and that anybody in that room may stop him at any word and put their own words against his, and that what he reads is what is on the paper and not what he makes of it," he said, "and I am going to print that rule on the paper so that a stranger with a pencil can see that the paper says it and that the room did it."

Nessa Pike wrote that down too.

---

At about four in the afternoon, in that shed, with the door open on the slip and the light going off the bench stone, he said it to her.

"Mara. There is a piece of paper going into a public record in an office on that hill and on that paper there is going to be a line with three names against it under a head that says who holds it, and one of the three names is yours."

She had been sitting on the upturned crate at the head of that bench since about half past two and she did not get up.

"I know you are telling me before and not after," she said. "I have had about six hours to work out that this is what you were going to do, because I have been on this slip every day since the twenty-second of July and I have not been thanked once and I have not been asked once, and a thing that has not been asked for is not on anybody's sheet, and the only way it comes on a sheet is if somebody puts it there."

"I am putting it there."

"Then put it there." She put her hands flat on the bench stone on either side of her knees. "I am not going to ask you to take it off and you are not going to offer to take it off, and I want that said in this shed now so that neither of us has to stand up in a room in about six weeks and make a speech about it. I have been on this slip since the twenty-second of July and nobody in this harbor has ever put my name on a piece of paper, and a thing that has never been on a piece of paper is a thing that has never been in this harbor in the way this harbor counts things. Forty marks first after food stands against my name and is neither reduced nor paid by my name being on a piece of paper in an office on a hill, and it is not going to be reduced by it and I am not going to be restored by it and I am not going to be thanked, because there is nobody in that room who has done anything that wants thanking, and I am one of the three names on it now, and I have not asked to be one of the three names on it and I am on it anyway, and that is the shape of the thing and I would rather it were said out loud than discovered in six weeks."

"You have not asked to be one of the three names on it."

"No. And I am not going to ask to be taken off it either, because a person who asks to be taken off a piece of paper is a person who has been on it."

That was the whole of what was said about it in that shed, and neither of them said one word about it again on any of the ten days that followed, and there was no speech and there was nothing put on a slate about it and it is in the second column of that leaf in the yard's own mouth with the reason against the line.

---

At about two o'clock in the afternoon the room over a joiner's shop at the top of Fish Street was let out for the sixth time since the first of September.

A bowl was put on the top stone of the step at two o'clock and about twenty people went up those stairs over the next half hour and three came down, and eleven of them could not read. Hobb Lee, of about forty-six, read the head-notes off the long table out loud from two o'clock until about half past three and wrote his own name and the hour under each in his own own hand, and there was no claim in any of them and no claim on that table and nobody asked him for one.

The twenty-second head of that volume carries three lines and a dash, and it carried three lines and a dash at two o'clock on that Sunday, and there is a finding about it in pencil on the inside of the back cover of that volume with a name and a date against it, and it is not to be repaired, explained or named against, and it was not repaired, explained or named against on the said day and nothing was said about it at that table.

On the way down, at about half past three, a man of about sixty-one who lives in two rooms above a net-loft and who has not been in that room before asked Hobb Lee whether a person could put a thing in that book that they had not seen with their own eyes. Hobb Lee said that they could not, and that what went in that book was what somebody had in front of them, and that if a person came in and asked for a line that was not there, the answer was that the line was not there and that was the end of it, and the man said that was fair and went down the stairs.

**A boat of about nineteen tons is on a bank about four hundred yards down that foul off the south end of that mole and there is nine foot on that bank at the top of the water and about seven foot and a half at the bottom of it, and about a hundred and five minutes of ebb in twenty-four hours, and a company of seven boats and about thirty men is worth exactly that and no more, and there is a named tender lying off that bank with fifteen fathoms of chain off her quarter and a weight on the tail of it that a man of about twenty-three can pull without asking anybody's leave, and the reason he can is that no penny of his comes out of any of the seven boats and none of the seven can tell him not to. THE JOINT IN THAT BOAT IS ABOUT ELEVEN FEET AND IT HAS BEEN WET ONCE AND IT HAS TO BE WET TWICE, AND A FIGURE OF NINE FOOT AND A FIGURE OF ELEVEN FEET ARE THE WHOLE OF WHAT IS OWED TO HER AND NEITHER OF THEM IS A PRICE AND NEITHER OF THEM IS GOING ON A SLATE. THERE IS NO FLOUR IN THAT MARKET AND THERE IS NO BARREL OF LAMP OIL IN THAT HARBOR AND A FIGURE OF FOUR HUNDRED AND FIFTY PENCE, BEING THIRTY-SEVEN SHILLINGS AND SIXPENCE, IS ON A BOARD AT AN END STALL QUOTED ON THE FIRST OF JUNE WITH FEBRUARY'S THREE HUNDRED AND SEVENTY-EIGHT PENCE, BEING THIRTY-ONE SHILLINGS AND SIXPENCE, STANDING VISIBLE ABOVE IT AND BOTH DATES ON THAT BOARD, AND THE FIGURE OF THE FIRST OF JUNE DID NOT COME DOWN AND CANNOT BE TAKEN OFF AGAIN, AND ON THE OUTSIDE OF THAT BOARD, ABOUT FOUR INCHES TO THE LEFT OF IT, IS A SHEET OF SIX LINES THAT HAS BEEN ON THAT WALL FOR THE EIGHTEENTH DAY AND IS ANSWERED BY NOBODY. A WOMAN OF NO OFFICE HAS HAD HER NAME PUT ON A PIECE OF PAPER IN A PUBLIC RECORD FOR THE FIRST TIME SINCE SHE CAME TO THAT HARBOR AND HAS NOT ASKED FOR IT TO BE TAKEN OFF AND HAS NOT BEEN THANKED FOR IT AND NOBODY HAS OFFERED.**

*First column, entered on Sunday the third day of October next at about half past nine in the evening in that shed: that the said day was not a rota day and was not a wage Tuesday and was not a wipe, and that no charge was placed on that cistern on it and that the wall behind the reeve's bench came down and went up whole at the angle it has been at since the twelfth and nobody put it back; and that one hundred and sixty-eight pence came into that yard at about ten in the morning and one hundred and sixty-eight pence went out of it at about four in the afternoon, and that one hundred and ninety-six plus one hundred and sixty-eight less one hundred and sixty-eight is one hundred and ninety-six pence at the close of the said day, being one mark and three shillings, being short of the wage of four marks by four hundred and forty-four pence, being two marks ten shillings and fourpence; and that the said day is the last day of a twelfth week of the three nots standing against the eighth of June past and that no word was said about them in that week by anybody in that crew; and that a boat of about nineteen tons is on a bank about four hundred yards down that foul with about eleven feet of caulked joint in her that has been wet once, and that a woman of that yard of about twenty-seven said out loud in her own mouth at about eleven in the morning on that boat's own deck that the joint has to be wet twice and dry once between and is coming off on the Monday the fourth, and that she said it would not do to tell a boat's owner that a boat comes off on a day a man cannot check; *Second column, not checkable from a mole, entered on the said Sunday at about half past nine in the evening in that shed, and no name is against it: that the reason a woman of about twenty-seven of that yard is paying for a day at the figure in chalk on a slate at an end stall, being one hundred and sixty-eight pence, being one mark and eightpence, for a day's work with a boat and two men at a berth, and that the same figure is a caulker's day, is in the ship's own mouth and is that a boat and two men on a berth is what the money is for and that a caulker has to be asked for a day by name on a day and is paid at the same figure on the flat of the bench stone at about four in the afternoon; and that the reason no name is against the woman of no office in that column is that she has not asked to be on anything, and that her name is on a piece of paper in an office on a hill because she is one of three people who hold it, and that forty marks first after food stands whole against her and is neither reduced nor paid by her name being on a paper; and that the reason no figure is set against a boat of about nineteen tons on a bank, or against eleven feet of caulked joint in her, or against about thirty men going out on four days with a small steam tender ahead of them, is that labour and a joint are not checkable from a mole and that a figure against any of them would be a price put on about thirty men by a woman with a slate; and that the reason the sixth thing on that account is on a page of its own is in the ship's own mouth and is that a person who cannot read is going to be told that page by a man with it in his own hand and that is not the same as a page and that a page that carries both will get the other one shrunk; and that the reason the refusals are on the account at all is that a refusal that only stands in a shed is a thing a person can be told did not happen; and that the standing list entered in full on the fourteenth of August past is not entered in full on the said Sunday and stands, and no part of it was paid, reduced, advanced or improved on that day.*
