# Chapter 406

*Sunday the Twenty-Ninth of August, a Man of About Twenty-Three at the Other End of a Garboard on the Second Lay With Nobody Standing Over Him, Two Rooms That Cannot Both Be the Room a Thing Was Cut In, a Woman of No Office Who Says One of the Two Out Loud and Is Not Asked One Question Back, the Fourth Block of a Return Counted at a Wall on the Same Morning, the Fifth Day of a Refusal on a Door, and Two Lights at About a Mile Off the Point That Have Not Moved in Twenty-Eight Days*

Sunday the twenty-ninth of August, on the second lay, at the office on the west basin, on that mole, and in that shed.

**The said Sunday was not a wage Tuesday and was not a rota day and was not a wipe, and the wall behind the reeve's bench came down and went up whole at the angle it has been at since the twelfth and nobody put it back. Nothing came into that yard on the said day and nothing went out of it, and there are two hundred and eighty-four pence in the bag, being one mark ten shillings and fourpence, and the figure that bag is short of the wage of four marks is three hundred and fifty-six pence, being two marks three shillings, and it is not going to be paid and nobody is to write one word under it saying anybody is going to. Nothing has been in a tin in that shed since the twenty-eighth day of April past, and the caulker of about twenty-three was on the flat of the bench stone at half past three that morning because he cannot sleep, and nobody in that harbor was on that stone with him, and he was not asked for that day by anybody and did not offer it, and there is no coal in this harbor for a private fire and he did not light one.**

**And the far bench was occupied on the said Sunday from about ten in the morning until about four in the afternoon and was not approached on it, and the holder of that writ did not go over to him, and nothing was put on that bench and read as a gift, and neither brother has said why. Mara Venn was in that harbor and going nowhere, of no office, not on the crew, not restored, not forgiven, forty marks first after food, neither reduced nor paid, and she was on that mole at about half past four in the afternoon and said two things and was asked nothing back. Tobias Wren was on the crown of that mole from ten until four, being sixty-one, with eleven pieces of a boat behind him and a boat's hook in his right hand, and he worked the whole of that day at the one joint in the fourth piece with the hook and his thumbs and got it closed about as far as his left hand would close, and the light went at about a quarter to eight and he did not move for it. The notice of the seventeenth of May past is on its wall in a different street for one hundred and four days and has not been touched and has not been taken down. The bellwright of that harbor has not been asked for one hundred and six days, the chest is shut, the eight marks and a day of a boat have not been argued, and no digit of anything he ever said is on a page.**

---

He stood at the other end of that garboard for about twenty minutes on the Sunday morning and said nothing at all.

Isaac Fell was three days into a seam about nine feet long in oak that is under water every tide and out of it twice a day, and he was doing it the way he does everything, which is slowly and in short runs, and the short run was about two feet and then a pause, and the pause was not for anybody.

"I am not going to say anything," Calder said, from the gunwale.

"I know you are not," said Isaac Fell, without looking up. "You have been standing there about four minutes."

"Five."

"Five," said the caulker, and drove the irons in, and pulled them out, and drove them in again, and after a while he said: "You are going to stand there all morning and I am going to let you, and neither of us is going to talk about it, and I want that understood before noon, because if we are still standing here at noon I am going to have to eat and you are going to have to go and do whatever it is you do at noon."

"Understood."

That was the whole conversation and it took eleven minutes and neither man in it afterwards referred to it.

---

The refusal on that door was five days old on the Sunday morning. He did not go up the hill to read it. It was under the first paper and it would say the same four lines it had said on Wednesday and it would say the same second of September at the end of them, and he had read it four times and there was no fifth reading in it.

Peat Marrow was at that wall at about half past two on the same afternoon and did the fourth block, and July is thirty-one days and twenty-five of those lines carried a figure and six were struck across, and the sum of the twenty-five figures was eighty-eight, and the small figure in the clerk of about sixty's hand at the head of that block was twenty-five and was right for the fourth time running.

The clerk of about sixty, who had been in that room twenty-two years and was owed a quarter of a pound and had no book and no power, came out from behind the counter on that afternoon and looked at the sheet on the wall for about as long as it takes to look at a thing a man has walked past four thousand times.

"I would not have thought that one was right," he said. "July."

Peat Marrow looked at him.

"You have not counted it," he said.

"No," said the clerk of about sixty. "I have not counted it and I am not going to and it is not my sheet. But I have ruled it and I have had it in that room since April and I have watched that thing go up and down the wall all summer and I would not have thought that one was right." He put his thumb on the counter. "I would not have thought two of them were right."

"April and May," said Peat Marrow. "I counted the lines of those two twice and the figures of those two once, and I set them against your small figures at the head of each block as I came to it, and your two were right, and I did not rub mine out."

The clerk of about sixty went back behind the counter and said nothing else, and Peat Marrow went out sideways with two figures on the flat of his own left hand, and that was the end of it.

---

At about half past four in the afternoon she was on the flat of the bench stone with her back to the water and the light out behind her the way it always is, and the lens has been out of it since the seventh of May and a barrel of lamp oil is four hundred and fifty pence and has been since the first of June and that figure has not come back down.

"I am going to say one of the two," she said, before Calder had got to the stone. "I told you on Wednesday I would not say it and I am saying one of them and not both, and you are not to work out the other one from it, and if you work out the other one from it I will stop coming up that hill."

"I will not work it out."

"You will work it out. Everybody works it out. I am telling you first so that when you have, you know I gave you the half on purpose and did not keep the other from you to be difficult."

"Go on."

"There is a smell," said Mara Venn. "Tar and a hot iron and something under it that I have not got a name for and that I have smelled about four times in my life and never in a room. And there is a woman counting. Not saying anything. Counting, the way a woman counts a row of stitches, and she is counting in the dark, and I know she is in the dark because the counting has no light in it and I know it is a woman because the count is not even and she is not losing her place and she is going to the end anyway."

Calder Marr stood on the flat of the bench stone and listened to that and did not say anything for long enough that a gull came in over the parapet and went out again.

"I have not got a smell," he said.

"No."

"I have got a window. It is in the wall at about the height of my head when I am sitting down and it has a board across the outside of it and daylight comes under the board and not over it, and I know that because the line of it goes across the whole thing from one side to the other and does not move, and the line does not move on a Thursday in a room where a thing is made. So either the window is not a window or the day is not a day, and I have been in a hundred rooms with windows in them and every one of them had a light in it that moved."

"And what is behind the window?"

"Water," Calder said. "Not sea. Moving, and going the other way, and about four feet down, and there is a man's hand on it going by and he is going fast and his hand is white. And the hand is not frightened. That is the part I cannot put down. Whatever made that thing, one of the two of them was not frightened, and it was the one with the hand, and I do not know whose hand it is and I have not got anything that would tell me."

"Have you got a counting?"

"No."

"Then you have got the hand and I have got the counting and the smell," said Mara Venn, "and neither of us can put them together, and if they are the same afternoon then one of us is carrying the wrong one, and if they are not the same afternoon then there are two afternoons in a thing about nine inches by six that a man can hold up to a white light, and both of those are true and neither of them can be settled on a mole by anybody with a pencil."

"No," Calder said. "It cannot."

"So we are going to stop," she said. "I am not going to say any more of it and you are not going to say any more of it and neither of us is going to try to match them, because the day one of us matches them by guessing is the day one of them is wrong for the rest of our lives and we will not know which."

"That is a decision and not a finding."

"I know what it is," said Mara Venn. "I have been in a room that labels things for eleven weeks and I know what a decision is."

She went down the slip at about ten to five and did not say anything else and Calder Marr stood on that mole for another twenty minutes with the light out behind him and did not go and knock on a door on a hill.

---

At about half past nine that evening Nessa Pike came in with her slate and put it on the bench stone and stood over it.

"Five days," she said. "I am going to price the five days and then I am going to stop, and then you are going to tell me what I have priced and if you do not like it you can say so and I will write it down that you did not like it."

"Go on."

"One day and a half of a man's time on a hill and in a room, being Tuesday and Wednesday, and a night. A day and a half at that wall on three afternoons, being Thursday, Friday and Saturday, and two mornings of not going up that hill on the Tuesday and the Wednesday, which is a day of somebody standing deliberately in a street. One hundred and sixty-eight pence in and three hundred and thirty-six pence out, and the two are not the same figure and neither of them is the wage of four marks. And four days of two people carrying a thing that is not in either of them and that neither of them will say the whole of, which is the largest item on the sheet and the only one I cannot put a rate against." She looked up. "I have put the last one in the second column and the reason against the line is that it is a real thing and I have not got a rate for it and I am not going to invent one at eleven o'clock at night because it would make the sheet tidier."

"That is right."

"That is not the reason," said Nessa. "The reason is that the first column is a place a stranger can check, and there is no stranger in this harbor who can check that, and I would rather be untidy than wrong."

**Nothing came into that yard on the said Sunday and nothing went out of it, and the bag is one mark ten shillings and fourpence and neither moved on that day nor on the Saturday before it, and it is short of that wage by two marks three shillings, and it is not going to be paid and nobody is going to write one word under it saying anybody is going to. The caulker of about twenty-three was on the flat of the bench stone at half past three that morning alone and was not asked for that day by anybody. A man of about fifty-three counted the fourth block of a return at a wall and twenty-five of the thirty-one lines in that block carried a figure and the sum of them was eighty-eight, and a clerk of about sixty who has been in that room twenty-two years came out from behind a counter and said he would not have thought that one was right, and was told he had not counted it and was not going to and that it was not his sheet. A woman of no office said one half of what she is carrying out loud on that mole and was not asked one question back, and the holder of that writ said a window with a board across the outside of it and a hand going by on water four feet down that is not frightened, and the two of them agreed in their own mouths to stop and not to try to match them. Two lights at about a mile off that point have not moved in twenty-eight days, being the twenty-eighth day of them counted from the hour they were first seen on the evening of the first of August past, and nobody in that harbor has hailed them and nobody in that harbor knows whose they are. The far bench was occupied from ten until four and was not approached, and neither brother went near the other at any hour of the said day.**

*First column, entered on Sunday the twenty-ninth day of August next at about half past nine in the evening in that shed: that the said Sunday was not a rota day and not a wage Tuesday and not a wipe; and that a caulker of about twenty-three was on the flat of the bench stone at about half past three in the morning and nobody in that harbor was on it with him, and that he was not asked for the said day by anybody and did not offer it; and that the holder of that writ stood at the other end of a garboard on the second lay for about twenty minutes on the said morning and said that he was not going to say anything, and that the caulker said he knew he was not, and that the caulker said that if they were still standing there at noon he was going to have to eat, and that the whole of that conversation was eleven minutes and that neither man referred to it afterwards; and that the holder did not go up that hill on the said day, and that the refusal on that door was five days old; and that a man of about fifty-three counted the fourth block of that return at about half past two in the afternoon, and that twenty-five of the thirty-one lines in that block carried a figure and six were struck across, and that the sum of the twenty-five figures was eighty-eight, and that the small figure in the clerk of about sixty's hand at the head of that block was twenty-five and was right for the fourth time running; and that a clerk of about sixty came out from behind a counter on that afternoon and said he would not have thought that one was right, and that he was told he had not counted it and was not going to and that it was not his sheet, and that two of them were right and that the man who counted them had checked them against the clerk's own figures and had not rubbed his out; and that the woman of no office said one half of what she is carrying out loud on that mole at about half past four in the afternoon, being a smell of tar and a hot iron and something under it she has no name for, and a woman counting in the dark and not losing her place and going to the end anyway; and that the holder said that he has not got a smell and that he has got a window at about the height of his head with a board across the outside of it and a line of daylight under the board that does not move, and water about four feet down going the other way and a man's hand on it going fast and not frightened; and that the woman of no office said that one of them has the hand and the other has the counting and that neither can put them together, and that they agreed in their own mouths to stop and not to try to match them, because the day one of them matches them by guessing is the day one of them is wrong for the rest of their lives; and that there were two lights at about a mile off that point on the evening of the said day which have not moved in twenty-eight days, counted from the hour they were first seen on the evening of the first of August past, and that nobody in that harbor has hailed them and nobody in that harbor knows whose they are.*
*Second column, not checkable from a mole, entered on the said Sunday at about half past nine in the evening in that shed, and no name is against it: that the reason against the line of what two people are carrying is that it is a real thing and the ship's hand has not got a rate for it and is not going to invent one at eleven o'clock at night because it would make the sheet tidier, and that the first column is a place a stranger can check and there is no stranger in that harbor who can check this; and that the ship's hand priced five days at about half past nine on the said evening, being a day and a half of a man on a hill and in a room, a night, a day and a half at a wall on three afternoons, two mornings of not going up that hill, one hundred and sixty-eight pence in and three hundred and thirty-six pence out, and four days of two people carrying a thing that is not in either of them; and that the two sums are not the same figure and neither of them is the wage of four marks, being six hundred and forty pence; and that two halves of one afternoon that cannot be matched are not a disagreement between two people and are not a mystery anybody has to solve on a mole, and are not going in the first column; and that a caulker who says that if the two of them are still standing there at noon he is going to have to eat is a man telling another man he is going to eat, and is not a scene and is not a friendship and is two men on a gunwale; and that a clerk of about sixty who has ruled that sheet since April and has watched it go up and down a wall all summer and has not counted it is a man who keeps a thing and is not a man who has looked after a thing, and the difference between those two is the whole argument of this yard; and that the standing list entered in full on the fourteenth of August past is not entered here again and stands, and the whole of it stands unpaid and unmoved and no part of it was paid, reduced, advanced or improved on the said Sunday, and that the two people carrying a half each of what came off that thing are not on it and were not asked about it, and the reason against the fourth column entered on the twenty-eighth of August is entered once and is not entered again.*
