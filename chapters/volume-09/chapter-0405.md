# Chapter 405

*Saturday the Twenty-Eighth of August, a Full Market and a Man at the End of the Middle Aisle Who Asked How Many and Was Told That There Was Not a Figure and That If There Was One It Was Not Going Up on Anything, a Woman of About Fifty-Eight Who Did Not Put a Hand on Her Slate, the Third Block of a Return Counted at a Wall on the Same Morning, and the Reason Entered Once and Only Once in the Second Column in That Yard's Own Mouth, Being That the Yard's Time Went Into the First Column and Into a Convoy and Into a Caulker's Day and the Fourth Column Is a Page No Man Can Check and There Was a Man on That Stone at Four in the Morning on the Friday Who Had to Be Paid First*

Saturday the twenty-eighth of August, in that market, at the office on the west basin, and in that shed at about half past eight in the evening.

**The said Saturday was not a wage Tuesday and was not a rota day and was not a wipe, and the wall behind the reeve's bench came down and went up whole at the angle it has been at since the twelfth and nobody put it back. Nothing came into that yard on the said day and nothing went out of it, and there are two hundred and eighty-four pence in the bag, being one mark ten shillings and fourpence, and the figure that bag is short of the wage of four marks is three hundred and fifty-six pence, being two marks three shillings, and it is not going to be paid and nobody is to write one word under it saying anybody is going to. Nothing has been in a tin in that shed since the twenty-eighth day of April past. There is no flour in that market and there is not going to be any this month, and a barrel of lamp oil is four hundred and fifty pence on a board in the front street with February's three hundred and seventy-eight standing above it and both dates on the board and neither figure going anywhere, and physic is fourteen pence and there is none of it in this harbor, and a figure put on a board cannot be taken off again and this yard did not put either of those two figures on either of those two boards.**

**And the far bench was occupied on the said Saturday from about ten in the morning until about four in the afternoon and was not approached on it, and the holder of that writ did not go over to him, and nothing was put on that bench and read as a gift, and neither brother has said why. Mara Venn was in that harbor and going nowhere, of no office, not on the crew, not restored, not forgiven, forty marks first after food, neither reduced nor paid, and she was in that shed from about seven in the evening and did not go up the hill. Tobias Wren was on the crown of that mole from ten until four, being sixty-one, with eleven pieces of a boat behind him and a boat's hook in his right hand, and he was not sent for and not asked and not thanked, and he is not a teacher and not a door and not a sacrifice, and he is not the man on the far bench, and neither brother went near the other at any hour of the said day. The notice of the seventeenth of May past is on its wall in a different street for one hundred and three days and has not been touched and has not been taken down, and there are two older naval notices further along the same wall. The bellwright of that harbor has not been asked for one hundred and five days, the chest is shut, the eight marks and a day of a boat have not been argued, and no digit of anything he ever said is on a page.**

---

The market on a Saturday in that harbor has about nine hundred people in it between ten and one and about forty after two, and the middle aisle is four feet wide and everybody in it is going the other way, and it is the worst place in that town to stand still in and the best place in that town to say something that will be in nine people instead of one.

The man who asked was about forty-four and he was a cooper with a shop on the far street and he had been in three salvaging boats in six years and he had no papers on any of them that anybody ever saw, and he was not a man anybody in that market had ever heard speak about a number before.

They were at the end of the middle aisle with the end stall on their right, and he came up out of the crowd and put his hand on the end of it.

"How many," he said. "Recovered glass. Landed and entered, all of it, everything there has been in that harbor since the blockade came off. How many."

Calder did not move and did not put his hands anywhere.

"I have not got a figure for you."

"You have got a yard."

"I have got a shed with a bench stone in it and four columns on a leaf, and one of those four columns is the one where I put the things that no man standing on a mole can check, and that is what it is for." He looked at the end stall and at the slate on it and back. "And even if I had it, it is not going up on anything, and it is not going in that front street, and it is not going on that wall in that other street where your notice is, and it is not going on the front of that building where that man keeps his board, and if I put a figure of this harbor's glass on a wall in this town then in nine months there will be two figures and one of them will be a lie and I will not know which of them I wrote."

The cooper took his hand off the end of the stall.

"Whose is it, then. The count."

"It is a sheet nailed to a wall in a room off the west basin and it has been kept since the first of April, and it is a return and it decides nothing and it binds nobody, and the man who keeps it is a clerk of about sixty who has been in that room twenty-two years and is owed a quarter of a pound and has no book and no power and I am not going to stand here and use him."

Peat Marrow was four feet off with his hand over the small of his back, looking at a basket of clams, and he had heard every word of it, and he said nothing, and he went on looking at the basket of clams.

The woman of about fifty-eight came out from behind her stall with the slate under her arm and put it flat against her hip and did not put her hand on the chalk, and she said one thing to the cooper and it was not to Calder.

"Buy your clams," she said, "and go home, and do not stand in my aisle."

---

At about half past two on the same morning Peat Marrow was at that wall and did the third block.

June has thirty days and twenty-one of those lines carried a figure and nine of them were struck across, and the sum of the twenty-one was seventy-nine, and the small figure in the clerk's hand at the head of that block was twenty-one and was right again.

On the third block he wrote both figures on the flat of his own left hand under the two he had already put there on the Thursday, so that there were four figures on that palm in two columns, and he looked at them for a second and then he rubbed two of them out with his own thumb because he did not want to be carrying four, and he left the two of Thursday's alone because he had said he would say those on a day.

Then he went out sideways and the room went on being a room with a tide board and a list that is not a schedule and nine feet of wall.

---

The shed at about half past eight in the evening had four people in it, which is more than it has had on a night in a long while, and one of them was a woman of no office, and one of them was the ship's hand, and one of them was the holder of that writ, and one of them was a man of about thirty-one of the third lay with a chart in a bag, who had come up the slip at about eight and had put the bag down by the door and had not opened it, and who is a person of no office and is not a witness and was not turned into one.

The leaf was on the bench stone. The first column had one hundred and thirty-five entries in it and the second had five hundred and forty-four lines in it and the third had nothing in it and nobody had been appointed to it, and the fourth had one hundred and twenty-five lines in it and had not taken one since the twenty-third of August.

"We are not going to write in it," Calder said.

Nessa looked up.

"I am not going to write a line in the fourth column on any of the days of this week or the next three, and there is a reason and I am going to say it out loud in this shed tonight and then Nessa is going to write it in the second column with the hour against it, and it is not going to be said again."

"Go on," said Nessa, and put her pencil down, which is the way she says she is ready.

"Our time went into the first column and into a convoy and into a caulker's day. That is where it went. Every hour I have spent on this since the twenty-fourth of June has gone into a first-column entry that a man on a mole can take with a pencil, and the only other places it went were a boat of about thirteen tons going out on a notice twice in a fortnight on a man's own book, and one hundred and sixty-eight pence on a stone at four o'clock on Friday, and one hundred and sixty-eight pence before that on the seventeenth, and one hundred and sixty-eight pence before that on the tenth. That is the bill, and it is in the first column where it can be checked, and I am not moving a penny of it into a page about what a thing is not."

He put two fingers on the fourth column of the open leaf and did not move the leaf.

"The fourth column is a page no man can check. A stranger could come into this shed with that leaf and a pencil and count one hundred and thirty-five lines in the first and one hundred and twenty-five in the fourth and he would be right about both, and there is nobody alive who can take this page and check a word of it, and neither can you, and I cannot, and a page a man cannot check is a page he has to take on somebody else's word. This yard has spent the better part of a year printing figures on the front of things with the day of the month beside them, so that a stranger can go and take one and add it up again, and that is the whole of what I have learned to do and this is the one page in the leaf where it cannot be done."

Mara Venn, at the back of that shed with her arms folded, said one thing.

"It is the only page in here that is worth anything," she said.

"I know what it is worth."

"Then you know what you are doing by leaving it shut for a while."

The holder of that writ did not answer her and she did not press it.

"Here is the whole of it and I am saying it once," Calder said. "The hundred and twenty-fifth line of that column was the last line on Monday the twenty-third and it is where that ledger stops for now, and the column is not closed and nobody in this yard is going to close it, and there is no hundred and twenty-sixth line in it and there is not going to be one. And the reason there is not one is not that there is nothing to say. It is that on the Friday of this week there was a man on that stone at four in the morning that nobody in this harbor had been on since the fifteenth of May, and he had to be paid first, and he was paid, and that happened before anybody in this yard put a word down about what a thing is not. So the reason goes in the second column, with the hour against it, tonight, and it does not go in the fourth, and it is not going to be said again in this shed."

The man of about thirty-one with the chart in a bag, by the door, with the bag still shut, said: "You are leaving a page blank and telling people why in a book they cannot read."

"Yes."

"Then somebody will tell them the page is blank and not tell them why."

"Yes," said Calder, "and that is a thing that will happen inside a month, and there is no arrangement of any kind I can make that stops it, and I would rather it happened with a reason in the second column than happen by somebody deciding on their own that the page is empty because the man is idle."

---

Nessa wrote it in the second column in her own left hand with the hour against it, and the hour was about ten past nine, and it took her a while because she wrote it in full and she did not shorten it, and when she had finished she read it back to herself once and did not read it aloud.

**THE REASON AGAINST THAT LINE WAS ENTERED ONCE, IN THE SECOND COLUMN OF THAT LEAF, IN THE YARD'S OWN MOUTH, AT ABOUT TEN PAST NINE IN THE EVENING OF THE TWENTY-EIGHTH DAY OF AUGUST, AND IT IS ENTERED ONCE AND IT IS NOT ENTERED AGAIN, AND EVERY LEDGER THAT FOLLOWS IT CARRIES THE FACT THAT IT HAS BEEN ENTERED AND DOES NOT ENTER IT A SECOND TIME.**

---

**Nothing came into that yard on the said Saturday and nothing went out of it, and there are two hundred and eighty-four pence in the bag, being one mark ten shillings and fourpence, and the figure that bag is short of the wage of four marks is three hundred and fifty-six pence, being two marks three shillings, and it is not going to be paid and nobody is going to write one word under it saying anybody is going to pay it. The fourth column of that leaf stands at one hundred and twenty-five and has taken no line since the twenty-third day of August and is not closed and is not struck out and is not a closure, and the reason it has taken no line was entered once, in the second column, in that yard's own mouth, at about ten past nine in the evening of the said day, and is not entered again. A man of about forty-four with a shop on the far street asked in the middle aisle of that market at about half past ten in the morning how many pieces of recovered glass had been landed and entered at that harbor and was told that there was not a figure and that if there was one it was not going on anything, and a man of about fifty-three with a bad back was four feet off looking at a basket of clams and said nothing, and a woman of about fifty-eight told him to buy his clams and go home. The far bench was occupied from ten until four and was not approached, and neither brother went near the other at any hour of the said day.**

*First column, entered on Saturday the twenty-eighth day of August next at about half past nine in the evening in that shed: that the said Saturday was not a rota day and not a wage Tuesday and not a wipe; and that a man of about forty-four with a shop on the far street and three boats behind him in six years asked in the middle aisle of that market at about half past ten in the morning how many pieces of recovered glass had been landed and entered at that harbor, and that the holder said he had not got a figure for him, and that the holder said that the sheet it would come off is nailed to a wall in a room off the west basin, has been kept since the first of April, is a return and decides nothing and binds nobody, and is kept by a clerk of about sixty who has been in that room twenty-two years and is owed a quarter of a pound and has no book and no power; and that the holder said that if he had the figure it was not going up on anything and not going in that front street and not going on the wall in that other street and not going on the front of the building where that man keeps his board, and that a man of about fifty-three with a bad back was four feet off looking at a basket of clams and said nothing; and that a woman of about fifty-eight told him to buy his clams and go home and not to stand in her aisle; and that a man of about fifty-three counted the third block of that return on the same morning at about half past two in the afternoon, and that twenty-one of the thirty days of that month carried a figure and nine were struck across, and that the sum of the twenty-one figures was seventy-nine, and that the small figure in the clerk of about sixty's hand at the head of that block was twenty-one and was right; and that the said man wrote two of his figures on the flat of his own left hand under the two of the Thursday's and rubbed out the two he had just written with his own thumb and left the two of the Thursday's alone because he had said he would say those on a day; and that the fourth column of that leaf has taken no line since the twenty-third day of August and is not closed and has not been struck out, and that the reason it has taken no line was said out loud in that shed on the said evening in the holder's own mouth and was entered in the second column in the ship's own left hand with the hour against it at about ten past nine, and that the reason is that that yard's time went into the first column and into a convoy and into a caulker's day, and that the fourth column is a page no man can check, and that there was a man on the flat of the bench stone at four in the morning on the Friday of that week who had to be paid first and was paid before anybody in that yard put a word down about what a thing is not; and that the said reason is entered once and is not entered again.*
*Second column, not checkable from a mole, entered on the said Saturday at about half past nine in the evening in that shed, and no name is against it: that the reason in the last line of the first column is itself a second-column fact, being a yard's own reason about its own page, and that it stands here and is not repeated; and that a page a man cannot check is a page he has to take on somebody else's word, and that the yard is not going to ask anybody in that harbor to do that with an empty page, and that a man of about thirty-one of the third lay with a chart in a bag said in that shed that somebody would say the page was blank and not say why, and that the holder said yes and that there is no arrangement that stops it and that the reason is in the second column and not in the fourth; and that a figure a stranger can take off a leaf with a pencil is one hundred and thirty-five in the first column and one hundred and twenty-five in the fourth, and that neither of those is a count of recovered glass and neither of them is going to be; and that three blocks of a return counted at a wall on three days are three blocks of a return counted at a wall on three days and are not yet a figure, and the reason against the line is that a man on a mole cannot check what a man counted at a wall in a room he was not in; and that a man of about forty-four asking a question in the middle aisle of a market on a Saturday is a man asking a question in a market, and that there is no name against him in this yard's leaf and there is no figure against him anywhere, and that he has a shop on the far street and three boats behind him in six years and nothing else that has been asked of him this month; and that a woman of about fifty-eight who told him to buy his clams and go home did it in her own market and not for this yard; and that the standing list entered in full on the fourteenth day of August is not entered here again and stands, and the whole of it stands unpaid and unmoved and no part of it was paid, reduced, advanced or improved on the said day.*
