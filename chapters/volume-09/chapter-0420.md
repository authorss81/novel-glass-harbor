# Chapter 420

*Sunday the Twelfth Day of September, a Barrow with Two Handles at the Top of Two Flights of Stairs at Half Past Twelve and a Woman of About Sixty-One of the Third Lay Coming Up Them at One o'Clock With a Box on It and Nobody Thanking Her at the Top of the Stair or at the Bottom of It, About Twenty People in a Room of Whom Eleven Cannot Read, a Man of About Forty-Six Reading Twenty-Two Head-Notes Out Loud and Writing His Own Name and the Hour Under Each, a Panel of About Eleven Inches by Eight Put on a Long Table by the Woman Who Made It and Looked at by About Twenty People, a Room That Emptied, a Box Carried Down Two Flights of Stairs, a Bound Volume Shut and Squared to the Edge of a Table, and a Man of About Thirty-One With a Stub of Pencil Behind His Ear Who Counted Thirty-One Heads on the Back of His Own Left Hand and Found That One of Them Carries Three Lines Where the Head of That Volume Says Four and Wrote It on the Inside of the Back Cover and Was Not Asked by Anybody Who Put It There*

Sunday the twelfth day of September, in that room, in Fish Street, in that shed, and on that mole head.

**The said Sunday was not a wage Tuesday and was not a rota day and was not a wipe, and the wall behind the reeve's bench came down and went up whole at the angle it has been at since the twelfth and nobody put it back. Nothing came into that yard on the said day and nothing went out of it, and there are four hundred and eight pence in the bag, being two marks seven shillings and fourpence, and the figure that bag is short of the wage of four marks is two hundred and thirty-two pence, being one mark six shillings, and it is not going to be paid and nobody is to write one word under it saying anybody is going to. The whole of what came into that yard in the ten days that ended on the said Sunday is five hundred and four pence on three days, being the fourth, the seventh and the tenth, at the figure in chalk on a slate at an end stall, and the whole of what went out of it is three hundred and eighty pence on three days, being one hundred and sixty-eight pence in coin on the flat of the bench stone at about four in the afternoon on the sixth and on the tenth to a caulker of about twenty-three at that same figure, and forty-four pence, being three shillings and eightpence, on the eighth at about half past ten in the morning for a loaf and a half, a candle and a pot, and one hundred and sixty plus five hundred and four minus three hundred and eighty is four hundred and eight, and the two sums are not the same figure and neither of them is the wage of four marks, being six hundred and forty pence.**

**And the far bench was occupied on the said Sunday from about ten in the morning until about four in the afternoon and was not approached on it, and the holder of that writ did not go over to him, and nothing was put on that bench and read as a gift, and neither brother has said why. Mara Venn was in that harbor and going nowhere, of no office, not on the crew, not restored, not forgiven, forty marks first after food, neither reduced nor paid, and she was on that slip at about ten past six in the evening and did not go up to Fish Street at any hour of the said day, and a man of about twenty-three was on the flat of the bench stone at about half past three that morning because he cannot sleep, and nobody in that harbor was on that stone with him. Tobias Wren was on the crown of that mole from ten until four, being sixty-one, with eleven pieces of a boat behind him and a boat's hook in his right hand, and on the said day he was sat on the eleventh piece with the hook across his knees and the light behind him, and at about half past three in the afternoon he put the hook down, and stayed where he was until four. The notice of the seventeenth of May past is on its wall in a different street for one hundred and eighteen days and has not been touched. The bellwright of that harbor has not been asked for one hundred and twenty days, the chest is shut, and the eight marks and a day of a boat have not been argued The three nots standing against the eighth of June past are a tenth week in which nobody in that crew has said one word about them, and the hand of a boy of twenty-two is still at the head of the third column with a name beside it that has not been said out loud since the thirteenth of May past, and that name is not on any page of this harbor.. The two objects of nobody's on that crown are four hundred and fifty-eight days old and neither has moved. The bench under that pump is bare for the two hundred and twenty-fourth night. Two lights at about a mile off that point have not moved in forty-two days, counted from the hour they were first seen on the evening of the first of August past. A man of about fifty-one has said nothing to anybody about a page of six names for sixty days. A nine-line sheet with a seal on it has been in that front street for twenty-nine days and has been answered by nobody. The paper at the top left of that door in Fish Street is twelve days old and has been answered by nobody. There is no flour in that market and none is coming this month, and there has been no barrel of lamp oil in that harbor since the twenty-ninth of August, and the mole-head light is not lit and its lens has been out since the seventh of May past.**

The barrow was at the top of the two flights at half past twelve and Tom Barlas took it up himself after Hobb Lee had carried the first half of the way and complained about the second half.

She came up at one o'clock. The box is about three foot long and a foot wide and eight inches deep and it has a rope round it, and it went up the first flight on a woman's back with a bad knee and a stick in one hand and on the second flight it went up on the shoulders of a woman of about sixty-one with a bad knee, and it took her about an hour and a half, and Hobb Lee stood at the top of the stairs with the two handles of the barrow and did not put a hand on the box, and did not say one word.

"There is the table," he said. "Put it where you want it."

There is no one else in that room. Hobb Lee had been in it since about eleven and Ned Vaile had been in it since about half past twelve, and Vaile stood at the end of the table with his hands behind his back and then went downstairs at about five to one and came up again at about a quarter to six and stood at the foot of the stairs.

Nell Carrow cut the rope with a short knife she had in her pocket and took the lid off, and lifted the thing out with both hands, and set it down on the long table, and stood looking at it.

It is about eleven inches by eight. It has a frame round it, a plain one, deal, mitred, with a nail in each corner, and the glass is set into the frame about a quarter of an inch proud of the wood, and the glass has a face in it and the face is about four inches high and the face is turned away from the room.

That is a room. It is a room with a door in it, and the door is shut, and there is a figure a little way inside the door with its back to whoever is looking at it, and the figure's hand is up at the height of its own chest with the fingers not shut, and there is light coming in from the left in a way that glass does when a thing is put into it under a weight and held for a long time, and the light is coming in on the side of the room that the door is not on.

"I am not going to say anything about it," said Nell Carrow. "I said on Thursday I would not be thanked and I am not being thanked and I am not going to say what it is, and if anybody in this room says a kind thing to me I will walk out, and I have told the man who reads that book that I would and he has told nobody."

She put her hand flat on the frame and looked at it for about a minute and a half, and then she stepped back from the table so that the people coming up could see it, and she went and stood at the back of that room against the wall, near the door, with her stick in front of her, and she did not look at it again for the rest of that afternoon.

---

The bowl went on the step at two o'clock. About twenty people went up and four came down, and eleven of the twenty could not read.

Hobb Lee stood at the end of the long table with the volume open and a stub of pencil in his right hand and read twenty-two head-notes, and under each one he wrote his own name and the hour, and the twenty-second was at about twenty past four in the afternoon.

The thirty-first was the oil.

"Thirty-first. A figure on a board at an end stall. Where it came from: the first of June. Who stands where it is: the end stall, and the figure is on a board and February's figure stands above it with both dates. What it does not show: it does not show that a figure read out in a room is a different kind of figure from a figure on a board, and it does not show that she was asked on the fifth of September and said no, and that no was given and written at the same size as every other line on this page."

*Twenty-six minutes past three in the afternoon. Hobb Lee.*

"There," said the woman of about fifty-eight from the middle of the room, "that is the last one and I have heard every other one, and it is a strange thing to hear your own no read out in a room, and I would not have thought of it, and I would not have had it any other way, and I am not going to say a kind thing about it now or ever."

Hobb Lee shut the volume and squared it to the edge of the table, and the pencil went behind his ear, and that is how it stood when the room emptied.

---

It emptied at about ten to five.

There was no sound in it that anybody in that street heard from the pavement. There was a set of feet going down two flights, quickly, and then a door, and then a gap of about four minutes, and then feet again, and then a woman of about thirty-four saying something to nobody, and then nothing.

Nell Carrow put the lid on the box and cut the rope round it and put the barrow's two handles up against the frame and started down, and the first flight she went down backwards with the barrow's wheel against the step above her, and Hobb Lee took the other end of it without being asked and without saying anything and without being thanked for it, and he said nothing at the top of that stair and nothing at the bottom of it, and he did not say one word to her on the step, and she went away down that slip with the barrow and did not look back, and nobody in that room or on that step said one kind word to her, and nobody in that harbor has said one kind word to her since.

Calder Marr had been outside in Fish Street since a quarter past four, standing in the doorway of the joiner's shop across the road, and he had not gone in, and he did not go in.

---

A man of about forty-one came down the slip at about ten past five and found Calder Marr at the bench stone in that shed with the slate and no lamp lit, because the lamp on that bench stone has not been lit since the fifth of May and the lens is out of the mole-head light and a barrel of lamp oil is four hundred and fifty pence with February's three hundred and seventy-eight above it on a board in the front street, and there has been no oil in that harbor since the twenty-ninth of August, and both figures are on that board and neither of them is going anywhere.

He had been in that room. He had gone up those stairs at about half past two on the afternoon with about nineteen other people and had come down them at about ten to five and he had come down them in the middle of the crowd and he had not gone out of the door at the front.

"I want to say a thing and I have not got it right and I am going to say it wrong," he said. "I was in that room from about half past two until about five. I was not looking at the thing on the table. I was looking at the door."

"What about the door."

"When I came out of that room I came down two flights of stairs and I got to the bottom of them and I stood in that passage and I could not tell you which door I had come out of." He put his hand flat on the bench stone. "I know the passage. There is a door at the top and a door at the bottom and the one at the top is the joiner's back door and the one at the bottom goes out under the stairs. And I stood at the bottom of those stairs and I could not tell you in what order I had come down them, and I had to go back up and stand in the doorway at the top with my hand on the frame to find out that it was the top one. I have been in that passage about four hundred times in my life. I have carried a barrow of shavings down it in the dark. And I had to go up and put my hand on the frame to find out which door I had come out of, and I want you to understand that I am not a fanciful man and I am not a man who has been in a room and come out funny about a thing, and that it was not the thing on the table, I did not look at the thing on the table, I was looking at the door the whole time."

"You have it now."

"I have got which door," said the man of about forty-one. "I have not got anything else. I have had a cold in my teeth since about three o'clock this afternoon and my hands have not been right since about three o'clock this afternoon, and there is a taste of iron in my mouth like a nail, and it is not a fever and I have not got a headache and I could go out and lift a barrow of shavings. My boy is going to be seven in November and I know the number of his fingers. And I am telling you that I have got a thing I did not have at two o'clock this afternoon and I have not got any idea what it is, and I am not going to be one of eleven men in this harbor who goes round saying that, because I have got a wife and she is going to ask me tonight why I am standing at that bench stone at ten past five instead of carrying shavings, and I am going to have to tell her something, and I have not thought of the something yet."

Calder Marr had nothing to give him. He did not have a smell and he did not have a counting and he did not have a window with a board across the outside of it, and he had not been in that room, and he had gone and stood in a street on purpose.

"Tell her the truth," he said. "Tell her you were in a room over a joiner's shop and it did something to you that you cannot name, and tell her that you went to a man who has not got a better answer than you have, and tell her that both of those are true and neither of them is the end of it."

"You are not going to tell me what is on the table."

"No," said Calder Marr. "There is a woman of about sixty-one in that room who made that thing when she was about nineteen and nobody in that room asked her one word about it and nobody is going to ask her one word about it now, and I am not going to stand in a street and tell a man of forty-one what a woman of sixty-one has not offered to say about a thing she made with her own hands. If you want to know what is on that table, go and ask her, and she will tell you no, and you will have to be satisfied with that, and so will I."

---

Arthur Sennett came up that stair at about ten past five in the afternoon, on his way out, and Hobb Lee was holding the volume under his arm and Tom Barlas was at the bottom of that stair with the barrow.

"Mr Lee. I am going to ask you for that book and I am going to be five minutes and I am not going to talk to anybody while I do it."

"Take it."

He took it and he sat down on the bench at the joiner's end, at the clean end, and he turned to the back of it, and he put his left hand flat on his left knee and he wrote on the back of it with a stub of pencil he took from behind his ear.

He counted for about four minutes. Then he said:

"Thirty-one heads. Thirty of them have four lines under the head. One has three. And the head of it at the front says four lines under every head, and it says it in five lines, and the second of those five says the refusals are on it too and are not smaller than the rest." He did not look up. "It is the twenty-second. It is of a panel of worked glass about eleven inches by eight out of a hull landed at the third lay on the fourth day of June. The third line ends in a dash and there is nothing after the dash. That is the whole of what I have found and I have found it with a pencil on my own hand and I did not know there was a dash in it before about four minutes ago."

Ned Vaile had come back up at the sound of the door and was standing at the foot of the stairs.

"You did not know there was a dash in it," said Hobb Lee.

"No."

"I have not read the twenty-second since the eighteenth of June."

"You have not read it," said Arthur Sennett, "but you have got the pencil behind your ear and you have been in this room every Sunday since the twentieth of April, and a man who has been in this room every Sunday since the twentieth of April has read that page. That is not me saying you did it. I am not going to say that and I am not going to be asked, and if anybody asks me who left a dash at the end of the third line of the twenty-second head of this book I will say that I was not in that room on the eighteenth of June, and that is the true answer and it is the whole of the answer I have got."

He turned to the back of that volume, to the inside of the back cover, which is blank, and he wrote on it in pencil, in a small hand, four lines.

*Twelfth day of September. Thirty-one heads. Thirty with four lines under the head and one with three. The head at the front says four. It is the twenty-second and its third line ends in a dash. Found with a pencil on my own hand. I was not in the room on the eighteenth of June and I am not going to say who was.*

*— A. Sennett, thirty-one.*

He gave the volume back and Hobb Lee shut it and put it under his arm and the door went down at six o'clock with a bowl on the top stone of the step.

Calder Marr was in the passage when they came out and he asked Ned Vaile, in the passage, in about six words, whether it was true, and Ned Vaile said that it was true and that he had known there was a dash in it since the eighteenth of June and had not come back to it, and Calder Marr said that he had read that head on Friday the third of September and had put his thumb on the third line and had left the volume shut on that table, and had not counted anything, and had told the man of about thirty-four in that shed on the Thursday why a man with a stake is not a count, and then neither of them said one word about it, and they went down the hill together and neither of them said one word about it.

Nessa Pike read the leaf back at about ten past five in the evening, on the flat of the bench stone, in the open, in her own left hand, all four columns including the empty one, and the holder of that writ stood on the other side of it and did not touch it.

"The first column of that leaf has one hundred and fifty-two entries in it at this hour and one hundred and fifty-three at the close of the day," she said. "There were one hundred and forty-three of them at the close of Thursday the second day of September past and ten have gone in since, one against each of the ten days from Friday the third to this day, and not one day in two of them, and this evening's own is the tenth of the ten and makes one hundred and fifty-three at the close. A day of the first column carries one entry against it whatever else that day printed, so that a stranger with a leaf and a pencil can count it."

"The second column has six hundred and thirty-seven lines in it at this hour and six hundred and thirty-nine at the close of the day," she said. "Five hundred and eighty-nine of them were in it at the close of the second, and five have gone in on each of the ten days since, three before the hour of that day's reading and two after it, and three of this day's went in before this reading and two have gone in in this afternoon after it, which is six hundred and thirty-seven at this hour and six hundred and thirty-nine at the close. **That figure is that book of that yard's own and it is not a count of anything anybody can check from a mole. The first column's one hundred and fifty-two and the fourth column's one hundred and twenty-five, at this hour, are a count of entries and a count of lines, and both of them can be taken with a pencil. Those are the two definitions and they are printed here so that a later writer with a pencil knows which of them they are counting and at which of the two moments.**"

"The third column is empty and nobody has been appointed to it, and the hand of a boy of twenty-two is at the head of it with a name beside it that is not that boy's and is not the holder's, and that name was said out loud once in a boat on the thirteenth day of May past and has not been said since and is not going to be said again in this harbor. The four lines above his named line are four, are nameless, are not struck out, and are in about sixty boats on that coast. He was not on the stone on any of these ten days and was put on nothing, and nobody asked him one word about the three nots standing against the eighth of June past, and there is now a tenth week in which nobody in that crew has said one word about them, and no day of any week before the first of July was entered against that line and none is going to be."

"And the fourth column has one hundred and twenty-five lines in it at this hour and one hundred and twenty-five at the close," she said, "and it has taken no line since the twenty-third day of August. It has taken no line on any of these ten days and there is no hundred and twenty-sixth line in it. That is not a closure and it is not a strike-out and nobody has closed that column and nobody in this yard is going to. The reason it has taken no line was entered in the second column at about ten past nine in the evening of the twenty-eighth of August, in that yard's own mouth, with the hour against it, and it is entered once and it is not entered again, and the reason it is in the second column and not in the fourth is that a man on a mole cannot check the second column and a man on a mole can check the fourth."

She shut it and put it under her own coat and neither of them said one word about the figure it gave.

**Nothing came into that yard in the ten days that ended on the said Sunday and nothing went out of it beyond three days at a berth, two caulker's days and a loaf and a half and a candle and a pot, and the bag is two marks seven shillings and fourpence against a wage of six hundred and forty pence, being four marks, and the figure it is short of that wage is two hundred and thirty-two pence, being one mark six shillings, and it is never zero and nobody is going to write one word under it saying anybody is going to pay it. A room over a joiner's shop at the top of Fish Street was let out on the said Sunday for the second time and about twenty people went up its stairs and four came down, and eleven of the twenty could not read, and a man of about forty-six read twenty-two head-notes out loud off the long table and wrote his own name and the hour under each of them in his own hand, and the last of the twenty-two was at about four minutes past four in the afternoon and was a refusal of a woman of about fifty-eight, and the first of them was at about two minutes past two in the afternoon. *First column, entered on Sunday the twelfth day of September next at about half past nine in the evening in that shed: that the said Sunday was the second day on which a room over a joiner's shop at the top of Fish Street was let out with a man at the end of the long table reading, and that about twenty people went up those stairs and four came down and eleven of the twenty could not read; *Second column, not checkable from a mole, entered on the said Sunday at about half past nine in the evening in that shed, and no name is against it: that what came off a panel made by a woman of about nineteen in a room with a prepared frame and a man with a paper is not a fever and is not a complaint and is not glass and is not an illness, and that it was told out of the mouth of a man of about forty-one on the evening of the said day on a bench stone in this shed and not out of a paper, and that it is a cold in the teeth and a taste of iron and a man's own body not knowing which of two doors he came out of, and that the man who said it is not one of eleven men and is not going round the harbor saying so, and that nobody was charged with anything on the said day and nobody was made whole; and that the standing list entered in full on the fourteenth of August past is not entered in full on the said Sunday and stands, and no part of it was paid, reduced, advanced or improved on any of the ten days, and that there is still no flour in that market and no oil in that harbor and no light in that mole head.*
