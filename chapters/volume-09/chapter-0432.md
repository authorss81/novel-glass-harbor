# Chapter 432

*Friday the Twenty-Fourth Day of September, a Bell Down to a Boat of About Nineteen Tons Lying in About Nine Foot on a Bank Four Hundred Yards Down a Foul at a Quarter to Four in the Morning and Up Again at About Twenty Past Five, Two Hours and Thirty-Five Minutes of Air and Pumping With a Boy of Fifteen on the Pump and the Owner and a Man Hauling on the Fall, a Shore of Two Baulks of Oak Faired to a Keel in the Dark With a Light and a Hand and Made Wrong Once and Made Again, One Hundred and Sixty-Eight Pence Going Out of That Yard at About Nine in the Morning for a Pump Leather and a Coil of Marline, and a Man of About Thirty-Four of the Second Lay Who Keeps the Whole Fouling Ladder in His Own Book Saying Out Loud in a Shed How a Heel Is Fitted Where the Reeds Move the Ground, and a Packet Going Down to a Coast Where the First Public Route Codes Are Kept*

Friday the twenty-fourth day of September, on a bank about four hundred yards down that foul, in that shed, and on that mole head.

**The said Friday was not a rota day and was not a wage Tuesday and was not a wipe, and no charge was placed on that cistern on it. One hundred and sixty-eight pence went out of that yard at about nine in the morning for a pump leather and a coil of marline and canvas and nails, and there are three hundred and sixty-four pence in the bag at the close of the said day, being two marks three shillings and eightpence, and the figure that bag is short of the wage of four marks is two hundred and seventy-six pence, being one mark nine shillings and eightpence, and it is not going to be paid and nobody is to write one word under it saying anybody is going to.**

**And the far bench was occupied from about ten in the morning until about four in the afternoon and was not approached, and the holder of that writ did not go over to him, and nothing was put on that bench and read as a gift, and neither brother has said why. Mara Venn was in that harbor and going nowhere, of no office, not on the crew, not restored, not forgiven, forty marks first after food, neither reduced nor paid, and she was not on that bank. Tobias Wren was on the crown of that mole from ten until four, being sixty-one, with a boat's hook in his right hand, and on the said day he sat on the fourth piece with his back to the water for the whole of the four hours and got up at four and went home, which he has done twice in fifty days. The notice of the seventeenth of May past is on its wall in a different street for one hundred and thirty days and has not been touched. The bellwright of that harbor has not been asked for one hundred and thirty-two days, the chest is shut, and the eight marks and a day of a boat have not been argued, and no digit of anything he ever said is on a page. The three nots standing against the eighth of June past are an eleventh week in which nobody in that crew has said one word about them, and the hand of a boy of twenty-two is still at the head of the third column with a name beside it that has not been said out loud since the thirteenth of May past, and that name is not on any page of this harbor. The two objects of nobody's on that crown are four hundred and seventy days old. The bench under that pump is bare for the two hundred and thirty-sixth night. Two lights at about a mile off that point have not moved in fifty-four days. A man of about fifty-one has said nothing to anybody about a page of six names for seventy-two days. The sheet of six lines at the back of that end stall is on that wall for the ninth day and the paper of four lines in Fish Street is on that door for the twenty-fourth day and the nine-line sheet with a seal on it is in that front street for forty-one days.**

---

The ebb at the bottom of that bank is at about a quarter to four in the morning and the top of it is about twenty past five, and it is a hundred and five minutes, and the bank comes up under a hull about a foot and a half on the flood.

Nine foot of water over her keel at the top of the water. Ten foot and a half at the bottom of it. That is the whole of it.

"You have got a hundred and five minutes in twenty-four hours," said Nessa Pike, on the stones on the Friday before, "and about two foot of water to work in, and the second of those two is the one you can do nothing about."

"I have got a hundred and five minutes tonight and about a hundred and twenty tomorrow and about ninety on Sunday," said the holder of that writ, "and I have got a boat's hook, a lamp and two lungs, and I am going to use them."

---

They went down at about a quarter to four in a punt with the bell in it and three men in the punt and the boy of fifteen on the pump.

The two men on the rope were the owner's own two. Jos Lund took the fall. A man of about twenty-six off the second lay with a hod of lime who had come down the slip on the word stood and held the light over the side of the boat while the ladder went down, and he held it for about two hours and forty minutes without being asked to move it and without being paid for either hour.

Calder Marr went down to about eleven foot on a shot line off the boat's quarter, because she was lying with her port bilge up and there was no ladder on that side that would go down in that water.

The bottom of her was not eleven foot. The bottom of her was about nine foot, and there was a foot and a half of that mud bank between him and the plank.

He put a lamp on his chest and laid two baulks of oak under her keel, one on the port side of the keel and one under the garboard strake where it was open, and wedged them up hard with a marlinspike and a piece of three-inch stuff, and it took him about an hour and ten minutes to get them to bear, and when they bore the boat came down about two inches and stopped dead, and the garboard did not open any further, and there was a noise like a hatch shutting that everybody in that punt heard.

Then he went along the garboard for about thirty feet with his hands on it, feeling for soft plank.

Nine feet aft of where he had shore her the plank was soft. It has been soft since June and it is soft now, and he put his thumb through the veneer of it about the size of a shilling and the boat's hand knows it and nobody has had a caulker in her since June.

He came up at about twenty past five with his hands black to the wrist and his right knee not right, and he said one thing on the stones and it was this.

"There is a second hole in her that I did not make and did not know about, and it is nine feet aft of the first one and it is soft plank, and it is not open yet and it is going to open, and a shore does not stop soft plank and a patch does not stop soft plank and the only thing that stops soft plank is a new piece of plank faired to what is left of the old."

---

The pump leather went at about half past four in the morning and the boy of fifteen put his hand into it up to the wrist to find the split and came up holding it, and the fresh water that came in through the pump for the last fifty minutes went over the side and nobody said anything about it and it is on that bank's own list and not on anybody else's.

Nessa Pike had the figure of it on her slate by about nine in the morning, being one mark and eightpence for a pump leather and a coil of marline and canvas and nails out of that yard's own figures, and a man of about fifty with two papers on a table in an office off the west basin had nothing to do with it and the owner of no boat in that harbor had anything to do with it.

---

At about half past ten in the morning Ollum Trevask, of about thirty-four, of the second lay, who keeps the whole fouling ladder in that harbor in his own book and has never shown it to anybody, and who is not the other man of about thirty-four that came off the water off a pressure house with a paper he did not want to carry, and who is not going to be asked about that either, came into that shed with the book under his arm and put it on the bench and did not open it.

"You are going to fit a new piece to her keel," he said. "Nine feet aft, on soft plank, with a shore under her and a foot and a half of water on the flood. And you think you are going to take a pattern off her to fair it to."

"How would I not."

"Because you cannot get a light on a keel in nine foot of water with a shore in her and a bell over her, and a man with his hands on a garboard in the dark is not measuring, he is guessing, and he will guess twice and get a different answer the second time."

He put two fingers on the bench and drew nothing and took them away again.

"They do not do it that way in Lowfen," he said. "In Lowfen a man takes the mould off a piece of the old keel with the boat still floating, in about two foot of water, with two men on a pole and a third with the battens, and he lets it down onto the marks as she comes off, and the ground under a Lowfen hull is not the ground there next year, because the reeds put it out and pull it back, and a man who measured it and wrote it down in a book would be writing down last year's ground. So they keep the mould in a head and not in a book, and they cut the battens to a pitch and they do not write the pitch down either, because a pitch is for the man who set it."

"You have never been to Lowfen in your life."

"I have never been to Lowfen in my life," he said, "and that is how I know, and nobody in this shed asked me where I got it, and I am not going to say."

Nessa Pike had the slate against her knee and wrote nothing on it while he said it.

"You are not on any sheet," she said, when he had the book under his arm again. "That was not a claim and it is not going on anything, and if anybody puts it on a sheet I will not read it out at a table."

"I am not after a sheet," said Ollum Trevask.

---

The packet went down the coast road at about four in the afternoon in a mail bag with a stone on it.

It was not a request for a person by name and it did not name a route and it did not say what it was for in so many words. It said that a boat of about nineteen tons was lying in nine foot on a bank four hundred yards down a foul off the south end of Morrow Quay with her garboard open forward and soft plank nine feet aft of it, and that a man here wanted a mould taken off a keel to a pitch and could not take it himself in nine foot of water in the dark, and that this yard would pay a day at the figure in chalk on a slate at an end stall and would not pay a penny of any other kind, and that the figure it was offering was not the figure anybody ought to take and that whoever came would be told so at the hour.

Mina Kett, of about thirty-four, of Tidewatch on the western reef line, came down off that coast on the Sunday with her own tools in a box and a paper of lead cuts in the lid of it.

---

There is no flour in that market. There has been no barrel of lamp oil in that harbor since the twenty-ninth day of August and a figure of four hundred and fifty pence is on a board at an end stall with February's three hundred and seventy-eight pence standing visible above it and both dates on that board, and that figure was not taken down and cannot be taken off. The sheet of six lines is on the wall at the back of that stall for the ninth day on the outside of that figure. The nine-line sheet with a seal on it is in the front street for forty-one days and names no person and no harbor and puts no figure on it. The paper of four lines in Fish Street is on that door for the twenty-fourth day. The mole-head light is out. The north berth is out. The fire party's hose is dead until the spring. The tide gang is four men short. The hard at Ashtide is unanswered since the ninth of November past. Forty-eight weeks stand fallen due with no envelope against any of them and the forty-ninth falls on the Tuesday after this day.

**A boat of about nineteen tons named the *Alewife* was lying in about nine foot of water on a bank about four hundred yards down the foul off the south end of that mole with her port bilge up, and the holder of that writ went down to her in a bell at a quarter to four in the morning on the said Friday and came up at about twenty past five, being about two hours and thirty-five minutes at the bottom and on the pump, with two men of the crew on the rope, a boy of fifteen on the pump, the owner on the fall and a man of about twenty-six holding a light over the boat's side for about two hours and forty minutes without being asked to move it; and he shored her with two baulks of oak under her keel and under her garboard strake and got them to bear, and the boat came down about two inches and stopped dead and the garboard did not open any further, and he went thirty feet along that garboard with his hands on it in the dark and found nine feet of soft plank aft of where he had shore her, and put his thumb through the veneer of it about the size of a shilling, and came up and said that a shore does not stop soft plank and a patch does not stop soft plank and that the only thing that stops soft plank is a new piece faired to what is left of the old, and that he cannot get a light on that keel in nine foot of water in the dark and would be guessing. The pump leather went at about half past four in the morning and about fifty minutes of fresh water came over the side. A man of about thirty-four of the second lay who keeps the whole fouling ladder in that harbor in his own book came into that shed at about half past ten, did not open the book, and said out loud that they do not do it that way in Lowfen, where a man takes the mould off a piece of the old keel with the boat still floating in about two foot of water with two men on a pole and a third with the battens and lets it down onto the marks as she comes off, and that the ground under a Lowfen hull is not the ground there next year because the reeds put it out and pull it back, and that a mould there is kept in a head and not in a book and a pitch is for the man who set it; and he said that he has never been to Lowfen in his life and that nobody in that shed asked him where he got it. The woman who prices things told him he was not on any sheet and that it was not a claim. A packet went down that coast at about four in the afternoon asking for a person who can take a mould off a keel to a pitch and offering one day at the figure in chalk on a slate at an end stall and telling whoever came, in the packet, that the figure being offered was not the figure anybody ought to take. One hundred and sixty-eight pence went out of that yard at about nine in the morning and three hundred and sixty-four pence is in it.**

*First column, entered on Friday the twenty-fourth day of September next at about half past nine in the evening in that shed: that a boat of about nineteen tons named the Alewife was lying on a bank about four hundred yards down the foul off the south end of that mole in about nine foot of water with her port bilge up, her mast lying across her starboard side and her garboard open about two foot forward of the keel; and that the ebb at the bottom of that bank was at about a quarter to four in the morning and the top of it at about twenty past five, being about a hundred and five minutes in twenty-four hours, and that the bank comes up under a hull about a foot and a half on the flood; and that the holder of that writ went down to her in a bell off her starboard quarter at about a quarter to four in the morning on the said Friday and was at the bottom of her and on the pump for about two hours and thirty-five minutes and came up at about twenty past five; and that two men of that crew were on the rope and a boy of fifteen was on the pump and the owner was on the fall and a man of about twenty-six off the second lay stood and held a light over the side of that boat for about two hours and forty minutes; and that he shored her with two baulks of oak under her keel and under her garboard strake and wedged them up, and that the boat came down about two inches and stopped dead when they bore and the garboard did not open any further; and that he went about thirty feet along that garboard with his hands on it and found about nine feet of soft plank nine feet aft of where he had shore her, and put his thumb through the veneer of it about the size of a shilling, and said out loud that a shore does not stop soft plank and that a patch does not stop soft plank; and that the pump leather went at about half past four in the morning and about fifty minutes of fresh water came in and went over the side; and that one hundred and sixty-eight pence went out of that yard at about nine in the morning for a pump leather and a coil of marline and canvas and nails, and that five hundred and thirty-two minus one hundred and sixty-eight is three hundred and sixty-four pence at the close of the said day, being two marks three shillings and eightpence, being short of the wage of four marks by two hundred and seventy-six pence, being one mark nine shillings and eightpence; and that no charge was placed on that cistern on the said day and the said day was not a wipe and was not a wage Tuesday; and that a packet went down that coast road at about four in the afternoon offering one day at the figure in chalk on a slate at an end stall and telling whoever came in that packet that the figure being offered was not the figure anybody ought to take.*

*Second column, not checkable from a mole, entered on the said Friday at about half past nine in the evening in that shed, and no name is against it: that the reason the holder of that writ would not fair a new piece to that keel by eye in nine foot of water in the dark is in his own mouth and is that he cannot get a light on a keel with a shore in her and a bell over her, and that a man with his hands on a garboard in the dark is guessing and will guess twice and get a different answer the second time; and that the reason a mould has to be taken off a keel that is out of the water at all is that the hull has to be taken off the bank and put on a hard before a new heel can be fitted to her at all, and that no hard in that harbor will take a boat of about nineteen tons before the middle of November and that this yard did not find that out in a day and had it in a book before it came; and that the reason the account of how a heel is fitted in Lowfen is entered in this column and not in the first is that it is not a count of anything anybody can check from a mole, being a thing said by a man of about thirty-four in a shed about a place he has not been in, and that a man who has not been there and can be asked where he got it has been asked and has said that he is not going to say; and that the reason no figure is entered against a hundred and five minutes of ebb and about two foot of water on the flood is that a foot of water is a fact about a bank and not about a price, and that putting a figure on the number of minutes in a day that a boat can be worked on would be this yard putting a price on the tide; and that the reason the packet offered the figure in chalk and said out loud in the packet that it was not the figure anybody ought to take is that this yard has two figures and no third one, and that a man who is sent four hundred miles on that figure deserves to be told the shape of it before he gets on the boat; and that the reason a man of about twenty-six stood on a third lay wall in the dark for about two hours and forty minutes holding a lamp over the side of somebody else's boat is entered here and not in the first column is that a man who holds a light is not doing the work and is not paid for the work and is not on any of the boats that did it, and that this yard has two figures and no figure at all for standing in the dark; and that the standing list entered in full on the fourteenth of August past is not entered in full on the said Friday and stands, and no part of it was paid, reduced, advanced or improved on that day.*
