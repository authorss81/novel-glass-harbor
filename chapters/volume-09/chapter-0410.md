# Chapter 410

*Thursday the Second Day of September, the Seventy-Eighth Time the Cistern Was Charged and Walked on None of It, the Ninth Day of a Refusal and a Second Paper on the Same Door With a Second Hour on It and a Figure at the Foot of It in the Hand of the Man Who Wrote the Refusal and Who Was Not Asked Anything About the Office He Works In and Was Not Thanked, a Mole Head at Ten Past Five in the Evening With the Light Not Lit, a Leaf Read Back in Full on the Flat of the Bench Stone With the Four Figures on the Face of It and the Definition of Every Figure on the Face of It and a Fourth Column Standing at One Hundred and Twenty-Five Where It Stood and Taking No Line, and the Standing List Referred to and Not Entered in Full*

Thursday the second day of September, in that shed, in a corridor of that office on that hill, and on that mole head.

**The said Thursday was not a wage Tuesday and was not a wipe, and it was the day the cistern at the back of that market was charged at about eight in the morning and walked on by none of it at about half past nine, being the seventy-eighth time, and the top name on the rota board is still off it. The wall behind the reeve's bench came down and went up whole at the angle it has been at since the twelfth and nobody put it back. Nothing came into that yard on the said day and nothing went out of it, and there are two hundred and eighty-four pence in the bag, being one mark ten shillings and fourpence, and the figure that bag is short of the wage of four marks is three hundred and fifty-six pence, being two marks three shillings, and it is not going to be paid and nobody is to write one word under it saying anybody is going to. Nothing has been in a tin in that shed since the twenty-eighth day of April past, and the whole of what came into that yard in the ten days ended on the said day is five hundred and four pence on three days, being the twenty-sixth, the twenty-seventh and the thirty-first, at the figure in chalk on a slate at an end stall, and the whole of what went out of it is three hundred and eighty pence on three days, being the twenty-seventh and the thirty-first in coin on the flat of the bench stone at about four in the afternoon to a caulker of about twenty-three at that same figure, and forty-four pence, being three shillings and eightpence, on the twenty-seventh at about half past ten in the morning for a loaf and a half and a candle and a pot, and one hundred and sixty plus five hundred and four minus three hundred and eighty is two hundred and eighty-four, and the two sums are not the same figure and neither of them is the wage of four marks, being six hundred and forty pence.**

**And the far bench was occupied on the said Thursday from about ten in the morning until about four in the afternoon and was not approached on it, and the holder of that writ did not go over to him, and nothing was put on that bench and read as a gift, and neither brother has said why, and neither brother went near the other at any hour of any of the ten days. Mara Venn was in that harbor and going nowhere, of no office, not on the crew, not restored, not forgiven, forty marks first after food, neither reduced nor paid, and she was on that mole at about half past five in the afternoon. Tobias Wren was on the crown of that mole from ten until four on every one of these ten days, being sixty-one, with eleven pieces of a boat behind him and a boat's hook in his right hand, and on the said day he was sat on the eleventh piece with his back to the water and the hook across his knees doing nothing at all and looking at the two objects of nobody's about nine feet off him, and the holder of that writ stood at the other end of that crown at about half past five and looked at the water and came back down the slip. The notice of the seventeenth of May past is on its wall in a different street for one hundred and eight days and has not been touched and has not been taken down. The bellwright of that harbor has not been asked for one hundred and ten days, the chest is shut, the eight marks and a day of a boat have not been argued, and no digit of anything he ever said is on a page. The two objects of nobody's on that crown are four hundred and forty-eight days old and neither has moved. The bench under that pump is bare for the two hundred and fourteenth night. Two lights at about a mile off that point have not moved in thirty-two days. A man of about fifty-one has said nothing to anybody about a page of six names for fifty days and has been given nothing by anybody since the thirtieth of July. A nine-line sheet with a seal on it has been in that front street for nineteen days and has been answered by nobody.**

---

He went up the hill at about twenty to ten in the morning.

The refusal was off the door. It had been taken down by the hand that wrote it, which was the only hand that could reasonably take it down, and the first paper with its nine lines and its hour and its seal line was still at the corner where it had been since the twenty-second day of August, and a second paper was pinned under it at the same corner, and it was the size of the first one.

He read it.

It had three lines at the top. At the hour named on it, Calder Marr and Mara Venn were permitted in that room. At the hour named on it, and for the whole of it, Saul Drury, copying clerk of that office, would be present in that room.

Under those three lines it had three more, and they were the same three, in the same words as the first paper: that it is a look at one thing out of three and not a taking; that nothing is to go out of that room in any hand; and that the hour named on it is the end of it and not the middle of it.

The figure on it was a time and a place and an arrival, as the figure on the first one was. The hour was ten in the morning. The place was that door. The day was the fifth day of October, being a Tuesday.

And at the foot of it, where the first paper had a seal line that is not the shape of any other seal line that yard has seen, this one had five lines in a clerk's own hand, and there was no seal at the foot of it at all:

*Asked on the twenty-fifth. Held nine days. A second hour is given on the terms of the first paper, of which there are three, and the hour named on this paper is the end of it and not the middle of it. The reason this office held this paper nine days is a figure, and the figure is seven. A copy of that thing, marked with where it came from, is seven hours at that bench and a pair of hands. One hour is one. Both of those are true and neither of them cancels the other.*

*— S. Drury, clerk.*

Calder Marr read that five times.

A copying clerk of about forty with a jacket gone at both elbows had sat down at a table in that building, or stood at one, and had written out, in his own hand, on a piece of paper, the number of hours it takes to make a copy of a thing about nine inches by six that carries with it where it came from.

Seven.

And the hour they had won, on the first morning of this month, on a corridor, with a knock on a door, and that the party of three had gone in and spent and had been very careful in, and that the paper had said in three lines was the end of it and not the middle of it, was one.

Seven is not one. And the man who had written the seven was not a minister and not a face and not a courier and had not been asked a single question about the office he worked in and had not been thanked, and had put his own name under it and the word *clerk* after his name, and had gone back to a table.

Calder Marr stood in that corridor and did not knock and did not put his hand on the door, and then he went back down the hill, and the door was shut behind the papers the whole time.

---

He sat down in that shed at about eleven and put his hand flat on the bench stone and left it there.

"An hour and a room and a look," he said. "That is what we won. I have been saying for a week that an hour is not very much and I have been saying it like a man who has not been told what an hour is worth at that bench, and a man with a flat rule and a piece of pencil behind his ear has told me what it is worth, in a figure, on a piece of paper, at a door, and he is not going to be thanked for it and he is not going to be asked anything about it and he has told me anyway."

Nessa did not look up from the bench stone.

"What is the figure."

"Seven. Seven hours at that bench and a pair of hands, for a copy of that thing, marked with where it came from." He took his hand off the stone. "And we have one, and the paper says the one is the end of it and not the middle of it, and that is not a trick and it is not a kindness and it is not somebody in an office being difficult. It is a man doing arithmetic. He has done it and written it down and put his name under it and gone back to his table, and if I had a bench and a pair of hands and that thing in front of me I should write the same seven on the same paper."

"Then what are we going to do with an hour."

"We are going to go in it when the fifth of October comes and we are going to come out at the end of it having looked at one thing out of three for an hour, and we are not going to have made anything, and that is what was on the door and it was on the door in the first week of August in three lines and we read all three of them and understood two." He sat down. "And I am not going to go up that hill between now and then and I am not going to write to anybody asking for a different day, and if a man in this town wants to say that I was given an hour and did not ask for a second one, he can come and say it to me and I will not have an answer for him."

Mara Venn was not in the shed at that hour. She came up the slip at about half past five in the afternoon, when the market had gone off the mole and the light was out behind her, which it is, and has been since the seventh day of May past, and the lens has been out of it since then and oil costs what a barrel of lamp oil costs on a board in the front street and a figure put on a board cannot be taken off again.

"Read it to me," she said.

He read it to her from the inside of his own coat, all five lines of it, and the figure, and the name at the foot of it and the word after the name, and she stood in the dark and let him get to the end of it.

"Seven," she said.

"Seven."

"And he is a clerk and he is forty and he opened that door for me on the first morning of this month and he put a lamp on a table without being asked and he stood six feet off and said the hour in five words and I did not thank him either, and I have not thought about him once since, and I am thinking about him now."

"That is the whole of it," Calder said. "We got an hour, and it is the end of the hour and not the middle of it, and the man who told us is the one who was standing in the room the whole time and neither of us asked him a thing."

Nessa Pike read the leaf back at about ten past five in the evening, on the flat of the bench stone, in the open, in her own left hand, all four columns including the empty one, and the holder of that writ stood on the other side of it and did not touch it.

"The first column of that leaf has one hundred and forty-two entries in it at this hour and one hundred and forty-three at the close of the day," she said. "There were one hundred and thirty-three of them at the close of Monday the twenty-third day of August past and ten have gone in since, one against each of the ten days from Tuesday the twenty-fourth to this day, and not one day in two of them, and this evening's own is the tenth of the ten and makes one hundred and forty-three at the close. A day of the first column carries one entry against it whatever else that day printed, so that a stranger with a leaf and a pencil can count it."

"The second column has five hundred and eighty-seven lines in it at this hour and five hundred and eighty-nine at the close of the day," she said. "Five hundred and thirty-nine of them were in it at the close of the twenty-third, and five have gone in on each of the ten days since, three before the hour of that day's reading and two after it, and three of this day's went in before this reading and two have gone in in this afternoon after it, which is five hundred and eighty-seven at this hour and five hundred and eighty-nine at the close. **That figure is that book of that yard's own and it is not a count of anything anybody can check from a mole. The first column's one hundred and forty-two and the fourth column's one hundred and twenty-five, at this hour, are a count of entries and a count of lines, and both of them can be taken with a pencil. Those are the two definitions and they are printed here so that a later writer with a pencil knows which of them they are counting and at which of the two moments.**"

"The third column is empty and nobody has been appointed to it, and the hand of a boy of twenty-two is at the head of it with a name beside it that is not that boy's and is not the holder's, and that name was said out loud once in a boat on the thirteenth day of May past and has not been said since and is not going to be said again in this harbor. The four lines above his named line are four, are nameless, are not struck out, and are in about sixty boats on that coast. He was not on the stone on any of these ten days and was put on nothing, and nobody asked him one word about the three nots standing against the eighth of June past, and there is now a ninth week in which nobody in that crew has said one word about them, and no day of any week before the first of July was entered against that line and none is going to be."

"And the fourth column has one hundred and twenty-five lines in it at this hour and one hundred and twenty-five at the close," she said, "and it has taken no line since the twenty-third day of August. It has taken no line on any of these ten days and there is no hundred and twenty-sixth line in it. That is not a closure and it is not a strike-out and nobody has closed that column and nobody in this yard is going to. The reason it has taken no line was entered in the second column at about ten past nine in the evening of the twenty-eighth of August, in that yard's own mouth, with the hour against it, and it is entered once and it is not entered again, and the reason it is in the second column and not in the fourth is that a man on a mole cannot check the second column and a man on a mole can check the fourth."

She shut it and put it under her own coat and neither of them said one word about the figure it gave.

"The whole of the standing list is in the closing block of the last chapter of the month that ended on the twenty-third of August," Calder said, "and it is in the second column of that leaf on the fourteenth of that month, and it is not going to be written out again this week and it is not going to be written out again this year. Three marks to a man of Ashtide. Seven pence to this yard's own shipwright. Eight marks and a day of a boat to that harbor's own bellwright. Eight shillings and tenpence to a woman with her name on the figure. Forty marks first after food. Forty marks. And a page of six names on a mole with a boat as a price and no figure. And a claim against one of the three things in a room on a hill. And a box in this office that is not empty and another box that is. And a sheet in a drawer with a figure on it and no name on it. And a nine-line sheet in the front street."

"None of it moved in ten days."

"None of it moved in ten days, and none of it is going to move, and the fourth column of that leaf has a hundred and twenty-five lines in it and is not going to get a hundred and twenty-sixth one, and the man on the far bench was on that bench from ten until four on each of these ten days and was not approached on any of them, and the shoe beside him that is not his is on every one of them and has been there since the third of May past."

**Here is the whole of where that yard is at the end of ten days, and the standing inventory is not in it. Nothing came into that yard in ten days but three days at a berth and nothing went out of it but two days of a caulker and a loaf and a half and a candle and a pot, and the bag is two hundred and eighty-four pence, being one mark ten shillings and fourpence, against a wage of six hundred and forty pence, being four marks, and the figure it is short of that wage is three hundred and fifty-six pence, being two marks three shillings, and it is never zero and nobody is going to write one word under it saying anybody is going to pay it. The wall came down bare once in the ten days, being the sixty-ninth, on Monday the thirtieth. The cistern was charged three times and walked on none of them, being the seventy-sixth on Thursday the twenty-sixth, the seventy-seventh on Monday the thirtieth and the seventy-eighth on this day, and the top name on that rota board is still off it. Two wage Tuesdays fell and the column did not move on either of them, so that forty-four weeks stood fallen due with no envelope against any of them at the open of these ten days and forty-five stand at the close of them. The notice of the seventeenth of May past is on its wall for one hundred and eight days. The bellwright of that harbor has not been asked for one hundred and ten days and the chest is shut. The two objects of nobody's are four hundred and forty-eight days old and neither has moved. The bench under that pump is bare for the two hundred and fourteenth night. Two lights at about a mile off that point have not moved in thirty-two days, counted from the hour they were first seen on the evening of the first of August past. A man of about fifty-one has said nothing to anybody for fifty days and has been given nothing by anybody. A nine-line sheet with a seal on it has been in the front street for nineteen days and has been answered by nobody. A custody room on a hill was entered once in these ten days, for one hour, at one thing out of three, with a clerk of that office in it the whole time, and the thing on the far shelf went back on the same shelf under the same number and nothing went out of that room in anybody's hand, and the middle shelf was between the door and the far wall on the way in and on the way out and nobody turned his head at it or at the book above it, and the claim in that book has not been withdrawn and nobody in that harbor has asked for it to be withdrawn. A second paper is on that door with a second hour on it, and at the foot of it, in a clerk's own hand and with no seal, is a figure, and the figure is seven. And the light is not lit, and there is no flour in that market, and a barrel of lamp oil is four hundred and fifty pence, and the north berth is out, and the tide gang is four men short, and the hose is dead until the spring, and the hard at Ashtide is unanswered, and the third lay fills on the first of April and one berth is short. Three hundred and ninety-one pieces of recovered glass have been landed and entered at that harbor since the first of April and the figure is checkable with a pencil and there is no name against it and it is in no mouth. And the far bench was occupied on all ten of the ten days from ten until four, and was not approached on any of them, and neither brother went near the other at any hour of any of them, and the fourth column of that leaf stands at one hundred and twenty-five and has taken no line in ten days and is not closed.**

*First column, entered on Thursday the second day of September next at about half past nine in the evening in that shed: that the said Thursday was the seventy-eighth time the cistern was charged at about eight in the morning and walked on by none of it at about half past nine, and that the wall came down and went up whole at the angle it has been at since the twelfth; and that the forty-fourth wage Tuesday fell on the twenty-fourth and the forty-fifth on the thirty-first and the column did not move on either of them, so that forty-four weeks stood fallen due at the open of the ten days and forty-five stand at the close of them; and that a refusal pinned at the corner of a shut door in a corridor on that hill on the twenty-fifth of August, in four lines in a clerk's own hand, was taken down on the morning of the said day by the hand that wrote it; and that a second paper was pinned under the first at the same corner, carrying the same three lines naming two as permitted and one as present for the whole of the hour, and the same three lines under them, being a look at one thing out of three and not a taking, nothing to go out of that room in any hand, and the hour named on it being the end of it and not the middle of it; and that the hour on the second paper is ten in the morning on the fifth day of October, being a Tuesday, at that door; and that at the foot of the second paper, where the first had a seal line, there are five lines in the clerk's own hand and no seal, being that a second hour is asked for on the twenty-fifth, that it was held nine days, that a second hour is given on the terms of the first paper, that the reason it was held nine days is a figure and the figure is seven, that a copy of that thing marked with where it came from is seven hours at that bench and a pair of hands, and that one hour is one; and that the holder of that writ read those five lines five times at about twenty to ten in the morning and did not knock and did not touch the door and went back down the hill; and that three hundred and ninety-one went into the first column of that leaf on the first of September with the five summands and the line count under it and with no name against it, and that it is a figure of pieces of recovered glass landed and entered at that harbor since the first of April, counted off one line at a time with a pencil at a wall in a room off the west basin, and that a stranger with a leaf and a pencil can add the five summands and take it again without anybody's word; and that the leaf was read back at about ten past five in the evening of the said day on the flat of the bench stone, in the open, by the ship's hand in her own left hand, all four columns including the empty one, with both definitions of the four figures on the face of that reading; and that the fourth column has one hundred and twenty-five lines in it at the hour of that reading and one hundred and twenty-five at the close of the said day, and that it has taken no line on any of the ten days and that there is no hundred and twenty-sixth line in it and that the column is not closed and has not been struck out and nobody in that yard is going to close it.*
*Second column, not checkable from a mole, entered on the said Thursday at about half past nine in the evening in that shed, and no name is against it: that the reason the fourth column took no line in these ten days is that that yard's time went into the first column and into a convoy and into a caulker's day, and that the fourth column is a page no man can check, and that there was a man on the flat of the bench stone at four in the morning on the twenty-seventh who had to be paid first, and that the reason is entered once, in this column, in that yard's own mouth, at about ten past nine in the evening of the twenty-eighth of August, and is not entered again, and every ledger that follows it carries the fact that it has been entered and does not enter it a second time; and that a copying clerk of about forty who wrote a figure at the foot of a paper on a door is a working man doing a job and not a face and not a courier, and was not asked anything about the office he works in and was not thanked then and is not being thanked now, and that what he wrote is a piece of arithmetic and not a refusal dressed up as one; and that a party of three won a door and an hour and a look at one thing out of three, and the hour is the end of it and not the middle of it, and a copy of that thing marked with where it came from is seven hours at that bench, and there is no way of making the one into the seven by asking for it and this yard is not going to ask for it; and that a woman of no office read that paper out of the inside of a coat in the dark on a mole at about half past five in the evening and said that she had not once thought about the man who was standing in that room the whole time, and that he is the one who was there, and that he is not to be turned into a thing she is going to be grateful for; and that the standing list entered in full on the fourteenth of August is not entered in full here and stands, and no part of it was paid, reduced, advanced or improved on any of the ten days, and that a ninth line of six names on a mole is not a return and is not a figure and has a boat against it as a price; and that the whole of it is a harbor with no flour in it and a light out and a bag of one mark ten shillings and fourpence and a wage column forty-five weeks short, and nothing in these ten days made that harbor a penny better off and nobody in it is to be told that anything was done for it.*
