# Chapter 3

*Work Before Proof*

Nessa Pike had the yard's account book open on the workbench when Calder came in, which meant she had been in his things, which she did about twice a week and always said she had not.

"You've drawn the air pot," she said.

"I drew the air pot."

"You drew a third of the air pot on a market day with a pump job and a chain job in it, which is a hundred marks of air against thirty-one of work, and I have been standing here since the tide turned doing arithmetic that I do not like."

Calder put the slate down on the bench and squared it with his knuckle, which he did with slates and did not do with paper.

"I want to go out to the old shoal this afternoon."

"I know you do. I've had Bevin in here twice asking me for a spare pull of the pump's displacement gauge and I sent him away both times with a straight face, which took effort." She set the pen down. "So here is what I want, and you can say no to it, in which case I will take my gauges and my labor and I will be somewhere else by the middle of the week and I will not be sorry about it."

"Go on."

"One. We log it as ordinary salvage. A fouled ground tackle, a piece of iron, a wreck nobody has claimed. That is what we go out for. Not for glass, not for a story, not for a bell. If anybody ever asks me what the Free Keel did in the old shoal, I am going to have to be able to say the same three sentences, and if I can't say them, I am going to be the woman who did not know what the Free Keel was doing."

Calder did not answer at once, and she watched him not answer.

"Two. The position goes on the wall. Your mark, my numbers, the hour. It goes up the day we go out, not the day it is convenient, and it goes up where the fish market can read it and not only where salvage can read it. Three. Bevin on the watch and not on the bottom. Bevin Rook is twenty-two and he will do whatever you tell him to do, including the thing that gets him killed, and if you take him down I will take my gauges and go to Ternwatch where they pay on time and ask no questions."

"He has been on the watch before."

"He has been on the watch before at eight fathoms, in the harbour, in daylight, with a boat over him. Nine fathoms in the old shoal with a bottom you cannot see is a different job." She picked the pen up again. "If you want him off the watch, say so out loud and give me a reason I can write down. If you want him on the bottom, say that too. What I will not do is be the one who decides later that you were wrong."

Calder looked at the slate, and then at the air pot's line in her book, and then out through the open door, where Bevin Rook was circling the *Kittiwake* and pretending not to be.

"Watch," he said. "Agreed. And he reads the depth and the air aloud every ten minutes, and if he misses a reading he comes up, and that is not a discussion."

"Good." She wrote it. "Then I want your word that if the shift puts that rib in a bad place, we come off it and come off it with the iron, and not with a piece of it."

"You have my word."

"Then write it on your slate, because a word to me is worth nothing and a word on a slate in your own hand is worth something in a hearing."

He wrote it. That took ninety seconds and it cost him more than the eleven marks off the chain had.

The whistle went at the yard gate, two short and one long, which was Tobias Wren announcing himself, because nobody could hear him coming and he had stopped apologising for it forty years ago.

He came in with a hand on the doorframe and looked at the fragment on the bench, and then at the harbor.

"Marr," he said, coming close. "You have a piece of glass in my yard. Old stock. Older than the Registry's grandmother. I pulled plate like that off a hull out past the salt banks when I was thirty and I never once heard where the builders came from, and I stopped asking, because asking is how a diver ends up going down for an answer instead of for a job." He straightened up. "Do you know what it is?"

"I know what it isn't."

"That is not the same as knowing what it is, and you will hear people tell you it is later." He looked at the door. "There's your boy. He's been circling that boat for an hour waiting for somebody to say yes."

Calder went out into the yard and down the row of benches, and his father was at the far end with his back to the door, planing a scarf on a paddle that had been out of work since Michaelmas.

Alden Marr kept every tool in its place. The bench was not so much a bench as a diagram of a man's memory: the spokeshave, the drawknife, the caulking irons in their rack in the order they were used, the pots of tar in the order they were opened. He had a cough that came on a cycle of about forty minutes, and it was a dry one, and it ended in a sound like a door closing on a box.

He did not turn round when Calder came up.

"You'll be wanting the report," he said.

"I want the plate note," Calder said.

The drawknife stopped.

"There is a plate in the report," Alden said. "The copy in the inquest record says a plate was cut off her own iron and never came back to the pile. I've read it. You told me you'd read it when you were nineteen and you have not read it since, which is a thing I did not know until this moment."

"I read it again last month."

"Then you know what it says and you have come down here to see what I will say about it, and I am telling you now, in this yard, where I can see the door: I will not say anything about it." He went back to the scarf, and the blade came off in a pale curl. "There is an oil-store fire in a fortnight and a boat to relaunch and a hand that doesn't get as far up as it did. Do your work. Leave that paper where it is."

"There is a woman from the Registry in this yard this evening with a book, and the object is going to say things about where it came from, and if it says anything about a plate I will not be able to answer her."

"Then you will not be able to answer her." Alden set the drawknife down, which he never did. "I signed what I signed in a bad year with a bad cough and a reeve's boy standing over me, and the man who wrote the words is not going to help you by explaining himself to his son at the end of a working day."

The cough came and went on longer than forty seconds. Calder stood through all of it, holding nothing, and understood, for the fifteenth time in thirty-two years, that the silence was not protection and was not an answer and was the only thing his father had left to give him.

"Then tell me one thing that is not the report," he said. "Did you ever see the ferry's own survey?"

"Get out of my yard," Alden said.

---

The fouled ground line had been lying in a salt cloth on the Free Keel bench since noon, wet and slack, and Calder read it with a hand lens and a pair of good eyes, which was a worse combination than a lens and no eyes at all, but it was what he had.

Right-hand lay. Four-strand. Tarred hemp, and not new tar either; the sizing under it had gone to powder in places, which put the laying at four or six seasons old. The seizing was the useful part: round stuff, three plain turns and a hitch, dressed from the left side, the turns closing left-handed over the standing part. Left-handed dressing on right-hand lay is not a mistake. It is what you do when the man doing it was taught by somebody who laid line for a living.

"Eastern," Calder said to the bench.

He'd been at it a half hour when Sera Mott came in with a basket and her own lamp, because she had heard on the quay that the Free Keel had glass out, and Sera Mott was sixty-eight and retired and had a way of arriving at a conversation forty minutes before everybody else had finished being polite about it.

She put the basket down, looked at the fragment, said, "That is a bad thing to have on your bench," and then bent over the line and did not touch it, counting the strands with a finger held above them, the way a person does who spent fifty years paying out twine over a side.

"Right-hand," she said. "Ash Quay or the banks. Not this harbor."

"That is what the Registry woman said."

"Then the Registry woman can read." She straightened up. "Now. Where has that anchor of yours got itself to."

"Eight feet, in the mole's toe, off the third seam."

"Then you have not touched it. Well." She sat down on the end of the bench, which she was not supposed to do. "That came off a boat working the old shoal. Not out of the bar, because there is nothing in the bar to lose an anchor in, and not out of the channel, because nobody in the channel has a ground tackle that has been out six years. There is one boat laid out there that nobody has ever claimed, Marr, and everybody knows she is there and nobody has ever gone for her, because she is only iron and she is two days' work to get anything off her, and every soul in this harbor is two days short. Shoal-end coaster, gone eight years back, name of the *Alder Reach*. Her ground tackle was a fine lay of stuff and a new anchor, and when she went she lost both, and the new anchor came in on a westerly and sat in your mole for six years, which everybody knew and nobody cared about, because it was in the way of nothing."

"Nobody ever claimed her."

"Nobody ever claimed her. That is your wreck, then, and the harbor's list says so, and you will want that list before you go out on her, because the list is what makes her ordinary."

---

They went out at half past two with the tide making and a running swell on the mole's outside, and Bevin took the watch and read the depth and the air aloud every ten minutes exactly as he had promised, and on the fourteenth minute he said, "Nineteen fathoms, ninety, air two hundred and forty," in a voice that made Calder want to hit him, because a deck hand who learns to call numbers clearly at nineteen fathoms is a deck hand who has something to fall back on.

At the inner edge of the shoal the water went from nineteen fathoms to something shallower and colder in under twenty yards, and the bottom came up on the sounder until it came up fast, and where it came up fast the lead brought up a long pale line lying the way a thing lies when it went down with a list and stayed.

Nessa had said she would not come. She came, in the bow of the *Kittiwake* with her sounding lead in her hand, because a boat sailing over a shoal with an underestimating lead is a boat that has made a decision without its owner.

She sounded along it twice, and on the third time she put the lead over the shoulder into the water behind the frames and let it go on the sheet, and it did not come up, and she hauled it back in by hand.

"Eight fathoms on the frames and eleven on the shoulder behind her, and then twenty-two in the hole off her quarter, with no bottom in that I can find," she said. "And that pale line is the wreck. That is her keel, or whatever you call the thing a boat has got instead of a spine."

Calder put a glass down and looked, and had to make himself look a second time.

The frames ran in an even run of fourteen, the way frames run when something set them and did not hurry, and between them the sea had not filled in with the same grey nothing that fills in a hull. It was a pale line along the length of the bottom, and it was not one object. It was laid out on a bearing that was not a wreck's bearing, because no boat lies with her keel as straight as a rule for eighty yards unless somebody meant it to.

"That is not her," Nessa said. "The *Alder Reach* is iron and that is not iron, and it is a long way off the wreck, and it is on a straight."

"It is on a straight." Calder did not take his eye off it. "It is a straight line lying on a shoal with a bad bottom, going out toward the west, and I have not the least idea in the world what I am looking at, and I am not going to say one word about it in front of the boat until we know where we are putting a permit."

At the edge of the shoal, where the *Alder Reach*'s frames came up out of the water at a low shoulder, Calder got a hand lens and looked at her ironwork from four feet away, over the gunwale, in the swell, and found the mark.

It was small, and it was struck deep, and it was on the inside of the sixth frame, where a caulker's iron would land and where nobody would ever look at it again. Two short strokes crossed by a third, and a number, and the number was a year.

"Eleven," he said.

"Eleven what?" Bevin asked.

"Eleven, and a season mark. That is a shop mark, a repairer's mark, struck in plain reckoning by somebody who wanted a boat's owner to be able to read it and argue with the man who put it there." He put the lens down and wiped it on his cuff. "It means a yard on this coast opened a plate in her inside framing in that year and put in a new frame and did not fair it. It is ordinary. Every boat on this coast has four or five of them."

It was eleven years back, which is the middle of the Gannet decade, and it was a year Oren had been aboard the *Aster Gannet* with a slate of his own in his belt, and it was a year in which a boy of twenty had been signing his numbers the way Alden's drawknife lay on its bench, from the left, with the turns closing left over the standing part, and Calder had been on the quay that year and had watched him do it over a coil of line as if the whole world were a seam he was dressing.

He set his nails into the gunwale and left them there.

"A shop hand of that decade," he said. "That's all it is. There's a hundred of them on this coast and no more to it than that."

---

The reeve's office at half past three had a second stove going and the window steamed over, and Kest had a sheet in front of him that was not a form Calder had seen before.

"Sit down. Read this all the way through before you say anything, and then we will talk about the bond, and then, if we are still friends, I will tell you what it does not cover."

It was short, because it was a permit, and every line of it was a limit.

It named the site as the inner edge of Gannet Shoal. It named two dates, and Calder had to turn the sheet over to see that they were the next two working days, which meant that this afternoon, if the weather and the tide were good, was the first of them, and there was no second chance at it if it went bad. It named the harbor line as the boundary, and drew the line, and the drawing was a straight mark on a small plan of the shoal, and that mark was the boundary of everything Calder Marr and his crew were permitted to touch.

The bond was forty marks.

"Eleven off the chain," Calder said. "Nessa can put twenty against work. I can put nine against the *Kittiwake*, and if I have not got the nine by four o'clock, I will not have a boat."

"Correct."

"That is every mark in the yard."

"That is every mark in the yard, and the bond is the harbor's assurance that you will leave the site and not slip out to it again on a third day and call it a continuation." Kest laid his finger on the plan. "Marr, I am going to say the rest of this once, and I would like you to write it down, because a man who heard it will forget it and a man who read it will not."

Calder took out the slate.

"You may work the inner edge, on these two days, inside the harbor line, on ordinary salvage, and you will log every hour of air and every hour of labor and everything your hands raise, and I will countersign it. Your share of anything you bring up is yours against the harbor's share, and if the object of undetermined origin is still undetermined when your window closes, then it is not yours and it is not mine."

"That is the window. Now the other part." Kest turned the plan so the straight mark lay between them. "The *Alder Reach* lies with her frames at the shoal's inside shoulder, and you may work her. The ground tackle and the anchor bedded off her port quarter are inside the line and you may shift them, because an anchor is iron and iron is not glass and the harbor will not fight you over iron. The wreck's outer marks are past the line."

He put his finger on the water beyond the mark.

"And the cutter's recorded mooring ground is past the line, and has been since before your father was in the water. I did not draw it on this, because I have never been able to make a map on which it is not a lie, but I will tell you where it is, and then I will not say it again. It is out there, on the same bearing as your straight line, three-quarters of a mile, in water that is eight fathoms over old ground."

Kest sat back.

"You may not touch it. Not with a boat, not with a float, not with an anchor hook on a long line, not with a diver in a bell on your two lawful days. If you go out there, the harbor charter does not cover you, the permit does not cover you, and there is no paper in Morrow Quay that covers you, and I will be the man who writes the sentence afterwards, and I will write it plainly, because I am not Kest's man and neither are you."

"Who holds the cutter's ground?"

"You will find out when you read the paper in the public file. It is thirteen years old, it belongs to a naval salvage report, and it is the only paper in Morrow Quay that says the *Vigil* lay anchored off that shoal on the morning of the Gannet Cut." He put the sheet back in front of Calder. "Sign the bond, Marr. I have wanted to give you this permit since I read what the dockmaster wrote about you at that pump, and I have had to wait until you came in here with a log and a woman and a bearing, because I would like it on the record that you had something to bring me before I gave you anything."

Calder read the permit twice. He read the two dates, and he read the two words at the edge of the plan that said *harbor line*, and he thought about his father at a bench with a drawknife in his hand being asked to explain a thirteen-year-old piece of paper, and about a woman in a Registry coat writing in a nail hand that a man had a personal association with an object and had not said what it was.

He signed the bond on the ninth mark, and dated it, and did not say thank you, because Kest had not done him a favor and did not want one.
