# Chapter 33

*What Is Left Without the Sheet*

The room came back at half past two with the harbor authority's own book on the table in it, and the naval officer looked at the book for a while and then said that he withdrew his second motion, because the book was in the second drawer and not the first, and because the ground of it was the ground of his first motion, and a vessel does not put the same argument into a municipal record twice.

"Enter that as withdrawn and not as refused," Kest said, "and let the reason stand in the room as it was given, which is that the book was in the wrong drawer and not that the officer was wrong, because in three years somebody will read this page and will want to know which of the two it was. And the first motion stands and is heard on Thursday morning at ten, and the entry of the third is not touched before then, and Mr Officer knows where the book is now and so do I."

The officer's man had not finished with the vessel's own paper when the officer said the thing that Calder had been waiting for since the seventh, and it was not a question and it was not an objection.

"Mr Marr. You withdrew the bearing sheet. The reeve has struck it from this sitting at your own request in eleven seconds, and I have watched a man do that to a document of his own making, and I am not going to pretend I did not notice the speed of it." He had the notebook shut. "So I am going to ask the plain question, because you are a plain man and I would like to be shown a plain answer. You had three points on a market wall and two of them were yours and one was this office's, and the two that were yours were joined to each other and to the office's by a drawing of your own, and the drawing is now in a drawer with a slip of paper on it. What is left?"

"Nothing that is mine and yours together," Calder said. "That is the honest answer and I would rather say it in a room than find out what it was worth in a corridor."

"Then say what is left that is yours."

---

It took the better part of two hours and it came out of three drawers, and only one of the drawers was the harbor authority's own, and Kest opened it himself, on his own ground, in his own hearing room, without being asked twice, and Calder noticed that a reeve who produces his own records before he is asked for them is a man who has decided something and is only waiting for the room to catch up.

The first thing was the tag.

It was a strip of tarred board about a hand long with a hole at each end, and the harbor authority's own brass mark was burned into one end of it, and the office's number for its ground tackle was written on the other end in white paint by somebody who had not been in the building for forty years. It is the tag the office keeps on its own tackle, and it went down on the harbor's own crane before the middle of last month, on the float that the reeve's own hand was tied to, with the number of the seam still legible in chalk on the rag, and it came up on the twentieth out of eight feet off the mole's toe with two fathoms of old iron on the end of it and went into the authority's store, and the tag is in a tin in a drawer in the room Calder Marr was standing in.

"This is not a document about glass," Kest said, putting it down flat. "It is a board with a number on it, and it is the office's own tackle and not the yard's. Read the number aloud, Mr Clerk, and then put it away, because it is not in issue and I have put it in front of this room of my own motion."

"Third seam," the clerk said.

"That is the third seam of the mole's toe, and the tag came up off an iron that was lying under eight feet of water in the toe off that seam, and the lead line on it was not mine, it was a yard's, and it had parted before my men took the iron, and the float was mine and the float is the only part of that morning that anybody in this harbor can still read." Kest put two fingers on the board. "Mr Marr, before that iron was anything else it was a working anchor belonging to an unclaimed shoal coaster named the *Alder Reach*, and this office has known that since the seventh of October, and the ground it was lost on is the ground on which the *Alder Reach* lies, and the *Alder Reach* is entered in this office's own list as unclaimed and has been on that list for eight years. I have the list. Mr Clerk, the list."

The clerk brought a book with a limp cover and turned it to a page and read from it in the flat voice of a man filling in a box.

"*Shoal-end coaster, name the Alder Reach, unclaimed, ground anchor and ground line reported lost in a westerly some six years back, ground tackle entered as light, master and crew unknown, no claim entered.*"

"That is this office's own list and it is eight years old and it is the only document in Morrow Quay that says the name of the boat whose anchor was in your mole's toe," Kest said. "And I have not had it out of that drawer in eight years, and I want the room to understand that I had not thought about it once until this morning, and that is not a thing to be proud of in a room with a naval officer in it."

---

The second thing was the duplicate.

Bevin Rook brought it up from the chest — the chest was still pledged, and it came to the room in a hand-barrow with a rope round it, and the clerk signed for it and wrote the hour on the slip, and nobody in the room found that funny except the naval officer, who found it extremely funny and did not say so.

The duplicate was four sheets with ten crew copies' worth of the same hand on them, and Calder did not read it. He had it read. He put the officer's man in front of it — the officer had brought a man with him on the Tuesday, a lieutenant with a satchel and a bad coat, and had not said a word all morning — and he read the fouled line's entry out of the duplicate in the words of the yard's own record, in the order the record put them, with the hour on each.

"Line, fouled, seized into the lower cut face of the object, four feet of it, brought up with the object and surrendered with it and now in the custody of the circuit office under one of two numbers. Lay, right-hand, four-strand, tarred hemp, sizing under it gone to powder in places, which puts the laying at four or six seasons. Seizing, round stuff, three plain turns and a hitch, dressed from the left side, the turns closing left-handed over the standing part. District: the lay is not this harbor's, and the dressing is a thing you are taught by somebody who laid line for a living, and the record says the lay points to Ash Quay or the Lowfen banks and not to Morrow Quay, because a Morrow Quay boat lays left-hand and this was made by somebody who did not."

"It is a rope," the officer said.

"It is a rope," Calder agreed. "And a ground line is a thing that exists only where a boat has been anchored, and a fouled one is a thing that exists only where a boat has been anchored long enough and in water dirty enough to grow on it. There is no such thing as a fouled ground line in a market. There is one on the ground of the *Alder Reach*, or on the ground of some other boat lost on that shoal, and this office's own list says there is one boat on that ground and that her ground tackle was entered as light and went in a westerly some six years back. And six years after she went, her anchor was in the toe of my mole holding a piece of glass in eight feet of water, with her ground line cut and seized into it."

"That is a rope and a book," the officer said.

"That is a rope and a book and an iron with a kerf in it," Calder said, "and the kerf is the third thing and I am going to hand it to your man myself because I do not want you to think I have been keeping it."

---

The kerf was on the harbor's own iron.

It was in the crown shackle of a two-fathom shoal anchor that lay on the Free Keel stack under a tarred cloth with a harbor-authority float line's weight on it, and it had come up out of eight fathoms off the *Alder Reach*'s port quarter on the permit of the twelfth of October, and the shackle had a bright face on one side of it with no rust on it at all and a saw kerf in the metal at the same place as the wear from the shank.

"Somebody sawed that shackle nearly through," Calder said, "and then it was loaded again, and then it came off, and it is on our deck with a bright cut on it. That has been in our book since the twelfth of October and it is in the harbor's own entry of the twenty-eighth, and I am putting it in front of this room of my own motion, and I am going to say four things about it and I am going to say them in this order.

"It is a fact about iron. It is not a fact about a person. It does not show who cut it, or when, or why, or whether it was cut by a thief or by a man who could not get a proper shackle and would rather saw one than wait, and there are four harbors on this coast where a man does that. And if the vessel's man has a use for a cut shackle, the vessel's man may have a use for a cut shackle."

He put the book down and turned round to the room, and the room was very quiet, and there was a man at the back by the door who had a wax tablet and a bad coat and who had been in the room for four hours without saying anything, and Calder looked at him and then away, deliberately, because a man with a wax tablet is a man who is deciding what to write down and Calder did not want to be in the room while it was being written.

---

The fourth thing was the admission, and Calder made it himself, and he made it before the officer could ask for it, and Kest's pen stopped on the first line of it.

"Here is what my answer does not do, and I am going to say it now and not on Monday, because if the yard's whole geography is a drawing in a drawer, then the first thing a room wants to know is whether the drawing was load-bearing.

"It is not, and the plate is the reason it is not. The plate was lifted off a transparent keel on the shoal's inner edge at eight fathoms on the twelfth of October, on a permit this office issued, inside the harbor line, and the ground the permit named is ground that the *Vigil's* claim, as filed, describes — it is between the inside shoulder of that shoal and the outer marks, and the rib was on the shoal's inner edge. The plate came off ground this vessel has claimed. That is not a bearing. That is a permit and a depth and a date, and the depth and the date are in a labor log this office countersigned on the day, and I do not need a drawing to say it, because a permit names a place and a depth is a number a man put a lead over.

"And the claim was lodged on the fourteenth of October at ten-forty in the morning, and the permit was issued on the twelfth, and the two days in between are the only two days in this entire business that nobody has been able to do anything with, and I would like the room to have them and to make of them what it likes.

"And the shard." He stopped. The stove ticked. "The shard came up off the mole's toe, at the third seam, at about eight feet, and the mole's toe is this office's ground and not the ground described in the second sheet of that claim, and it is a mile and a quarter of stone and a mile of water away from the inside shoulder of the shoal, and there is no argument in the world that puts it on that ground.

"It never could. The only thing in this whole undertaking that ever tried to put it there was the purpose I filed on the twenty-seventh of last month, and the bearing sheet, and I struck the word *placed* out of my own copy of that purpose on the second of this month and left it standing in the reeve's hearing record because a filed document cannot come out, and this morning I struck the sheet itself out of this sitting at my own request, and I would like the record to show that the two of those things together are the withdrawal of the yard's only attempt to put a piece of glass on a naval claim's ground, and that the yard made it itself and in public and before it was asked to."

The naval officer of the *Vigil* sat in the corner with his hands on his knees and did not interrupt once, and when Calder had finished he said, in the flat voice of a man reading a gauge.

"Mr Marr, that is the first time in three days that anybody has said to me that their own exhibit is worth nothing. I am going to tell you what the vessel will do with it, because you have been honest and I am not going to be clever about a man who has just cost himself. The vessel will say that the plate is on claimed ground and that a plate lifted under a permit two days before a claim is a plate lifted on ground that had already been claimed, because the cutter's claim of the fourteenth of last month is not the first claim of that shoal and the harbor authority has never produced the earlier ones."

Kest said, "That is a matter the harbor authority will answer for itself when it is asked, and the harbor authority's answer is that it will produce what it has and will not invent what it has not, and I will deal with it in this room and not in a corridor. Enter it in the record as a statement and not as a request, Mr Officer, because it is neither."

---

Mara Venn was called on the question of whether the yard was asking her to certify anything, and she answered it before Calder got to his feet, which was a thing she had been doing to him since the fifteenth of October and which he had still not learned to be comfortable with.

"Mr Marr has not asked me to certify the ground, and if he had, I would have refused, and I would like the reason on the record because the reason is that a ground is a position and a position is not a thing I certify, and I have already refused once in this business and I am not going to refuse differently twice in a month so that one of the refusals looks better than the other." She had her book shut on her knee. "I will witness the method. I will say that the fouled line was wet and slack when it was laid on a bench and that the seizing and the lay were read under a lamp and written down in the order they were read. I will say that the object was under the anchor and that the anchor's shackle held the glass down and that the line went out of the fragment's fold under the crown. Those are things a person can check. I will not say the ground was inside anything, and I will not say it was outside anything, and a person who has read the duplicate and has the authority's own list and has the tag in his hand can work it out in ten minutes with a pencil and I am not the office for that work."

"You have read my line in your own book," Calder said, "and you wrote under it that the district is the Lowfen banks."

"I did, and it is my hand and it is correct, and it is a statement about a rope and not about a place, and I will read it aloud if this office wishes and I will not certify it."

"Enter it as offered and not taken," Kest said, and the clerk wrote it, and Calder sat down, and understood about half an hour later, in the road, that the whole of the morning's work had turned on a thing he had not noticed at the time, which was that Mara Venn had refused in front of a naval officer before he could spend her on it, and had done it in a form that could not be quoted later as a refusal of anything else.

---

The last hour of the Tuesday went on the depth of the plate, which was a number four men had put a lead over and which the officer's man measured off the duplicate against the scaled drawing, and which came out the same.

At four-forty the officer stood up.

"Mr Kest, I have a question for the record, and it is not an objection and it is not a motion, and I will hand it to you rather than to the yard, because I would like it to be yours."

Kest said, "Mr Officer, the yard is in this room and the question is about the yard's figures, and if I hold it, the yard will hear it second and that is not how this office works. Put it to the yard and I will rule on it."

The officer turned round.

"Mr Marr. The three points are on the market wall with a difference of ten yards on the second of them, and the reeve has ruled that no line may be drawn between the three, and I have no quarrel with that ruling. I want to know what a difference of ten yards means when the number it is a difference of is not better than ten yards, and I want the answer on the fourteenth. I am not asking whether the three points are on a straight. I am asking whether the yard has a measurement or an agreement."

"Nothing is to be said to it today," Kest said. "The question stands unanswered in this office's record, and the yard is given until the sitting of the fourteenth to answer it, and if the yard's answer is that the interval is even, this office will receive that answer and will not test it, and if the yard's answer is that the interval is not even, this office will receive that answer and will enter it and will not be surprised, and I would like it understood by everybody in this room, including the yard, that this office is quite prepared for the second answer and has no preference in the matter at all."

Calder Marr said nothing at all, and Bevin Rook put his right hand flat on the cover of the crew's book and looked at the floor, and it was the first time in a fortnight that Bevin Rook had been the man in the room with the worst face on him.

"Then I will say what I think it is," the officer said, "before we stop, and I will say it in my own words and not as an answer, because the vessel has not been asked that question and the vessel has answered it anyway. The interval is a comparison of a distance measured by a tender with a hand lead from a boat on the outer side of a mole, against a distance measured by this office's own run along its own course with a wire on a lead, corrected by a column that this office has admitted in this room it has never had anybody read. The first is not better than ten yards. The second is not better than the wire. The difference between them is ten yards, which is the first figure's own tolerance, and a difference equal to the tolerance of one of the two numbers is not a measurement. It is an agreement, and an agreement is not a figure, and the vessel notes that the two numbers are of different kinds and were taken by different hands in different weathers on different days and cannot be set against one another in a room and called even. And the first of the three is not a bearing either. It is a thing a boy found with his hand on a bottom at nine feet."

"Entered as a statement and not as an answer," Kest said, "and the question stands unanswered, and the yard has until the fourteenth, and nobody in this room is to tell any other man in this room that the officer has told us what the interval is, because the officer has told us what he thinks it is, and those are two different things and only one of them is in the book."

At five o'clock the reeve adjourned the sitting to the fourteenth, and the clerk read the adjournment out, and the room emptied into a road where a fish market crowd had been standing since eleven in the morning in the cold for no better reason than that it was a Tuesday that had turned into something else, and the *Vigil's* tender lay a quarter of a mile off the mole on the bearing of the pale run with her floats run out and her oars shipped, and she had held that station for twenty-four days, and the tide was making off the mole face, and out in the road the harbor's tide bell went three strokes, and under the third of them, a third of a second behind and too even to be anything that hangs in a road, the note came up out of the bottom off the mole with its three marks and its flick.

Bevin Rook had the book out before the third stroke had finished.

"Four-forty-six," he said. "Top of the make, west-northwest light air, water rising two inches on the mole face, delayed note under the harbor's three. No source. Not written as a voice. Heard in the road by Rook and Marr and the officer of the cutter, who has written it down too, and I would like it read back because I have never once written down a thing a naval officer has written down with me."

The officer of the *Vigil*, from the doorway, said, "My man has it at four-forty-six. I do not know what it is and neither do you, and I would like the record to show that we agree about that, in writing, on a Tuesday, in the road."
