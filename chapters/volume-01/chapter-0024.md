# Chapter 24

*The Chain Comes Tight*

The southerly was on the slate at ten past five on Saturday the 29th and Sella Brigg read it standing at the yard gate with her own hood up, and she read it out loud because she had learned in September that men who work boats do not look at a sky.

"Southwest, four, getting up. That is a boat's warning and it is not a fisher's complaint."

"The board says twenty-six for the ninth seam," Bevin said.

"The board says twenty-six for the ninth seam and the ninth seam is on the mole's inner face, and the mole's inner face on a southwest four is the worst water in this harbor, and I have been in it twice in my life and I remember both times." She turned the slate round so the yard could see it, which was the courtesy she had extended to every one of them since the fifteenth. "I am going to say three things and I am going to say them in this order, and then I am not going to say them again.

"My master will take the second boat out on a morning's charter work because it is the harbor's ground and the harbor's own work and a day's wage, and he has said so. I have told him he should be off that water by ten. If he is not off it by ten, then the standing rescue cover my crew gave your yard on the nineteenth is not offered again this winter, by my crew, and I will not stand in a net loft and explain it twice, and I am telling you now so that it is a thing we both agreed instead of a thing you did to us.

"And I am not going to be the one who says no to twenty-six marks with a defense in sixteen days. So I am saying all three and going home."

"Agreed, all three, in those words." Calder looked at the crew. "Nessa."

"I want the pump hired, three a tide, and I want the ninth seam's fouling taken off by nine o'clock whatever it costs us, because the tenth hour in that water is when a man starts doing something he should not do to finish something he was paid for."

"And if the master wants to turn at ten."

"Then he turns at ten and we are ninety per cent done and we take ninety per cent of the money and we do not argue in the boat." She capped the pen. "Nobody in that boat finishes a job. Everybody in that boat comes up."

---

They were rigged on the mole's inner side at twenty past seven with the harbor's pump standing on the wall above them, and it took twenty minutes to shift the fouled mooring into work, and the fouling was worse than a fouling because the chain had taken a turn round one of the mole's own foundation stones and had gone round it twice and had set, and under the weed there was a good hand's width of shackle standing upright out of the stone with the pin in it and the pin seized.

"He is not cutting that," Nessa said quietly, from the boat. "He is going to try to drift that pin out with the shackle loaded, because that is the quick way and because it is what he would have done on the sixteenth of September when you were the man on the bottom and he was the man on the watch."

"Can he not do it another way?"

"He can do it another way in four hours with a shackle-setter and a saw and a fire, and we have not got a shackle-setter and we have not got four hours, and he knows both of those, and so do I, and so, in about a minute, will he." She put her hand on the fall. "Marr. He has nineteen minutes on the call. I want you to give him twelve."

"Twelve is not enough to drift a seized pin."

"Twelve is enough to know it is seized and to come up and tell us, and then we have you and me and forty minutes and no sea, and the shackle stands and the harbor pays us for a job with a stone in it. Twelve, Calder. Give him twelve and let him be a man who came up with the answer instead of a man who has to be pulled off the stone."

He gave him twelve.

Bevin went over the side at a quarter to eight into water that was already lifting, and the mole's inner face was ten feet at the ninth seam and eleven under it, and the visibility was a foot and a half, and Calder put the pump's stroke in his ears where he could find it and stopped listening for anything else.

The eleven minutes went. The twelve went at four minutes to eight, and the twelve and a half went, and Calder was on the point of saying the word when the boat came up bodily on a sea and the *Low Lantern*'s master put her helm over to hold the chain's lead off the stone, and the chain came up taut with a sound like a gun, and Bevin Rook came up the ladder nineteen seconds later with a wet canvas pocket in his teeth and a way of holding his left arm across his chest that nobody had taught him.

He got one rung and then two and then stopped.

"Nine," he said. "Nine by the call. Eleven by mine."

"Say the other thing." Calder was on his knees on the wet boards with the fall in his hand.

Bevin took the canvas pocket out of his teeth with his right hand and put it on the thwart and then held his left arm out away from his body, the way a man holds a thing he has been told not to touch, and his face was the color of the inside of a glove.

"The shackle is pinned and the pin is seized and it is under the stone and I could not have got it in a week," he said. "And the chain came up when she came up. My hand was in the bight and I did not get it out."

Nobody said anything for about two seconds, and in the middle of those two seconds Calder's whole body went for the pump and the fall and the boat's way, and then it went for the boy, and he caught himself doing both, which he had never done before in his life.

"Up," he said. "Both of us. Now."

Nessa had the fall before he got the word out. The pump man on the wall had the hose. The *Low Lantern*'s master came round the mole with the boat's bow into the slop and got the ladder away clean, and Bevin Rook came up out of the water and went down on his back on the boards with his arm held out and his eyes open and his chest going, and Calder knelt on him and got the collar open and Nessa came over the thwart and put her knee on his hip and got the hand.

She did not open it. She put two fingers above the wrist, and held them there, and looked at his face, and Bevin Rook said, "That is the fourth and the third," in a voice that was almost conversational, and stopped, because it was the wrong voice for it.

"I know what it is," Nessa said. "Marr. Get the boat in. Off the mole, now, do not wait for me, and take him."

"I am not leaving you on the mole with a boy who cannot lift his arm."

"You are, because you are the only one who can steer and I have both hands, and because if you stay I have to be here and here is where a man drowns in a southwest four." She had the oilcloth over the hand. "Go."

The *Low Lantern*'s master did not need telling twice. He took her round the mole head and out into the middle of the basin with the buoyage gone and the sea breaking white in the road, and the two of them got the boy up onto the thwart and Calder got his coat under his head because the boards were wet, and the boy's right hand found Calder's wrist and held it, and did not let go for eleven minutes, which was the boat's passage across the basin and not a moment longer.

The stove went in on the way up the slip.

Nobody saw it happen and everybody heard it, one flat crack under the counter, and the master's mate came up out of the bilge with his face white and said that there was a stove gone in and water coming, and Calder gave Bevin Rook's shoulder to the man from the fish dock who had come down on the quay because somebody was shouting, and went down into the bilge of another man's boat, and they bailed for fifty minutes, and the stove was a stove.

---

The harbor's clerk came out at two o'clock with Kest behind him and paid eighteen of the twenty-six.

"Eighteen for the part done, and the harbor's share off the top at three, and there is your fifteen." The clerk said it without any pleasure at all. "The shackle is under a foundation stone of this authority's mole and the fouling is on the mole and the fouling is not off, and I have written *work not completed, obstruction of the authority's own masonry* and the authority has read it and signed it, and you will get the same nine on any tide you like and I would tell you that as a service."

Kest did not look at the boat's bilge and did not look at the boy, who was sitting on the mole steps with his arm in a sling of oilskin and his face grey, and he said:

"I am not going to ask him what happened. I am going to write what the tide-gang saw, and the tide-gang saw a chain come tight and a diver come up, and that is the whole of it and it is in my book in the afternoon." He put the book under his arm. "Marr. What did he have his hand in when it came tight."

"He had it in the bight feeling whether the pin was seized. He was doing it right. It was the boat that came up, and it was your mole's own stone he was working against, and the twelve minutes you did not hear about was my doing and it is in my book, and you can read my book."

"I have read your book," Kest said, and went up the steps.

At the yard, the count was done in a closed room with the door shut.

Eighteen from the harbor. Three off for the charter's share. Three for the pump. Two for rope and the day's food. One for the air the pump did not save them. That left nine in the bag, and the bag went on the shelf above the labor log where it had lived in September, and there was nothing in it in September either.

Sella Brigg came at four and stood in the yard door with her hood down and did not come in.

"He turned at ten," Calder said.

"He turned at ten. He was in the right and he turned at ten and he would have turned at half past nine if I had asked him twice." She put her slate on the bench. "My crew's cover is not offered again this winter. It goes to the *Grey Widow* and the rest of them in the ordinary way, and they will be told about the mole's inner face and the boat you had and the boy, and they will do the same as my master, which is the correct thing, and it is still cover gone."

"That is the whole of it?"

"That is the whole of it and the stove is mine, and I have already paid the man who comes and looks at it, and I am telling you the number so that you do not put it in your book and then wonder where the money came from." She looked past him at the mole steps, where the boy was not any more, because the bonesetter on the fish dock had been sent for at three o'clock and had taken him. "There is one more thing and then I will go. He chose your nine when he could have had eleven and he chose it with the hand already caught, and my mate saw him do it. Every crew in this harbor will hear that within a week, and it is worth more to your yard than the nine marks was, and I would like somebody in that shed to write it down before it is a story."

Nessa wrote it down.

*29th. Mole, ninth seam, inner face, southwest four increasing, harbor's own fouling with a shackle under the authority's own foundation stone. Rook on the bottom at the tender's call. Tender's call twelve minutes given, thirteen given, and the chain came tight at thirteen when the master held the lead off the stone. Rook's left hand crushed in the bight, fourth and third, and the hand held out and not opened on the mole. Tender's word not given because the diver came up on the call. Boat stove in on the way up the slip, Brigg's boat and Brigg's cost. Cover of the Low Lantern not offered again this winter by Brigg's own statement. Harbor paid eighteen of twenty-six, obstruction of the authority's own masonry, three off for the harbor's share. Fifteen to the yard, three pump hire, two rope and food, one air. Nine in the bag. Nothing to the chandler. Nothing on the certificate. Rook's wages from this day until the bonesetter says otherwise, and the bonesetter has not said.*

Calder signed left-handed and pushed the pencil across, and Nessa countersigned, and there was no third signature that afternoon because the only man in that yard who signed that book had his arm in a sling of oilskin and was not in the yard.

At the top of the make that evening, standing at the end of the slip with the water coming up the mole's face, Calder heard the delayed note come up out of the bottom off the mole a third of a second behind the tide bell, and it said its three marks and the flick, and this time there was nobody in the yard holding a pencil.

He did not write it down. He had no book in his hand and no clerk and no slate and nobody to read it back, which made it the first thing in eleven days that he had got entirely to himself, and he stood on the hard with the *Kittiwake* behind him and the *Low Lantern*'s stove-in boat in somebody else's slip and understood exactly what that was: a record that existed in one man and could not be checked by any other, and that a man with a bad hour in a harbor full of watchers would be perfectly able to say he had never heard.

He went in and did not say it to anybody, and the pencil stayed on the shelf, and that was the arrangement.
