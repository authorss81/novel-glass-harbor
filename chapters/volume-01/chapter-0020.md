# Chapter 20

*The Leash*

Tuesday the 25th was fair and cold, and the yard opened with a bond to meet and a pump it could not work.

Calder got out the crew's book and laid it on the bench in front of all of them — Nessa, Bevin, Tobias — with Mara standing at the shed door because she had come to witness the iron's entry and would not come further.

He wrote the terms in his own hand, left-handed, slow so it would be fair: *Twenty days' defense from 24th to the 14th of next month. Bond: twelve marks paid, gear held to eighteen. Harbor's eighth off any future raising. No approach within four hundred yards of outer marks. No bell entry without two clean looks and conservator. Slip tides to Free Keel through November. Iron third to Low Lantern when sold fair. Certificate forty marks owed to Venn. Chandler's note eleven weeks into six, six marks lighter. Cradle to spring.*

He read it aloud. The bell in his ears gave every line a shadow a third of a second late, and Bevin said the numbers back after him so he would know he had them right, which was how they worked now.

"Air," he said. "We were a third gone on the fifteenth and we are a third gone now, less a finger's width, because fifteen marks buys fifteen marks and the pot was a hundred marks a third. There is no margin in it. A third of that pot does one morning of somebody else's hull and then the morning is gone, and we cannot take a job we would have taken in September. From today I do not dive the bottom till the burn takes a glove. Bevin takes bottom inside the line under Nessa. I tend. That is not for a week. That is till spring, unless a sitting lifts it."

Bevin stared. "You tend all winter?"

"I tend and I mend and I keep the books fair." Calder put his hand flat on the open page. "I wanted the shoal this month. I wanted a steel on that compartment before the Navy's floats grow over. I am giving that up, because the yard eats before the bearing does. If a tide comes that would let a man measure, I will not take it on this paper, and Nessa holds the shutdown on any bell work and Bevin holds the log on any voice I claim to hear."

"Write that last part down," Nessa said. "Not speak it."

"It is in what I just wrote."

"It is in what you just said. The book gets the other one."

Bevin wrote it, and read it back, and Calder did not change a word of it.

Nessa put her hand beside his. "Say the money."

"The *Merrit* eleven is gone. Four of it to the chandler and seven to air. The buoy's eighteen went four to air, two to the chandler, and twelve into the tin, and the tin went on the table on Monday and the tin is empty. Saturday's twenty-four went three to the harbor's share, three for the harbor's pump, two for rope and our food, twelve to the printer, and the last four to air. The iron stays on the stack till it fetches price, and a third goes to Sella when it does." He looked at the chest the drawing was in, and at the pump standing against the wall with its cracked housing and a sleeve packed with the reeve's tallow. "The pump is in pawn until the 14th and there is no new housing for it before then, which means the next job that wants a pump hires one at three a tide. We work inside the line for day rate and pump hire, and every mark over food goes to the forty owed to Mara and the note."

Mara shook her head. "The office queries my expense. Do not put me first to answer them."

"I put you first because the yard owes it, not to answer them." He wrote it: *Venn forty first after food.* "If the office asks, show them this book. We owe what we owe."

Tobias picked up the whistle and put it down.

"And the cradle?" he said.

"Beach on the hard through winter. Take the wear. Planks in spring if we earn them."

The crew's boat came off the slip at eleven and went up on the hard with her bilge blocked in with the oakum they had left and no cradle under her at all, and the yard could not even be certain she would be in the same water in March. Calder signed the page with his left hand and pushed the pencil to Nessa, who countersigned, and Bevin after her.

Mara stepped forward once, took the iron's entry line, checked the saw kerf note against the stack where the tar was off it, and signed as witness to method, and wrote the time. Then she went to the door.

Calder stopped her with a question he had carried since the press on Saturday.

"Would you have certified the sheet if I had taken your name off the print?"

"No," she said. "That is why you can ask me again."

"And on Monday, in that room, they made it a condition anyway."

"Yes," Mara said. "Which is how you learn the difference between a courtesy and a rule. You should be grateful. Rules can be argued with in a room. Courtesy goes the moment somebody wants it to."

She went. The yard was quiet in the way a yard is quiet when the boat is beached and the pump is gone and there is nothing on the board. Out in the road the *Vigil* lay at the pile with her funnels smoking, and her tender lay a quarter of a mile off the mole on the pale bearing with her floats run, holding the near end of a line that will go out to meet the outer marks a mile and a quarter off — where the officer's paper stops us a mile and a half out, and the pale run lies two and a half miles beyond that, and none of it is water this yard can reach.

Calder picked up the pencil again and added four words under the day's entry, as he had on the 14th, in front of his crew, with a bill attached and no threat received:

*Note entered by Marr. Stated to the crew.*
