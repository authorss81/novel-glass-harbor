# Chapter 45

*A Wider Paper for the Season*

The slate wall of the hearing room was blank when the yard came in, and it was blank because the clerk had taken the cloth off the top left corner at eight o'clock and gone across the whole board with it, and he did that every Monday whether there was a sitting or not, and this Monday there was a sitting and there were two hundred people in the road outside a window that looked at the inside of the fish market roof.

Ors Kest had told the clerk on the Friday that the room would be full by half past ten and that the sitting would open at eleven, and he had told him in the same words he had used on the fourteenth of November, that a reeve who cannot give a man an hour because a market is standing in the road is a reeve who has the wrong idea of what the room is for, and the clerk had put the hour in the book and the market had heard about the hour in about ninety seconds, which is the only thing a hearing room in Morrow Quay has ever done well.

Elias Voke was at the back with his hood down. Neale, foreman of the harbor's tide gang, was at the back with his cap in his hand, and had come because Kest had sent for him, which was a thing Kest had done twice in fifteen years and both times it had cost the office something. The naval officer of the *Vigil* was in his corner with the notebook shut on his knee and his man beside him with a satchel. Mara Venn was against the wall on the same chair as the seventh and the clerk marked her in the attendance column the same way, *Registry, present, not a party*, and she looked at the two words and did not ask him to change them.

---

Kest read the purpose first, all six pages, in the order they were written, and he read it twice, and the second time he read the nine lines of the third part and the six pages of the fourth and fifth parts at half speed, and the room understood that the reeve was being fair.

Then he read the supplement, which was four hundred and six words, in Calder Marr's own left hand, and he read the first three lines and then stopped and read the whole of it again, which he had not done for the purpose.

"And then I am going to do something I have not done in this room in nine years," he said, "which is to rule on a paper before the party has spoken, and the party may object, and if the party objects I will hear it at once and I will rule again."

"Objection," the naval officer said, from the corner.

"Noted," Kest said, and wrote it down, and then went on. "The purpose of the twenty-first of this month is received and is in the record. The supplementary paper of this morning is received and is in the record, and the supplement does not withdraw or alter any part of the purpose and adds one thing to it, which is a request to be put in the water inside the harbor line on a permit, on a bond, on named dates. Both are in the record with the dates on them and neither is a summary of the other.

"And the yard has said, in the third line of the supplement, at ten o'clock this morning and of its own motion, that its purpose of the twenty-first is complete as to what it asks for and is silent as to the ground, that the silence is not a withholding, and that a party which files a purpose in six pages on a Monday and asks for the bottom on a Saturday six days later is a party which ought to be asked about it. So I am asking, in this room, in front of the clerk, and the vessel may hear it.

"Mr Marr. Do you ask to be put in the water."

"Yes."

"With a tender who is not you."

"Yes, and she is named in the supplement and it is Pike, and it is the same woman who has held the cleat since the twenty-fifth of October, and she has never been asked to be asked."

"With a cutoff she can pull without asking anybody."

"Yes, and the word is the word we have used since the thirteenth of October and it is in the record of the seventh and it is not a new word for this room."

"And with a stated reason entered before the diver goes in."

"Yes, and the yard asks that the reason be entered in this office's own book before the diver goes in, and the yard does not ask this office to order it, and the yard asks because the six clauses this office drafted on the thirty-first of October said that and the yard thinks the six clauses were good and it was the countersign that was missing and not the clauses."

Kest wrote for a while. The clerk wrote for a while. Nobody in the room spoke for about a minute, and outside the window the shapes of two hundred people moved about and said nothing at all.

"Entered," Kest said. "And the supplement is received and the silence is entered as a silence and not as a withholding, and the reeve will say the rest of his own words at the end of this morning and not now, because I have a charter to grant and I have never in fifteen years granted one in a morning and I would like to be in a fit state for it."

---

"Grants a one-season provisional working charter," the naval officer said. "On what ground."

"On this office's own ground, inside the harbor line, and bounded by the outer marks and by four hundred yards off them, and it grants nothing upon or within them, which is the vessel's first term granted in the yard's own hand on the market wall on the twenty-third of this month and entered there with the date and the hour on the back of it."

"And the vessel's third term is had, and the vessel's second term is refused, and the vessel's officer asks the room to note that the vessel's first and third terms were answered within thirty-six hours of being asked and its second term within a week, and that a party which answers two of three things in a week and refuses the third has told this room which of the three it is afraid of."

"Enter that," Kest said. "Enter all of it, including the last sentence, and enter the yard's answer, which is in the supplement and which I am going to read in my own voice and not the clerk's, because the reeve is the man who has to defend it in Veyr and not a boy with ink on his cuffs."

He read it standing up with his hands behind his back.

*To the Free Keel yard, of Morrow Quay, a one-season provisional working charter, granted in this room on the twenty-eighth day of November, to run to the last day of March next and to be renewed or not renewed by this office at its pleasure and without any person in this harbor having any right to it. It permits the holder to work any wreck or object upon this office's ground inside the harbor line, and to be put in the water for that work, under a permit, on a bond, on dates named in advance by this office. It is a wider seasonal permission than any instrument this yard has held. It is not the Second Bell license, which is a Crown qualification, which no man in this room has the power to hand out, and which does not lead to one, and which this yard has not asked for and may not obtain by anything done in this room.*

*The harbor's eighth and the harbor's share of any raising follow the holder into this instrument, and the holder has not argued either of them and will not. The holder's own share, being the figure at which the crews of this association have agreed among themselves, is retained and is not reduced by the granting of anything.*

*The undertaking of this office of the twenty-fifth day of October, by which Calder Marr was off the bottom, is lifted, for the term of this charter and no longer, and only on dates named by this office, and only with a stated reason entered in this office's book before the diver goes in, a named tender who is not the diver, a cutoff the tender can pull without asking anybody, and a book that four men in that yard can read the same day it is written.*

*This instrument does not reach the fifth seam, does not lift, narrow, re-serve or alter the notice of the thirty-first of October, and does not reach the third clause of it.*

---

Kest stopped, and put both hands on the table, and did not read the last part for about four seconds.

"And now the price, and I am going to read it in my own hand because I wrote it into my own book on the tenth and it has cost this office nineteen winters of herring dues and four sets of moorings and two berths and one man's boat, and I have had fourteen days to decide who tells that man, and I have decided that a room is the worst possible place to tell a man what a decision has cost him and the only place I am permitted to be, and so I am going to read it and then I am going to read it again to him outside in about ten minutes, and I would like anybody in this room who thinks there is a better place to say so now."

Nobody said anything.

"Mr Clerk, the entry of the tenth of November, the fifth part. Read it."

The clerk read it out, and the room heard it again the way it had heard it on the tenth, and the fifth part said that the ground claimed by this office at the inner face between the third and eleventh seams is claimed pending examination, and that the cost of that claim to this office is the timber fenders, four sets of moorings and the two herring berths, out of service from that month and the first of March, and that the dues of the herring fleet are disputed and are entered as disputed, and the amount is nineteen winters.

"I am narrowing my own claim," Kest said, "on my own motion, in this room, and the narrowing takes effect on the first of December, and it is as follows. The claim of this office on the mole's inner face is reduced from the third seam to the eleventh, to the fifth seam and the fourth and the sixth. Three seams, at four foot of wall each, and the fifth, which is where the thing in the foundation lies, and the two either side of it.

"Consequence, in the order it bites. The timber fenders on that face come back into service on the first of December, all of them, because the fenders were out for the face and not for the seams. Three of the four sets of moorings come back into service on the first of December. The fourth set, on the ninth seam, stays out, and I am going to say why in a moment and I am not going to be helped with it. One of the two herring berths comes back into service on the first of December. The other stays out, and it is the north berth, and it is the one that takes a northeast blow.

"And the dues of nineteen winters remain disputed and are not paid, and I am not going to pretend that a berth coming back into service is nineteen winters of dues, and Mr Clerk will enter the amount and the date and the words *disputed and unpaid* and nothing else."

Neale, at the back, stood up.

"Reeve. The ninth seam."

"No, Mr Neale."

"That is where the boy went under on the twenty-ninth of October."

"It is," Kest said, "and I have given you three of the four back and I am leaving that one out, and I am going to tell you why in my own voice instead of in an order, because a man who has been on that face for forty years is owed the reason and not the notice.

"Every one of those moorings was worked off a ring and a shackle that were bedded in that face, and the fouling came off the ring, and the ring came off the shackle, and the shackle came off the chain. The closure was on the face and the fouling is on the face, and the notice closes the face. I can narrow my claim to three seams and I cannot narrow a fouled shackle bedded in stone, and a crew that wanted that mooring worked would want it worked by men in the water on the authority's work, and the third clause of the notice of the thirty-first stops every man in this harbor who has ever handled transparent material from being in the water on this office's work, and there are three of them and one of them has a hand that cannot close. So the ninth seam's mooring is out until that clause is lifted by the office that wrote it, and I cannot lift it, and neither can I be talked out of saying so."

"So the notice holds the mooring and my chart held it before the notice," Neale said.

"Your chart held it and my chart held it and the notice holds it now, and two of those three are mine and one of them is not, and the one that is not is the one that is doing the work." Kest put his hand down. "That is the price of this charter, and it is a price paid in moorings and berths and not by the yard, and I would like the room to notice that the thing the yard wanted and the thing the town paid for are not the same thing and the town is not being thanked for it by anybody."

---

Elias Voke came down the room on the strength of his name being in the entry of the tenth, and Kest gave him the fortnight's answer standing up, because he had promised a fortnight in this room on this day and a promise with a date on it is the only kind a reeve has.

"I wrote to the coast office on the twenty-second of this month about a hard at Ashtide and I have had an answer, and I am going to read it out because it is four lines and two of them are mine and two of them are theirs, and I have not had it copied because I have not had time and because a man's own letter is a man's own letter.

"It says that the coast office acknowledges the application of the authority of Morrow Quay of the tenth instant, that it notes the authority's statement that the north berth will remain out of service at the first of March, and that before it can consider a hard at Ashtide for the herring fleet it requires a statement of the authority's capacity to moor the fleet, and that the authority is invited to furnish it. The word is *capacity*. It is not *answer* and it is not *refusal*, and a man in an office in Veyr has written four lines to a harbor authority in Morrow Quay and has used the word *capacity*, and I have not got a better instrument and I am not going to insult the coast office by pretending to."

"Which is not an answer," Elias Voke said.

"It is a question with our own words in it, and it is a question that the harbor authority cannot answer honestly while eight of its own moorings are out of service for a notice it did not write, and I have said so in a hearing room and I will say so in a letter, and I have not written the letter yet and I will write it this week, and Mr Voke will have a copy of it and not a summary of it."

Elias Voke stood in the middle of a hearing room in a coat that had nineteen winters of dues in it and said the thing he had come to say, which was four sentences long and which nobody had put him up to.

"Eleven days ago I was in this room and I asked where I was to put my boat on the first of March and I was told the other side of the basin, which is the fishing fleet's, which is ours till April. I have thought about it for eleven days and I have been angry about it four times and I have not been angry about it once today, and I am going to say why. Because the truth is that on the first of March I am going to lie in the basin on the other side with the fishing fleet and I will get wet, and I have got wet on this coast for nineteen winters and I know what wet costs, and a man in an office in Veyr who has never been wet in a northeast blow does not have to be told by a harbor authority to work that out. He has to be told it by a herring skipper, and I am the herring skipper, and if he asks me for a statement of your capacity I will write it him myself, and I will charge him nothing for it, and I will put in it the north berth and the ninth seam and the name of the boy."

Neale said, "Elias," and Elias Voke said, "No," and sat down.

---

The accounts were the last thing and Kest called for them because he called for everything, and Calder Marr read them out of the crew's book in a room with a clerk in it and two hundred people outside the window, standing up, in his own left hand, and Bevin Rook read the figures back after him.

Nothing in the tin. Nineteen marks in the bag, and that is nineteen on the Saturday and nothing has gone into the bag or come out of it since. Air pot a third gone and thirteen marks bought since the twenty-eighth of October. Five marks of air bought in the three days of that job and the last of it on the Saturday. Six marks on the chandler's note in those same three days and none since, and the note still written up in the chandler's own book as eleven weeks into six, and thirteen weeks by the yard's own count, and the chandler had not written a new figure and neither had they. Three marks owing to the copying room for a certified copy of a hearing record, to be paid there on the first, and not one of them paid yet. Forty marks owed to Bevin Rook, sixteen for the reading of the book from the fourteenth of October and twenty-four for the hand from the twenty-ninth, and the reading is a trade and not a wage until the tenth of December and the wages begin at four a week on that date and are not paid. Forty marks owed to Mara Venn, first after food, unpaid since the fifteenth of October, and not thanked and not to be thanked. Twenty marks of iron on a stack with a saw kerf in one crown shackle and a third of the sale entered to another crew who has asked to be paid in February. A pump with a cracked housing and no new one to be had before the spring. A boat on the hard with a bilge blocked in and no cradle under her. November's slip tides given away for rescue cover and December's not yet asked for. Rescue cover offered by nine crews inside the line and withdrawn for the winter by one of them on her own slate.

"That is the whole of what this yard has on the twenty-eighth of November," Calder said, "and the charter is a paper, and the paper is worth nothing in this room today, and the harbor's eighth comes off the top of anything we ever raise, and the harbor's share comes off the top of anything we ever raise, and I have been in this harbor thirty years and I have never had a paper and never had money at the same time and I do not propose to complain about which one I have got."

---

The officer asked for the schedule of named dates at the end, in the flat voice of a man reading a gauge, and Kest gave it to him, and it was three dates and there was a fourth thing at the bottom of it.

"The sixth, the eighth and the tenth of December," Kest said, "at the top of the flood, and the permit for each of them on the morning of it, and the yard will name the reason and the tender and the limit on the morning of it, and Mr Clerk will enter the reason in this office's book before the diver goes in, and I will not read the reason to the room because a reason is entered and not argued.

"And the fourth thing is at the foot of the schedule and the vessel may take it down. The tenth of December is the date at which Bevin Rook goes back in the water, on his own statement and on a bonesetter's and on his mother's, and it is in a hearing record of the fourteenth of November and this office has not lifted it and will not, and the yard asked for the tenth to be one of the three dates and I have put it there, and on the tenth of December the diver on the bottom is Bevin Rook and the tender is Marr and the man on the fall is Pike, and if the three of them come up on the tender's number then the date has been kept and if they do not then I will write down that it was not kept."

"You have put a boy's bonesetter in your schedule," the officer said.

"I have put a date that was in a hearing record in my schedule, and if that is the vessel's objection then the vessel may have it in the same words and I will enter it, and the yard will tell the room why it asked for the tenth and not the sixth."

---

At twenty past one the room emptied into a road where a fish market crowd had been standing in the cold for two hours for no better reason than that it was a Monday that had turned into something else, and at the top of the quay a naval tender that had held a station a quarter of a mile off the mole for forty-four days came in and took up a new one at a cable's length, with her floats in and out and her oars shipped, which is a thing a naval vessel may do in a free harbor and which no man in Morrow Quay has ever seen done, and which nobody stopped, and which was on the quay's own chart at eleven o'clock that morning as a mark belonging to the harbor authority.

And the officer of the *Vigil*, coming off the quay with his notebook shut, said the one thing he said all morning and said it to Calder Marr and not to the room.

"Mr Marr. The vessel notes that the purpose of the twenty-first was supplemented on the morning of this sitting, of the party's own motion, and said so in the supplement before the sitting was half an hour old, and the vessel will read both documents in the same file and will read them in the order they were written, and the vessel does not object and has nothing to object to and I have said so in the room and I am saying it again in the road because a thing said in a room is a thing said in a room.

"And the vessel would like you to understand that the reeve entered your own incompleteness into his own book on Sunday night, which I know because the clerk of this office tells the harbor authority's clerk things at the end of a long Monday, and which is not a thing a reeve does, and that the vessel has been in this harbor for forty-four days and has watched a man refuse three things in one sitting and grant one and narrow one and I would like you to know that the vessel has taken a great deal of notice of your yard and has not formed an opinion about any of it, and that an office with no opinion is an office that is going to want one, and I would rather tell you that in a road than in a room."

"That is fair," Calder said, and had no idea what to do with any part of it.
