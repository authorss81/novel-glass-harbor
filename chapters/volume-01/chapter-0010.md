# Chapter 10

*A Bell Out of Time*

The notice on the fish market wall was nailed up at nine and had a crowd under it by half past, which was normal, and the crowd was not there about salvage.

Kest had written it himself, and the line *public use of the provenance record* sat in the middle of a paragraph with a good deal of room around it, having plainly been written last.

Nessa read it twice. "That's a gag written as a courtesy."

"It's a gag written as a condition of a hearing. And half the fish market has now read the word *provenance* and none of them know what it is, and every one of them will go home and tell two people about a word they do not understand." Calder took his eyes off the water. "He did that on purpose, and I am going to be extremely unpleasant to him about it for the next twelve days."

Across the outer road, at the naval pile where nobody had ever seen a naval vessel lie, a cutter was taking a berth. She was smaller than Calder had expected, and that was what put a cold finger down the back of his neck. He had built her in his head over thirteen years as a wall, and she was a three-masted cutter of maybe two hundred tons with a working bow and two working gun ports and a great deal of new paint, and she took a line off the pile's bollard the way a boat takes a line, without any ceremony at all.

"She's working," Nessa said. "She's putting her pumps in the water and her people ashore and she is doing a job. What she is here for is a thing a man gets from a notice and an officer's word, and not from me, and not today."

---

The inspection at eleven was not a hearing. Kest said so himself, twice, and was insistent about it, because the difference mattered to him in a way Calder had never been able to understand and had begun to.

"This is a limited inspection. I am not certifying the origin of anything. I cannot certify the origin of anything. I am establishing what was done, by whose hands, on which dates, under which instrument, and what came out of the water, and I am going to write it down, and the writing is the only thing that will survive the morning."

He wrote on the reeve's own slate, standing up, which was a thing he had never done before. He checked things. He asked Bevin Rook to read the air column out of the log and then asked him what the air column meant, and when Bevin said it meant how much air the bell had used, Kest wrote *air consumed, stated by the watch* and underlined the second half of it.

He asked Nessa about the doubled hose, the clamp that went, and the boundary line, and she told him all of it in the order it happened without embellishing anything, and when she came to the part where Calder would not let the boat over the pale line, Kest stopped writing.

"Say that again."

"The position goes on the wall with the hour on it, and he would not let the boat go over the line, and that is what I did, and the board is still up in the shed with his figures on it and mine."

"Why?"

"Because it was a straight line on a shoal with a bad bottom and we had no business sailing over it with a boat we cannot replace." She looked at Calder, and did not soften it. "And because he had no proof of what it was and no right to touch it, and he wrote both of those down before we went out."

Kest wrote that down too, and then read the whole of the slate aloud in the yard, in order, the way a man reads a will.

"The chain of labor is good, and I will say so in writing at the next sitting, and the writing will stand, because it is a thing of hours and hands and air and not a thing of belief. You worked the inner edge on two dates under a permit, you raised an anchor and a plate and a fragment, you logged it, measured it, drew it, and did not work one fathom outside the line." He squared the slate. "What I cannot do is make a harbor document outrank a Registry custody log. I have tried and it does not work and I have the file to prove it does not work."

"Then what do I have?"

"Twelve days." He said it flatly, like a price. "A disputed-claim defense entered in the harbor authority's hearing room, holding for twelve days, and in those twelve days nothing may be done on the ground under your permit, or on this ground either, without a sitting. On the thirteenth day it lapses unless the sitting is held, and the sitting will be held, and I have read enough files this year to know what the court will do with it. I am telling you now so that you can be as angry with me in advance as you like."

"And the record."

"The duplicate stays public, on the wall in the market for as long as there is a copy, because it is a record of the objects and not of anything inside them, and no office in Veyr has any power over a thing that is a record of a wreck rather than a piece of a wreck." Kest put one careful hand on the board. "That is the whole of what I can do. It is worth something. It is not a pardon."

"And your notice says I am not to make public use of the provenance record."

"My notice says that pending the inspection, and the inspection is over, and I wrote the notice, and I will write a second one with those words taken out of it." Kest's voice did not change at all. "I am not going to bargain with you, Marr. There is no offer. There is the notice, the notice is wrong now, and I will fix it at four o'clock this afternoon."

He picked up the slate.

"There is one more thing, and it is not mine, and I am telling you so that you cannot say later that I kept it back. At ten-forty a naval officer came ashore off the *Vigil* and lodged a salvage claim with this office, over the whole Gannet Shoal ground, and not a part of it, reaching from the inside shoulder out past the shoal's outer marks and taking in the mooring ground recorded in her own log thirteen years ago."

"And the harbor line?"

"Her claim does not stop at the harbor line, because a naval salvage claim is not bounded by a harbor line, and that is not a thing I have any power to say no to." Kest's hands went behind his back. "Marr, the permit for the inner edge expired at dark last night. Its two dates are used. There is no instrument in Morrow Quay that will let you put a boat on that shoal tomorrow, and if you put one on there yourself I will write the sentence down and I will not enjoy writing it."

"Understood."

"Say it back, because in nine years I am going to want to remember that I told you."

"I will not put a boat on the Gannet Shoal," Calder said, "or a float, or a hook, or a diver, and I have not got a permit and I am not going to get one by asking you for it."

Kest looked at him for slightly too long.

"No," he said. "You are not."

---

He went out on the mole at half past two, because there was a thing he wanted to do that he had written no permit for and did not intend to.

The tide was on the make, and the slop came up the mole's face and stopped and went back, and in the slop you could see things on the bottom that you could not see at any other time, because the surface of it goes to a moving sheet of glare.

He put the soundings in his head. The pale line on the shoal lay about two and a half miles out, on the bearing the yard's drawing gave, running out to the west-southwest.

He could not see it. He was not going to, and he had told them that and he had meant it. But he stood on the mole head with his hands on the rail and listened, and after the tide bell went at the top of the make, he heard it.

A note. Low, and not much of a note, more the shape of one, arriving a hand's width of a second behind the strike that made it.

He had heard it three times in his life: off the mole, under the water at the shoal, and in a bell three fathoms down over a platform. It was the delayed note a keel-line makes when it has carried a sound long enough to give it back late, and it was in the bottom off the mole and the shoal, and it was not in the harbor, because he stood in the harbor and heard it.

And then, in the space under the note, where a boy used to stand with a coil of line in his hands and answer a call out of a fog, a voice came up the half beat and said something.

It was the low short call with the lift at the end of it, three marks and a flick, and it was the one Calder had heard come out of his own mouth on a rotten platform at half past midnight two nights before, and it was in his own head now, in the place where his brother had been for thirteen years.

It answered the note.

He put both hands on the rail until his fingers went white. It could be a sound in the bottom and a boy in his own memory, which would be grief with a rope on it, which is what grief is. It could be a sound in the bottom and a boy in the glass, which would be a route and a witness, and there was no instrument on this quay that could tell the two apart. And it could be the third thing, which was that the aftertaste had taken a shape that matched what he wanted, and that a man listening very hard for a voice in a keel-line would hear his own.

He stood there until the slop went off the face of the mole and the glare went with it, and what was left in his ears was the flat tone Mara Venn had written down in a Registry hand on Monday night, and a third of a second under it, an answer.

He did not write it on the slate. That was the discipline and he kept it, and it was the hardest thing he did that day.

Instead he went down off the mole and back to the yard and got out the crew's book, the one they kept for wages, air, food, injuries, and the day's work, which Nessa had ruled into columns herself with a carpenters' pencil. There was no column for this, so he wrote a heading in the top margin.

*Hearing, 14th. Ringing in both ears, continuous, with a second sound under it that arrives late. Sounds on this quay are hard to place. Cannot reliably tell which sound belongs to the room. Fitted by Nessa Pike and countersigned by Bevin Rook, both of whom can read this and have read it.*

He put the book down in the middle of the bench and put the pencil on top of it, and Bevin Rook read it standing up, twice, and looked at Calder, and did not say a single word about it, which was the most decent thing he had ever done in his life.

---

The boat came in off the slop at four with a man in a cutter's coat standing in her bow with a sealed paper in his hand and no naval pennant showing.

He came up the slip without hurrying and gave the paper to Nessa, because Nessa was standing at the bench, and she read the first line and put it face down.

"That one is for him and not for me, and you are both standing in my yard, so you will both hear it."

"Read it out."

She read it out. The ground of Gannet Shoal was under naval salvage claim; the vessel claiming it was the imperial cutter *Vigil*; the harbor authority's instruments over that ground had lapsed and were of no effect; the Free Keel and its people were not to approach, sound, work, or lie within a stated distance off the outer marks; and any boat found inside that distance would be reported.

"There is no harbor line in that at all," Nessa said. "It is not asking us off our own ground. It is asking us out of water we have never been in."

"Four hundred yards off the outer marks," Calder said. "Half a mile we cannot touch, starting about a mile from the mole, and between here and there is ordinary water on a shoal the harbor's own list has carried for eight years with nobody's name on it."

"It is written down, and it is the only reason I was sent with it instead of a boat's crew. You keep a copy. I have a duplicate. It is a form, and the form is the point." He looked at the yard, and at the iron on the stack, and at the bench with the books on it, and did not appear to be looking for anything in particular. "My captain's instruction was the four hundred yards, and that is what I have said, and I am going back to my boat."

He was gone down the slip in a minute, and the oars were quiet, and the yard was very still.

Then Mara Venn came out of the shed, which she had not left all day except to eat, and she had not eaten.

"I have been in there since eleven reading the custody log's two entries against my own notes, and there is nothing wrong in either line, and that is the most alarming thing I have read this week. Two numbers. Two objects. Two perfectly ordinary lines."

"And the plate?"

"The plate is entered as a transparent hull plate, undetermined origin, the seam as I described it, the aperture as sealed and unopened, and nobody in Veyr has to care about any of it, because the claim window on it closed on the same day as the shard's." She put her book down on the bench. "So. Here is where the Free Keel stands at four o'clock on a day with a naval vessel at the pile."

Nessa had the book. Bevin had the book. Tobias Wren was sitting on the stack with his hat on his knee, because he had come over when he heard about the *Vigil* and had not gone home.

"Go on," Calder said.

Out in the road a tender had come in from the naval pile with a light line over her side, and she was setting floats in a long slow arc a quarter of a mile off the mole, on the bearing of the pale run, well outside the four hundred yards and exactly on the line of it. Two men of the Free Keel stood at the slip head and watched her do it and neither of them said anything, because there was nothing to be said that was not in the officer's paper already.

Tobias got his whistle out and put it away again, which was the thing he did instead of swearing.

"I will say it if you will not," he said, "because you have not said a word to anybody in this yard all day, and I have watched a crew go quiet around a man like that and it does not end well. So here it is, and you may stop me at any point. You have no glass. You have no permit. You have twelve days of a defense that a clerk can end with a stroke of a pen. You have a sheet of paper with a case's plating specification on it and a sheet of paper with a ferry's ninth page of figures on it, and the two have never been in the same room before this week, and there is nobody on this coast who can put them together except the two of us and a woman in a Registry coat. And you have a bearing west-southwest, and there is nothing that way but old ground and Orison, and Orison is a hundred and thirty fathoms down, and nobody here goes there, and every old fool on the quay has a story about it and not one of them is any good."

He put his hat on.

"And you have a bell in your head that you cannot put down and cannot trust. Stand in a boat out there and listen to it and you will hear whatever you came out to hear, and you will come back with a bearing and a date and a story about a boy, and not one office in Veyr will take a diver's word for any of it. And you have a crew, and the crew is what pays the wages, and if you spend the next twelve months chasing a line under the shoal there is no air money and no bench work and no pump repairs, and the *Kittiwake* wants a new cradle before the winter gales and she will not get it, and this yard closes, and you will be a man with a story and no boat."

"I have heard all of it," Calder said.

"Then say what you are going to do, and say it to us, because I am forty years too old to guess at what a man means when he is quiet. I have never once been able to guess at you, and it has cost this yard a great deal."

Calder looked at the four of them. He had not prepared anything, and he had had all afternoon.

He started with the money, because that was the part that was hardest to say and because if he started with the bell they would all hear it as sentiment.

"The air pot is a third gone. The pump housing went on Rask's scow and I ate it. The note at the chandler's is eleven weeks into six and will be fourteen by the end of this month, and I cannot take the *Kittiwake* out of the yard, and I cannot pay Bevin's wages out of salvage I am not going to be allowed to raise for a year." He put his hand flat on the crew's book. "Nessa, the cradle and the new clamp come out of the yard's own account and not out of that air pot, and I will find the money in the morning by doing other people's work, and I am telling you now so that neither of us is surprised at the end of the month."

"That is not what I asked," Nessa said.

"I know. I will come to it." He turned to the book on the bench. "I have written in this book that I have a ringing in my ears and a second sound under it that arrives late, and I cannot tell which sound belongs to the room, and both of you can read it. From tonight there is a rule and it is not mine. Nessa holds the shutdown on any bell entry until two clean days and a look in daylight. And if I begin to describe a sound out there as a voice, Bevin writes the hour and what I said and in what state of the water, and it goes in this book in the same book, and I do not get to see it first."

That got a look from Tobias that Calder would remember for a long time.

"And the last of it," Calder said. "I am going to keep at it. Under a naval claim, on no permit, on no glass, with a bearing and two sheets of paper a clerk could take off the wall this evening, and a bell in my head I cannot tell from a boy I remember. Not because I have proof. Because there is a number on the ninth page of a ferry survey and a plate specification in a public yard sheet, and the only thing standing between those two pieces of paper and an answer is a man with a steel standing on a shoal a mile off this mole. I cannot get him there, and I am not going to stand in this yard and pretend to you that not getting him there is the end of it. If it takes five years and a good hearing and every mark I have, then it takes five years, and I would rather be slow and straight about it than quick and quiet."

He stopped.

"That is the decision," he said. "I am telling you now because in about four days there will be a tide and a Monday and a bell in my head, and I am not going to be a man who says one thing and does another, and I would like it in the book that I said it here first, out loud, in front of you, on this day, with a cutter at the pile."

Nobody said anything for a while. The tide bell went at the top of the water, three strokes, out in the road where it had always hung, and the sound arrived, and under the sound, a third of a second late and much too even to be anything, the note came up out of the bottom of the harbor and said the three marks and the flick.

Nessa Pike's hand went flat on the bench.

"Mr Marr," she said. "Say it in the morning. Not now."

"Yes," Calder said.

He picked up the carpenters' pencil and turned the crew's book round to the heading he had written in the top margin, and read his own entry once more, and added four words under it in his own hand, and put the book back in the middle of the bench where the three of them could reach it.

*Note entered by Marr. Stated to the crew.*
