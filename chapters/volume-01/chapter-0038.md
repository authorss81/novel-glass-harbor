# Chapter 38

*The Reason a Room Can Use*

The reason had to be written because the eighth term had asked for it, and the eighth term had been granted in writing on the seventh of November, and a reason entered before a diver goes in is a thing a reeve can look at in a book in front of a clerk and read out loud, and Calder Marr could not go into a hearing room on the fourteenth of this month and say a reason out loud that he had not written down and had not read to three people first.

He wrote it at the bench from eight o'clock on Sunday the thirteenth and he wrote four drafts and Bevin Rook read all four back and Nessa Pike read two of them and said one of them was a speech.

The first draft was a sentence long and it was true and it was useless.

*The yard has an interest in the ground described in the harbor authority's hearing record of the seventh and eighth of this month, and that interest is recorded in the harbor authority's own chart and in the yard's filed purpose of the twenty-seventh of last month, and the yard has no other interest in it.*

"That is a lie with a date on it," Nessa said. "Not a big lie. A small one, and the kind that is easy to write at eleven at night and hard to read out in a room at eleven in the morning. You have an interest in that ground that came off a ferry, and the rest of this yard knows it, and if you stand up on Monday and read a sentence saying the yard has no other interest in it, then somebody in that room is going to ask what the interest is, and you are going to have to say it, and you will say it badly."

"Then what is the interest."

"That is not mine to say. Write what the yard can show and let the room find the rest of it or not." She put her hand on the slate. "You have a bedded seam in a mole's foundation with three keys on the upper edge and a bolted rim in the lower corner and darker material inside the rim, entered in the reeve's own book, seen and not measured, with three named witnesses. And you have a plate in a custody room with a bolted flange over a sealed aperture, described in a Registry hand with the same four words in it and a chain of custody from a courier with two numbers on it. And you have an ordinary anchor with a saw kerf in the crown shackle that was loaded again, and a fouled ground line of right-hand lay and left-handed dressing made east of the marsh, and a harbor list that names a boat lying on the shoal's inner edge with light ground tackle gone in a westerly.

"Write that. All of it. It is the yard's interest and it is checkable and every word of it can be read by a man in a room. And write at the end what the yard does not know, which is the purpose, and write at the end what the yard is not asking, which is a comparison, because the yard is barred from the custody room and has asked for nothing there."

"And what is left out."

"What is left out is what has been left out since the second of October," Nessa said, "and I am not going to say the word for you and I am not going to write it, and if you want it in a reason in a book then you will have to be the one who types it with your left hand at two in the morning and I will not have read it first."

He wrote the fifth draft, and it was two hundred and forty words long, and every word of it was checkable by a person standing in a room with a rule, and it was the best piece of writing he had done in two months, and when he had finished it he read it out twice to a boy with a sling and a woman holding the tender's cleat and neither of them said anything, and then he sat and looked at it for a while.

"It is thin," he said.

"It is thin at the end," Nessa said. "It is thick everywhere else. A man reading it will know exactly what the yard has and will have no idea at all why the yard wants it, and that is a good reason, because the second thing is the one nobody can use."

---

His father was at the far bench with a gunwale in his hand and a lamp at the end of it, and he had not been in that end of the shed since the ninth of October, and the reason he was in it on the evening of the thirteenth of this month was that Calder had carried a slate over and put it on the bench in front of him and said nothing at all, which is a thing their household had been doing for thirty years.

Alden Marr read the reason twice. He read it the way he read everything, slowly, and he turned the slate round once and read the last paragraph a third time, and the cough came twice while he did it, and he did not stop reading for the cough, which was a thing he had learned to do in about 1958.

"That is well written," he said.

"It is thin at the end."

"It is thin at the end because you have four men who can check every line of it and one fact that you cannot check, and you have written the four and not the one, and a man who is honest in a document is honest about the things he can show and quiet about the things he cannot, and that is not a fault. That is what a document is." He turned the slate round again. "You want to know whether I would help you with the thin part. I am not going to, and I am going to tell you why in one sentence so that it is on the table and neither of us has to spend an evening on it.

"I signed a document thirteen years ago that says what the divers could reach. There is a line in the copy of it in the inquest record about a plate that was cut off her own iron and never came back to the pile. That line is in a report and the report is in a public record and the public record is in a copying room in this city, and there is a copy of it in a shed four streets from where I am sitting, and it has your name on it as well as mine, because you are the witness of method and you signed the thing that you witnessed.

"In about four years there will be a room with a door shut in it, and a man will read the report and he will read your reason, and both of them are documents about the same ferry, and he will ask whether the yard is asking to look at a plate because of a private matter, and he will want an answer, and whatever you have written in the next two months is what he will have. That is the room you are writing for and you know it and I know it and there is no third man in this shed who does not."

"I know what room I am writing for," Calder said.

"Then I have said my one sentence and I am not going to say another, and I would like to know why you have brought it to this bench and not to any other in the harbor."

The cough came and went. Alden Marr put the gunwale down flat, and got up, and did not go out of the shed, which he had not done in eleven years, and got his own stool and his own lamp and a piece of ashwood and a knife, and sat down at the near end of the bench, four feet from his son, and started to fair an edge that did not strictly want fairing, with the knife in his right hand and his left hand behind his back, and worked there in the same room until the stove had been out for an hour.

He did not speak again for two hours. That was all of it, and Calder Marr sat at that bench with a slate and a pencil and a reason that was thin at the end, and understood that his father had done the only thing he had ever been able to do for him, which was to be in the room, and that the room was going to have a door shut on it in four years with two documents in it and a man in a chair, and his father's name would be in one of them and not in the other, and that there was nothing whatever he could do about that tonight and had never been able to do anything about it at all.

---

Mara Venn was at the yard door at half past ten with her book under her arm, because she had seen the lamp lit at both ends of the bench and had come to find out which of the two Marrs needed something, and found out that it was neither of them.

"You have written the reason."

"I have written the reason."

"Then you have written what is left out as well as what is in," Mara said, "and you have not told me what is left out, and I am not going to ask, and I want to say one thing about the asking before either of us is in a room tomorrow, and then I will go home in the cold.

"You are going to ask me to enter a fourth *not stated* against you."

"Yes."

"In the hearing record. Not in my book. In the hearing record, witnessed, read out, entered by the clerk under the reeve's hand, where a naval officer may read it and where a court of claims may read it and where I cannot lift it in four years with a query of my own or without one."

"Yes."

"Not in my book." She said it without any weight at all. "That is the whole difference and it is the only difference and I have been in this business for eight years and I can tell you exactly what it does. The three in my book are in my office and they are mine and they are a matter between me and a man in Veyr who queries my expense. The fourth, in the hearing record, is public. It will be read before the clerk and by the officer of the cutter and by whoever comes after him, and it will be a line in a municipal document that says a Free Keel yard came to this sitting and did not state why it wanted the bottom back, and that is a fact about a yard, and it is not a fact about a man, and I would like to hear you say that you understand which one you are about to hand me."

"I understand."

"Say it back."

"If the line says the yard did not state its reason, then the line is true and it is about a crew and it can be read in a room for thirty years without hurting one man in it, and the reason it can be read without hurting one man in it is that the reason is not in it, and that is the whole of what I want and it is the whole of what I have not got, and I have had four months to find another way of getting it and there is not one."

"That is correct," Mara Venn said, "and I will enter it, and I will enter it in those terms if you will read them to me first, and I am not being thanked, and you are not going to thank me, and we shall both of us say the correct things in the correct order and go home in the cold, as we did on the first of this month."

And then Calder Marr did the thing he had been carrying since the eighth of October, and he did it badly, and it lasted about nine seconds and nobody in the shed heard it except her.

"Mara. There is a mark on it."

She did not move. She stood in the doorway of a working shed at half past ten at night with her book under her arm and she did not move at all, and she waited, and she did not help him, which she had never once done for him in four months and had said she would not.

"It is a sound," Calder said. "It is not on the plate and it is not in the plate and it is not a crack and it is not a maker's mark and it is not a number. It is a thing that comes up out of the bottom of this harbor in a ground line's delay, three marks and a flick, in a call a boy on a quay used to answer me with when we were eleven, and I have known what it is since the fourteenth of October and I have not told you and I have not told my father and I have not told the clerk, and it is the reason I went into that shard on the thirteenth of October, and it is the reason I want the bottom back, and it is the whole of the reason, and I have put two hundred and forty checkable words in that slate instead of it."

The stove was out. Elias Voke's boat creaked once on her warp.

Mara Venn said, "I am going to say three things and I am not going to say them kindly, and then I am going to go home, and the third one is the only one that matters and I am going to give it to you now while you are in a position to do something about it.

"One. If you tell me that on Monday, then the hearing record which is being made out of your figures on the same morning has an unstated reason in it, and the unstated reason is a dead man, and I have three *not stated* entries and a query on my certificate expense and a conservator's second note dated the twenty-second of this month in a circuit officer's drawer, and I would then be a woman who has certified a record and then heard what the record was for. In this office a certificate given by a woman who knows the reason is not a certificate. It is a witness, and it is a witness for a party.

"Two. You have just told me in a shed, at half past ten at night, in front of a stove that is out, in a voice I could not have repeated if I had wanted to, and I am not going to pretend that I did not hear it, and I am not going to tell you that I have not understood it, and I am also not going to write one word of it, anywhere, ever, in any book of mine. That is not a promise to you. That is a thing I am going to do because a person has just handed me the only thing they have and I have spent eight years learning what happens to glass that travels.

"Three, and this is the one that matters, and you may hate it. On the eighth of this month, in the road outside this quay, at a quarter to five in the afternoon, a delayed note came up out of the bottom off this mole under the harbor's own three strokes of the tide bell, and the officer of the *Vigil* heard it, and his man wrote the hour and the state of the water on his own slate, and the officer said aloud in the road that he did not know what it was and neither did you. That note is in a naval file. It is in a naval file with a time on it, on a Sunday, in a road, and it is unattributed, and your own book has a hundred and forty entries in it and four of those entries say *knock, delayed, no source, not written as a voice*, and every one of those four has been written by a man in a yard who cannot tell which bell belongs to the room, and the other hundred and thirty-six are about air and money and fouling and wages.

"That file is going to Veyr. This book is not. And when a man in Veyr puts the two side by side he will not find a dead brother. He will find a Free Keel yard whose every document contains an unexplained sound off this mole, and a diver in that yard who has written in a book, in his own hand, that he cannot tell which sound belongs to the room, and a Registry conservator of the same harbor who has three *not stated* entries against the same man and a fourth coming on Monday, and no instrument anywhere in the Serein Dominion that can say what any of it is.

"That record is false. Not wrong — false, in the way a thing is false when what is absent from it is the only part of it that matters. And it is being made out of the hiding, and it is being made faster than anything you are making, and by Monday it will be in front of a man in a cutter's coat who has already read ten of your documents and will read four hundred more.

"That is what the concealment has cost you, Mr Marr, and it is not your brother and it is not your grief and it is not a bell. It is that you have left a space in the record exactly the size of the thing you did not say, and the sea and the Navy and the Registry have all three come and looked into it, and they have each written down that they do not know what is there, and in four years a man in a chair is going to hold up three documents that do not know, and lay them side by side, and say: a man in Morrow Quay heard something and would not say what."

The shed was very quiet.

"I am going to go home now," Mara Venn said, "and I am going to send you the date of my second note on Monday, which is the twenty-second, and which will be eight days after your defense lapses, and I am going to ask you one question before I go and I am going to ask it because I have earned it and I am going to ask it in this shed and not in a room.

"Do you want me to enter the fourth?"

The bench, the slate, the two hundred and forty words, the ungloved forearm, the boy with a sling at the far end of the bench with a pencil behind his ear and his right hand flat on the cover of a book he could not close.

"No," Calder Marr said. "Enter it. And do not soften it, and do not say the yard declined to state a reason, because the yard did not decline, the yard was not asked by anybody who had the right, and if that goes in the record as a refusal then in four years it reads as a man with something to hide, and it is not. It reads in this record as a yard that was asked nothing and offered nothing and is content to stand there."

"That is a very good sentence and it is not true, and I will enter it exactly as you have said it and you will not tell me which of the two it is," Mara Venn said, "and that is the last thing I am going to say to you in this shed tonight, Mr Marr, and I would like it noticed that I noticed."

She went up the road. The lamp at the far end of the bench went on for another hour, and the two of them did not speak, and at half past twelve Alden Marr put his knife down and said, without looking up, that there was a cup on the bench that had been there since the twenty-ninth of October and that somebody should wash it, and went out into the dark, and that was the end of the family beat and the beginning of nothing whatever.
