# Chapter 5

*Older Than the Registry*

The second working day began with nobody in the yard but Nessa, and by eleven the yard had cost them the day and had earned them nothing at all, and both facts were true at once and stayed true all the way to dark.

Bevin went off at eight with Tobias Wren to a paid slinging job on the fish dock, because Nessa made him go and because Tobias needed a hand who could read a crane load out loud. Two men earning, three at a bench, and a yard that could have been in the water.

They worked at the long bench under the north shed with the plate on a sheet of clean sailcloth, and Mara Venn had moved the lamp twice since nine o'clock to keep it off her own shoulder.

"Three things off it," she said. "One anchor, one shackle with a saw cut in it, and one piece of what you are all going to stop calling a plate. I will write down what came up, what came off, and what was attached, and I will not write down what it means. Go on."

Calder read from the slate in the order he had written it. The bell over at ten past five. The first clamp on at twenty-five past five. The rib settling two inches and three, silt curtain at six o'clock, four minutes of nothing. The second clamp off at five past six. The measurements off the plate before he touched the anchor: four foot two by eleven inches, three fingers at the near edge, two at the middle, eleven keys along the long seam with a fillet running the whole length of the join, and a keel line continuous with no break and no join running west.

Mara wrote it all down without looking up. When he came to the number eleven and the words *no break, no join*, her pen stopped for a moment and then went on at the same pace, and Calder watched her do it and did not know what it meant.

"And the shackle."

"Cut. Bright face, no rust, saw kerf at the point of the wear." He read his own note. "Somebody sawed it nearly through and then it was loaded again. It is a saw mark, not a wire-rope mark, and the harbor's float was still on it when it came up."

"That is a piece of iron with a history and I cannot date it, and the harbor authority will, because the float is theirs and the anchor is now, in law, whatever a person with a permit says it is." She wrote it. "Go on with the plate."

That was the job, and the reason it took until the middle of the afternoon was that the plate had three repair histories and none of them agreed with the others.

Mara found the first within ten minutes. It was the oldest, and it was the join Calder had measured under the water: a keyed seam down the long side with a grey fillet running the length of it, mica and pine resin and a little ash, pressed in with a hot iron and finished flat enough that a thumb could not find it.

"That is old work," she said. "It is a fillet laid in a cold enough fire and a careful enough hand that it has gone the color of wet slate instead of going to powder, which happens when the resin is good and the person knew when to stop heating it. Do you know that fillet?"

Calder did not touch the plate. He put his hand down on the bench on the far side of it, well away from the seam, and left it there like a man holding a rail in bad weather.

"Yes," he said.

"Then tell me what you know and I will write it as your statement and not as mine."

"My father uses it. Free Keel yard, and his father's yard before him, and my grandfather's. It is not a common mixture. It is a Morrow Quay mixture, because the ash comes off the slip timber, and if you do not have slip timber and a good pine you cannot do it, and there are four harbors on this coast where you can."

"Four." She wrote it. "You have just handed me the only fact in this examination that connects that plate to a place rather than to a date, and I am going to write it exactly as you said it and not one word more, and I would like you to notice that I am not asking you why your face did what it did."

"Write it," Calder said.

The second repair was the one that undid him.

It was let into the plate's upper quarter, a patch a hand's breadth square, keyed at four corners with keys cut by hand and filled with the same good grey compound, and the iron of the patch was thinner and bluer than the plate it was let into, and the shape of it was a plate cut out of something else, because one of its edges was a compound curve and no plate in a hull is.

"How old is it?" Calder asked.

Mara had her hand on it and did not move it. "I can give you a ceiling. The mill that rolled that iron closed its furnace two generations before the Serein Accord, and everything it rolled since has come back as scrap. So it was patched before the Accord, and the patch came out of some other vessel, and the two were put together by somebody who did not want to be asked whose." She took her hand off. "That is a very old repair. It is older than the ship this plate was cut from, or near enough that I would not put money on the difference."

Calder did not say anything for a while. Outside the shed the yard's gulls were working the fish dock and somebody was hammering a scuppers' iron four streets away.

"Thirteen years," he said at last. "That is what I wanted it to be. Thirteen years old and done in this harbor, and then the plate would have come past the mole inside a month of the Gannet, and I would have had a chain from a wreck to a mole to a ferry in a hand's breadth of water." He turned his hand over on the bench and looked at the split knuckles. "It is not thirteen years old. It is two hundred."

"That is what the iron says." He straightened up and put both hands on the edge of the bench. "Write it exactly as you said it. That patch is older than the plate's voyage, and it kills the short chain, and I would rather have it written down in your book on a Tuesday than carry it around in my head for a year and build a house on it."

Mara looked at him for a moment. Then she wrote it, and read it back, and he made her read it twice.

The third repair was on the plate's outer face, low down, in the corner where the plate's edge had been ground back and reseated. It was not keyed. It was a run of small stamped studs around a rim, in a metal that was Crown and stamped, with a spacing that any yard on this coast would have set by eye and a Crown shop had set by a jig, and inside the rim the glass was a different color by a shade you could only see with the lamp behind your hand.

"A flange," Calder said. "That is a bolted flange round the edge of a hole."

"That is a bolted flange round the edge of a hole." Mara had gone very still. "That is not a repair, Mr Marr. That is a hatch. Somebody cut this plate and put a rim on it and bolted a lid over the cut, and the glass under the lid is a different batch, and it is not newer. It is older, because it has gone darker the way the rest of it has gone and this has not."

"How old is older?"

"I do not know. I know that the plate was made before the rim was put on it, that the rim was put on in a Crown shop, and that whatever is under the lid has been shut up longer than anything on this coast has a name for."

She turned the plate over.

She did it the way she did everything to it, with two hands and a reason, and the sailcloth went with it and the whole bench got a shadow across it, and the underside of the plate was the underside of a thing that had been made in a hull and had lain against a keel for a very long time: fouled, pitted, with a run of old seam compound along the foot.

And in the middle of that run, low down, clear of the fouling, standing about two inches proud of the surface in a dark grey glass that was harder than the plate around it, there was a mark.

Nine short lines. Nine of them, each one a little under a finger long, and all nine of them joined at a single point, and the point cut deep enough to have been made with a tool and not with a mould.

Mara Venn read the mark twice before she said anything.

"That is not a Registry mark," she said at last. "It is not a cargo seal and it is not a claim seal and it is not a Crown ownership mark, and I have four books in this office that list every mark a Serein office is permitted to strike into glass, and that is not one of them. It predates all of them. The compound under it has gone to powder and the mark itself has not moved, which means it was put there before the plate was ever worked into a hull."

"A builder's mark," Calder said.

"A builder's mark, and not only that." She had the lamp in her hand and was reading the mark with the light coming across the surface, which was the way you read relief. "Look at the pattern and not at the mark. A keel-line does not run through a plate and stop. On anything built to carry a load it runs continuous, and it is a line and not a collection. This is not one line. It is nine lines joined at one point, struck into the middle of a plate that was set into a hull over a keel, which is a mark describing how the glass is tied to something else rather than who owns it. It is the opposite of a claim mark. It says a thing is held together from more than one place."

"More than one place," Calder said.

"And it is older than the Dominion, with the same ceiling I gave the patch iron and no more." She sat down on the stool. "The plate's own construction is older than the Serein Accord. The vessel it came off was built to a pattern I can still read on the underside, with a mark on it that no office in Veyr has authority to interpret, and I will not tell you that anybody in this harbor knows who built it, because I have asked and I have been told no and I believed them."

She put her pen down and put both hands flat on the bench, which Calder had not seen her do before.

"Three things, in my words, not yours. One. I have examined a transparent hull plate of pre-Accord construction, with a keyed keel seam, a pre-Accord patch, a builder's mark of nine joined lines struck into the plating, and a Crown-pattern bolted rim over a sealed aperture. Two. I cannot determine the vessel, the builder, the cargo, or the purpose of the aperture, and I will not guess at any of them, and if anybody in this harbor tells you what it was, they have read it out of a book about somewhere else."

She picked the pen up again.

"Three. That aperture is a chamber and there is something settled in it, and glass does not do that by accident and does not do it while it is dry. I am not going to look inside it and I am not going to permit a person to lift that lid in an open shed, because a chamber with something settled in it, with nothing seated against it and nobody holding a proper line, is how a record gets spoiled by two men being curious on a Tuesday. If that lid is ever opened, it is opened under a bell, with a physical weight held against it, with a stated reason written down first, and with somebody on the surface who can stop it. And I would like to be there, and I would like it to be somebody else's hands inside the bell."

Calder looked at the mark under the plate for a while, at nine short lines joined at a point that had been cut deep by a tool, by somebody, for a reason, in a harbor that no longer had a name in anybody's mouth.

"And nobody consented to anything?" he said.

"Nobody's consent is written on it," Mara said. "I have been telling you all day that a settled mark is not a settled witness, and that is exactly as true of a builder's mark as of a claim seal. I can tell you that somebody stood on a staging and cut nine lines into a plate and meant it to be there. I cannot tell you what they had agreed to, or with whom, or whether anybody who ever saw that ship in the water had any say in it. I will not have you writing down more than that, and I will not have anybody in Veyr writing down more than that."

"Nessa," he said. "Get Tobias off that dock."

---

They built the rig on the bench because that was where the crew was and because a bell is built at a bench.

Tobias came in at four with his coat over his arm and Bevin's slinging pay in a leather bag, stood at the end of the bench, looked at the plate and the cradle Nessa had drawn out and the two coils of line on the floor, and said, "You are going to try a bell entry."

"I am going to sit in a bell three fathoms down over the platform and put a weight against a piece of glass and see whether I can be in the room it came out of."

"Not the plate. It has a bolted rim on it and the Registry woman will not let anybody near the rim."

"That is the correct answer and I did not expect it from you." He came round the bench and put two fingers on the edge of the plate without touching it. "Here is what I have, and it is not much, and I would take it to a woman who writes things down rather than to a man who dives. Thirty-one years ago the cutters at Veyr used to put a man into wreck glass, because the glass had come up once with a note in it and a man came back and told them a thing about a hull that saved them three weeks. And it was not the glass that did it. It was the weight. You went down with a thing in your two hands that belonged to the room, and everything you know afterwards you know in your hands and not in your head. And every man who did it came up having been somewhere, and came up different, and two of them never worked again." He put his hand on the bench. "And every one of them had a tender who was told the time in advance and had a hand on the line, and every one of them was pulled up by that hand and not by his own judgment. That is the whole of what I know. It is thirty-one years old, and it is secondhand, and I would not build a bell on it."

"Then help me build a bell on it," Nessa said. "And tell me where to put the weight, and if you say a number I will not argue with the number, because I have to cut steel tonight and I cannot afford to be wrong twice in one winter."

So they made it. The cradle was a lead block with a seat turned in it, heavy enough that it would not swim, lined with oiled sailcloth, with a hinged clamp over the panel and a hand screw that could be turned from inside a helmet. The fixed air limit was a marked length on the hose, a written limit on the slate, and a number that Nessa said out loud twice so that two people in the world knew it.

The cutoff took the rest of the afternoon. It was not complicated and it could not be elegant. A wire ran from the diver's harness up inside the hose to the tender's hand, and the tender's end was made fast to a cleat on the working deck with a good long turn and a half hitch, and it meant nothing at all until the tender put her weight on it and went over the fall. Then the clip came off the shoulder, the harness let go, and the diver was a man on a line with a man on the surface, which is a survivable condition and is the whole difference between a decision and an accident.

"And the word," Nessa said, standing over the rig.

"Not a knock," she said before he could answer. "A word, out loud, once, and you do not argue with it and you do not ask me whether I have thought about it. If you are in there and something in there is telling you the air is fine and the line is fine and the tender is fine, then a word is the only thing that can come from outside. Anything else could come out of the glass."

"Name it."

"Enough."

"Enough."

"Once. Not twice, because twice is a conversation, and a conversation is something a man can join." She made the half hitch and went over the fall and the wire came taut and the clip came off, and she put it back. "Now you have to be able to say it."

Calder stood in the shed with his hand on the lead block, and said it.

"Six conditions," Mara said, from the bench.

She had a separate sheet, not the Registry book, headed in a nail hand with a place and a date and nothing else.

"One. A named tender on the surface, named, not volunteered, and if she goes home the dive does not happen. Two. An independent line, not the bell's lead. Three. A fixed air limit written before the diver goes in. Four. A physical weight seated against the panel, named, and here it is."

The plate was not the panel. The shard was in a lead bag on the bench beside it, and Calder had not touched it since Wednesday afternoon.

"What is it?" Mara said.

Calder reached into his coat and brought out a thing that was about the size of a fist.

It was a hand bell. Brass, with a hard brown handle gone shiny, and a wire loop through the crown for a lanyard, and a lip that had been filed down on one side where somebody had hit it wrong a long time ago, and a plain iron collar soldered into the shoulder where the handle met the dome. It was a rigger's hand bell, the kind a man rings to call a watch or to tell two hands apart in the dark, and it was worn out rather than broken, and it had been in a coat pocket for thirteen years.

"It is my brother's," he said. "It was his when he was apprenticed. It is a rigger's bell and it is a deck bell and it is not a Royal Navy thing and it is not a ferry thing and I have never seen it in a harbor office in my life. I have carried it since the day after they told me and I have never told a single human being in this harbor that I have got it."

Nessa did not say anything. Mara wrote what he had said and then wrote the line under it, which was the part that mattered, and then she read the whole of it back.

"Anchor: a brass rigger's hand bell. Property of the family, held by the crew. The witness who used it is dead and unlocated. Association with the site of the settled scene is claimed by the holder and not established by any document, and the holder states that he does not know where the bell was at the time of the scene." She looked up. "That last is true."

"That last is true," Calder said, "and it is going to stay written in your book whatever happens to me, and I want you to know that I have thought about that before I handed it to you."

"Five. A cutoff the tender can pull without asking anyone." She wrote it. "Six, and this is mine, because it is the only one of the six that belongs to me. The reason for the entry, stated before the diver goes in. And the reason is not curiosity, and it is not a bell, and it is not a brother." She waited until he looked at her. "The reason is that a piece of glass recovered on harbor ground carries a manifest that has never been publicly reconciled with the carrying space of the vessel it is alleged to have crossed, and this crew cannot afford to go on not knowing whether that manifest is worth anything. If the reason is ever anything else, the entry is not made."

Calder read it. Then he took the slate and wrote under it: *Second date of permit spent at bench. No salvage worked. Permit expired at dark.*

"Write that in your book too," he said. "Not because it helps me. Because the next person who says the Free Keel spent two days on glass should have to read the two lines next to it."

She wrote it.

At eight they carried the cradle and the lead bag and the rig out to the platform and put the bell in the *Kittiwake*'s slip, and Calder put the shard in the seat and clamped it and took the hand screw down until the lead sat against the glass, and the lamp was put where Nessa wanted it, and Bevin was sent up onto the dock wall with a lamp and a whistle because he was not to be on the working deck and had been told so in a voice that ended the discussion.

They were not going to put a diver in it tonight. Nobody had said that out loud except the permit, which had expired at dark.

But Calder put his hand on the outside of the lead bag to lift it the last foot onto the cradle, and the shard was under the lead and the lead was against the glass, and something happened in the bones of his forearm that was not in his ears at all.

It was a pulse. It came up through the lead and through the glass and into the two fingers he had on the bag, and it was not fast and it was not a rhythm and it was not like anything at the mole. It was a beat, and then a wait, and then a beat, and the wait was the shape of a man lifting his hand off a slate and putting it back down.

Calder knew that shape. He had watched it across a table for nineteen years while a boy with quick hands wrote a pressure notation in a bad shorthand, three marks and a number and a flick, over and over, the way a man signs a name he expects to be asked about later.

He took his hand off the bag. Nothing happened. He put it back. The beat came up again, and the wait after it, and the flick at the end of it.

"That is not the object," Mara said. She had not moved and she had not touched him, and she was looking at his hand and not at his face. "Mr Marr, what is happening to your hand."
