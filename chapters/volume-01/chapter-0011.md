# Chapter 11

*The Poor Week*

Saturday came up grey over the west basin with a wind that got under the shed door and lifted the edge of the labor log where Calder had left it to flatten.

He had been at the bench since seven, copying the air figures out fair in his own hand because Bevin's reading had gone up the margin in a hurry on Friday and Kest's clerk would want a clean line to stamp. The numbers did not improve for being fair. One third of the air pot gone on two days that had earned nothing, a pump housing cracked on Rask's work and eaten, a new clamp bought in spring and now on the shoal bottom, a doubled hose run once and split at the coupling where it had dragged the board off the *Kittiwake*'s floor, a forty-mark bond spent on a permit with both dates used, and forty more gone to the circuit office for a certificate with the survey figures on it. Eleven weeks into six on the chandler's note. He wrote the last line and put the pen down and did the sum that was not on the page, which was how many mornings the yard could open its doors before somebody had to be told there were no wages.

Nessa came in with sawdust on her sleeves and stood over the book without touching it.

"You've added it twice," she said.

"I've added it three times. It comes out the same every way."

"Then stop adding it." She took the kettle off the brazier. "The *Kittiwake* wants a cradle before the gales and we have no timber for one. Tobias has gone to ask about a lighter off the fish dock. Bevin is at the market seeing if anybody needs a hand who pays by the day and does not ask about glass."

Calder closed the book. "I'll go to the work board."

The harbor's work board stood under the fish market eaves, a slate wall where masters chalked jobs for the week: hull scrubs, chain work, tow jobs inside the mole, pump hire. By nine the good work was gone. What was left at the bottom in a coasting master's hurried hand was a lighter aground on the inner mud, the *Dove*, needing a diver to clear her discharge and stand by while she was hauled off on the evening tide. Ordinary harbor work under the Morrow Quay charter, inside the line, for a share and a day rate. Calder put his name under it and went to find the master.

The master was a Lowfen man with salt in his beard who looked at Calder's hands and then at his face and shook his head.

"Marr."

"Calder Marr. Free Keel yard. I can go down at noon and have her clear by three."

"I know which Marr." The man wiped chalk off his fingers. "You had the Registry on your deck yesterday and a cutter at the pile this morning. My owners will not have a seizure boat alongside their lighter, and the underwriters will not have a diver with a bell in his ears. Nothing against you. Come back when your paper is clean."

Calder stood at the board a while after the man had gone. He put his hand on the chalk where his name was and did not rub it out, because rubbing it out would have been a second refusal.

At the cooperage he tried again. A sunk butt barge in eight feet off the fish dock, a morning's work with a pump and a patch, twelve marks for the crew and timber found by the owner. The cooper looked past him at the duplicate posted on the market wall, the four sheets in Mara's fair copy with every name on it, and said the owners wanted a crew with no paper on them.

"You have the best paper on the wall," the cooper said, not unkindly. "That is the trouble. Nobody wants to be the second name on it."

He was on his way back to the yard when Sella Brigg found him at the slip head.

She ran the *Low Lantern*, a two-boat crew in the Free Keel, a woman of fifty with a voice that carried over a gale and a book she kept herself. With her was a boy with a slate.

"Marr," she said. "You owe my crew a share and I have come to put it where you cannot lose it."

Calder stopped. "Say it."

"On your dive day Tobias borrowed our lifting strop because your doubled hose had taken the board off the *Kittiwake*'s floor and you had nothing to sling the shoal iron with. He said it in front of my boy, and my boy wrote it, and the Free Keel charter says a borrowed tool earns a share of what it lifts." She held out the slate. "One strop, one tide, one anchor and plate raised with it. A third of the sale of the shoal iron when it sells. That is the paper."

Calder read the slate. Tobias's mark was on it, plain as day, with the hour. The strop had held the anchor on the gunwale coming up. There was no arguing it without calling Tobias a liar in front of a Free Keel crew, and Tobias was not a liar.

"It is good," he said.

"It is good, and I am sorry for the week it lands in." Sella did not soften it and did not sharpen it. "I would not press it, Marr, except my crew has a note of its own and iron is the only thing on your stack anybody will buy. When it sells, a third comes to the *Low Lantern*. I have entered it with the harbor clerk so neither of us can forget."

She went. Calder stood on the slip with the slate in his hand and understood the shape of the week entire: having been right about the glass had made the yard too dear to hire and too poor to refuse a debt, and the iron nobody had wanted since the day it came up out of the mud was now the only thing two crews could argue over.

Bevin came up from the market with his hands empty.

"No day work," he said. "They asked if I was the boy who carried the box. I said I was. They said to come back when the cutter's gone."

Nessa took the slate from Calder and read it and put it on the bench beside the labor log, where the two papers lay side by side and accused nobody.

"We keep the iron on the stack," she said. "We do not sell it under price because we are pressed. And you do not go out alone to find work that does not want us."

Calder looked at the bench, at the saw kerf in the iron's crown shackle where it lay in the yard, bright and unrusted and loaded again after it was cut, and at the duplicate on the market wall with his own name on it in Mara's hand.

"I'll find a job that pays," he said. "Not glass. Work."

Nessa put her hand on the log. "Then we find it together. You do not carry this yard by yourself this month."

He nodded, and the bell in his ears rang a third of a second behind her voice, and he turned his head to place her and got it wrong by half a room.
