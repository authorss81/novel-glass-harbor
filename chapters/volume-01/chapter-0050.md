# Chapter 50

*Slack Water*

They hung it on Sunday the twenty-fifth of December, at the top of the flood, with a shackle out of the harbor's own store and a length of chain the harbor had been paying for since the year of the *Gannet* Cut and a short moor laid in eight and a half feet off the south end, on a ground line of the authority's own, and the clerk came out in a boat with Kest and read the notation off the authority's own chart before anybody touched it, and the notation is in the reeve's own hand and has never been taken off.

*Mole's toe, south end, beyond the eleventh seam. A bellwright's core recovered on the sixth of December at eight and a half feet by the Free Keel yard under a permit of this office and hung on the moor above described on the twenty-fifth of December. Ground claimed by this office. Maintained by the Free Keel yard. Not a mark and not a light and not a danger. A signal, kept by hand, at the cost stated in this office's hearing record of the twenty-eighth of November.*

"Read the last four words again," Nessa Pike said.

"Maintained by the Free Keel yard," the clerk read. "Not a mark and not a light and not a danger. A signal, kept by hand, at the cost stated in this office's hearing record of the twenty-eighth of November."

"That is a reeve putting the cost of a thing on his own chart so that a man in Veyr cannot take a bell off a mole in a good cause without going and reading nineteen winters of herring dues first." Kest put his hand on the rail. "It is the only piece of chart work I have done in nine years that I expect to be read, and I have spent a harbor season on it, and I would like the yard to understand that I did this for the yard and not for the town, and that the town is going to find that out in March."

---

The tide bell went at the top of the water at four minutes past ten on Monday the twenty-sixth of December, three strokes, out in the road where it had always hung, and the slop came up the south end of the mole and stopped and went to a sheet of glare, and from the moored core a hand's width of a second under the third stroke, the answer came up: low, even, and flatter than anything a harbor grows, because nothing on that ground was growing. It was hanging.

Bevin Rook had the book out and did not write for two seconds, which was the shortest he had ever been late.

"Four minutes past ten," he said. "Top of the flood, the harbor's three, the answer out of the core a hand's width of a second under the third, the same second as the sixth, no source, not written as a voice. Eight logged since the twenty-sixth of October, and the eighth of November is the one a naval rating wrote at the same minute as me, and the sixth of December is the first one that has come out of a thing in our own hands."

He read it back twice, and then he wrote a second line under it, and he read that back twice before he would let anybody near the book.

"Knock, delayed, with a call in it. Three marks and a lift. Adult. Not written as a voice. Nobody in this yard has said whose it is and I am not going to ask tonight and neither is he."

Calder Marr did not turn round. He had his hands on the chain and he had had them on the chain for the whole of the answer and for a second and a half afterwards, and the flat note was where it always was, and under it, coming up out of the ground with the ground's own delay, there was a call.

Three marks and a flick. And it was not a boy's. It was a man's — not a young man's, not an old man's, a man's, level and unhurried, the way a man says a thing he has said ten thousand times to somebody he is sure of, and at the end of it, where he could not remember what used to be, there was a lift, and it was somebody's.

It was not his own. He knew his own the way a man knows his own boots in the dark, and this was a man in a boat, further off, saying a thing to somebody who was not answering, and it went out into the water and it did not come back.

He stood with his hands on the chain for a while after the bell had stopped.

"A call," he said.

"That is what Rook wrote."

"Yes."

"Not written as a voice." Bevin Rook had the pencil against the page and did not write. "Marr. I have been the man with this book since the eighth of October and you have been the man who cannot hear which bell belongs to the room since the fourteenth, and there is a call in that ground and I have written it down as a call, and I am not going to write one word about whose it is, and I have thought about it on and off since the eighth of December and I have a view and I am not going to have it tonight."

"Have it on Thursday."

"I will have it on Thursday," Bevin Rook said, "and I will ask you the question you have been waiting four days for somebody to ask, and you are not going to be able to answer it, and I have already worked out what I will write when you cannot, and it is four words on a line, and that is the job, and there is nothing in it that is anybody's."

---

Mara Venn was at the top of the steps with her coat on and her book shut and a printed sheet in her hand that she had certified for nothing, and she had been in that harbor four months and had nothing left.

"I am out of it by the end of the month," she said. "Not out of the office. Out of the other thing. The query of the twenty-first of October comes off my file on the fourth of January because a conservator with nothing left to refuse is a conservator who is expensive to employ, and after the fourth I am a woman who examines material and settles provenance marks and decides whether a piece of glass is physically fit to be moved or handled by a person, and that is a trade, and I have been doing it for eight years and I have never once minded it and I mind it now, and I have written that down and I have not entered it anywhere."

"I am not going to say thank you."

"You are not going to say thank you and I would have come to the mole to say something if you had." She looked at the chain and the core and the water. "Mr Marr, I am going to ask you one question and I am going to ask it in the open air because a thing said in a shed at half past ten at night gets written down by a man in four years as a thing that was said in a shed, and this has been said in a shed once and I will not have it said twice.

"On the eighth of December, in about eleven minutes, something came out of your mouth in a call, and I was thirty feet away at the far end of that platform with my hands on a mooring post, and I heard it, and I have not written one word of it anywhere ever, and I will not, and that is not a promise to you. That is a thing I did on purpose." She turned the printed sheet over and held it by the corner. "And in the entry that boy read out this morning, in a municipal ledger with a clerk's initials on it, there is a boy's fifteen seconds and no name and no sound and no lift, and there is a line in it in the conservator's own hand saying that the holder of the anchor states that he does not know where the object was at the time of the scene.

"That line is true today. I have watched you four days to find out whether it is going to stay true, and it is going to stay true, and I want to say one thing about it and then I am going home in the cold.

"A record that says a boy was there and does not say who he was is the only honest form of that page that exists, and it exists because the man who made it cannot bear to write his brother's name in an office in a city where a seal with a senior examiner's name under it is a thing that can be read by a man he will never meet. And that is not a fault. And it is not free. And there is no version of this in which you have both, and I have been eight years in a trade where nobody believes that, and I have believed it since the fourteenth of October at four in the morning in a shed, and I would like it recorded that I believed it and did not say so."

"Then say it now."

"No," Mara Venn said. "Because then it is a thing I said, and a thing I said is a thing I am a witness to, and I have been four *not stated* deep in a file already and on the fourth of January I am going to be a woman with no standing and one witness fewer. I would like to be the only person in Morrow Quay who knows what is in that anchor and who has never been asked to say it, and I would like that to be because I was good at my work and not because I was loud."

She went up the road, and she did not say thank you either, and the two of them stood on a mole head and watched a woman walk away up a hill in the cold and neither of them said one word about it for about a minute.

---

At half past eleven Calder Marr went into the shed and took down the slate from its own shelf, four feet from the crew's book, where it had stood since the fourteenth of October with its heading and its six paragraphs and its one false line.

*Account of the evidence heard by Calder Marr, unverified, from himself, and not corroborated by any document, witness, instrument, or second person.*

And the false line, which he had written in a hand that did not change, and which said that the personal association had been confirmed by the diver and not disclosed.

He stood in front of the shelf with the slate in his two hands for about a minute and a half.

It was two words. *Confirmed and disclosed* would make the line say what it now was, which was that a piece of transparent material recovered off this harbor's own ground is held by the diver to be the first minutes of a particular dead man, and that nothing in the world can prove it, and that it is written down in exactly three places, and that in all three it is written down as a thing the holder claims. In the ledger there is a boy's fifteen seconds and no name. In the crew's book there is a slate that says the same and a line a woman wrote underneath it saying she will not be asked. On a Registry conservator's certificate of method there are four *not stated* entries and a hand that declined to fill in the fifth. There is nowhere else it is written. There is nowhere else it could be.

Two words. And the slate would then be true, and the slate stood on its own shelf with its own heading, and a true slate with a boy's name in it is a document in a yard that any man may read, and there is no office in the Serein Dominion that could not find this yard with it in three weeks.

He put it back on the shelf, on its own shelf, four feet from the book, with the heading and the six paragraphs and the one false line, and it stood there and gained nothing in seventy-five days, and would go on standing there.

---

Kest came down at one o'clock on his own and did not go up to the office, and stood at the bottom of the steps where the chart of the inner ground is kept in a tin under a tarpaulin in a locked box that the clerk opens with a key the clerk owns.

"Two things and then I will let you get your work done," he said.

"The first is the charter." He had the chart rolled under his arm and did not unroll it. "A one-season provisional working charter is not a permission to work whenever the holder feels like it. It may be suspended by this office, at any time, after an inspection, for cause, and I am telling you that on the twenty-sixth of December in the open air, six days before the New Year, because a man who finds it out in the second week of January will think I hid it, and I have spent this month spending authority I did not plan to spend and I am not going to spend any of it by being the office that kept a thing back.

"I am not going to suspend it. I want that said too, in the same breath, because a notice with only a threat in it is a notice written for somebody else's file. I have no cause. You have a reason entered in my book before every bottom and a tender who is not you and a cutoff and a log four men can read, and you have turned up on every date I have named and you came up on the tender's number on every one of them, and the tenth of December is in my book and the boy went in the water on it and I am going to enter that he did. So it stands. And it can be stopped by me, and if that ever happens you will be told in a room, and not by a piece of paper on a market wall."

"And the second."

"The second is the nineteenth of January." He put the chart down on the step and unrolled about a foot of it and put his finger on the mole's toe at the third and fourth seams. "You want the nineteenth. I have had the notice in front of me since Monday and I have known since Wednesday what you would ask for and I have had four days to decide whether to name it before you asked or after, and I have decided after, because a reeve who names a man's date before he asks for it is a reeve who is managing him, and I have spent this year managing a great many people and I would like one transaction in it to be clean.

"So: the nineteenth of January, the top of the flood, the mole's toe, the bedded seam in my foundation running north, a lead and a slate and a bell hung, and no diver unless the reason is in my book at eight o'clock in the morning and Pike is on the cleat. And I will say why I have named it, and then I will not say anything else about it for a year.

"The first point of the three is not a point. It is a bedded seam of about forty feet in my mole's own foundation, three keys, and you have been using its south end as a position since the twenty-eighth of October because the boat lay there. And on Monday the twenty-sixth of December at four minutes past ten a bell hanging eighty feet from that south end was struck, by this harbor's own tide bell, and answered through the ground a hand's width of a second late, and Bevin Rook wrote the second. That is a figure nobody can take off you and nobody can take off me, and it is the first figure in four months that this office has got out of that mole on its own account, and it came out of a bell we have hung for nothing on ground we own because a boy read a book out loud to a tide gang on a Saturday.

"And the run does not stop at the mole. It does not stop at the shoal. It is under the town. And this office has no chart of what is under the town because the town was built on it and nobody has ever wanted one, and if you go out on the nineteenth of January with a lead and a bell and you find that it goes in under a cooperage and comes out under a chandler's shop, then you will have a route inside a mole, and there is no instrument in this harbor or in Veyr that will tell you what a route is, and no forum in either of them that will rule on one, and you will be standing in this office's hearing room in about a year asking for a paper to do a thing that no paper has ever been written for."

He rolled the chart up.

"A leash, Marr. A longer one than the leash you had, and I put it on you and I would put it on you again, and the thing you get out of it is the January, and the January is worth having."

"Yes," Calder Marr said. "The nineteenth of January is worth having."

---

There was slack water on the mole face on the twenty-sixth of December a little after four in the afternoon, and the tide that had been going off since before noon stood still for the best part of ten minutes, and then began to come back, and the harbor lay flat and grey with the last of the year's light in it. The first of March was nine weeks and two days off and was a Wednesday. This was a Monday. The yard's whole autumn had been a fight with a day of the week. Out in the road a quarter of a mile off the mole a naval tender lay at a cable's length with her floats run and her oars shipped and seventy-two days on station, and at the naval pile a second cutter's boat that had not been in Morrow Quay in October was lying with her oars up and nobody in the town had been told why.

On the market wall there were six things and the top one was a naval claim and under it, slipped four inches so that a man had to lift a corner, were three sheets of three points with a difference of ten yards withdrawn off them and a fresh sheet saying so, and a notice of four clauses with a circuit's seal over its nail, and the public record of two objects in a conservator's fair copy with every name that had touched either of them on it, Marr and Rook and Pike and Wren and Mott and Venn, and two crew copies of the same thing printed without her name, and at the bottom, under a rule, the fifth-seam sheet saying a thing had been seen in eleven feet and had not been measured and had not been touched and was under a bar.

The clerk's slate wall in the hearing room had been wiped at eight that morning and was blank and would be wiped again on the fourth of January.

In the freezermen's shed on the fish dock, a shed, a bench, a book, a chest of drawings and a stack of iron worth twenty marks with a third of the sale entered to another crew who had asked for February, and five marks in a bag and nothing in a tin, and a chandler's note standing in the chandler's own book as eleven weeks into six because nobody had asked him to write a new figure, and a pump with a cracked housing, and a *Kittiwake* on the hard with her bilge blocked in and no cradle under her, and a boy of twenty-two with forty marks claimed and none of it paid, and a woman with a tender's cleat who had taken the word *wages* out of the yard's book on Wednesday in her own hand, and a man of sixty-one at the far bench of the same yard who had not spoken to anybody since the thirteenth of November and had a cough that came on a forty-minute cycle and had not been asked for anything.

The fragment was in the custody room in the west of this city, in a padded box on a shelf, entered as one piece of transparent mineral, worked, in two portions keyed at a join, of undetermined origin, not compared by this office with any other item, and a municipal claim was entered against it in a small square hand, and the yard was barred from the room by the second clause of a notice that was correct, and had asked for nothing in there, and would not withdraw the claim.

And on the twenty-sixth of December, at four minutes past ten in the morning, at the top of the flood, a bell hanging on a moored ground line eighty feet off the south end of a town's own mole answered this harbor's tide bell a hand's width of a second late, and said three marks and a flick with a lift on the end of it, in a grown man's voice, out of ground that had been there before the mole and would be there after it, and no man in Morrow Quay and no man in Veyr and no woman in a Registry coat with four *not stated* entries against one of them could say whose it was, and it was not a boy's, and it was not his, and the boy with the book wrote it down as a call and not as a voice and read it back twice.

Calder Marr put his hand on the chain and left it there, and the going out in the weather was the whole of the craft, and he had signed up for that and for nothing else, and the first of March was nine weeks and two days off, and the second herring berth and the mooring on the ninth seam were still out of service, and nineteen winters of dues were disputed and unpaid, and the notice was on the wall, and the bell had a fouled waist and a tongue half worn through and a year's rope on it before somebody came and gave it a new one, and the charter was a paper and not a license and could be stopped by one man with a key and a book, and the reason for the seizure of the fourteenth of October was on no paper he had ever been handed.

He took his hand off the chain and went up the steps to the shed to write down the hour, because that was the job, and the book was on the bench in the middle of it with the pencil on top of it, and the boy of twenty-two put it in front of him without being asked, and Calder Marr read the whole of the year's work back out loud, in his own left hand, from the eleventh of October to the twenty-sixth of December, and Bevin Rook read it back after him, and Nessa Pike countersigned, and the three of them signed the year out in a shed with the stove going, and outside the tide was making on the mole face and the bell was answering.
