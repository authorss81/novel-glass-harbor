# Chapter 47

*Three Foot Nine*

The piece of oak came up on the fall with the core on the sixth and nobody wrote it down, because it was a board and they were lifting a bell, and it lay against the bulwark of the barge all morning with the rain on it and Bevin Rook did not once look at it, and on the Monday morning he brought it into the shed in both arms and put it on the bench and said the thing he had come to say.

"It is a stern board," he said. "It is oak, and it is six foot long with about two foot of it left, and there is a rabbet cut in the back of it where a moulding went, and there are three letters under the fouling at the broken end and one of them is most of a letter. I have had it since Saturday and I have not written it in the book and I am writing it in the book now and I am going to read it back, and then you are going to tell me what it is for, because you know and I have known since Saturday and I have been sitting on it for two days because I could not work out which one of us was carrying it."

He wrote it in. *Sixth, December. A piece of oak, six foot with about two foot of it remaining, rabbetted on the back, three letters under the fouling at the broken end, one of them most of a letter. Came up on the fall with the core and not written down on the day. Entered on the seventh by R. Rook, who had it since the sixth and did not know what it was for.*

Then he read the letters out.

"*A* and *s* and the most of a *t*," he said. "*Aster*."

---

The fragment came up at two o'clock on the eighth, in the same haul as the grating, and it was not a lift and nobody on the south end of the mole treated it as one for about four seconds.

It was a hand's breadth and a half of worked glass, in two pieces still keyed into one another at a seam, and it came off the plate's outboard edge below the aperture on the seaward side, out of the fouling, with the fall on it and the hook through the fouled weed and nothing done to it at all. It was clear. It was three fingers thick at the fat edge and thinner at the broken end, and along its lower cut face a band about the width of a thumb had been ground flat the whole length of the break with the worked lines running down into the band and stopping dead at its edge, and the band was not straight, it curved the way a band curves when the thing it was cut out of was round.

"That is a ground band," Mara Venn said, from the bank, before anybody had got the words out.

"That is a ground band," Calder Marr agreed, "and I am going to write down four things about it and I am going to write the fourth one first, because it is the one that matters and it is the one I am not allowed to be clever about.

"The yard has in the custody room of the Morrow circuit office, under one of two numbers, an object whose lower cut face carries a band ground flat the whole length of it and curved. The yard is not the custodian of anything in that room and is not entered as a custodian, and is kept out of the handling of it by the second clause of a notice of the thirty-first of October, and has asked for nothing in that room and asks for nothing today. The yard has not compared these two objects. No person in this harbor has compared these two objects. The yard enters the *form* of the band on this object as a measurement and states under it, in the conservator's own words of the second of October, *purpose not stated*, and the yard will not draw a line between this object and any other object, and the office of the circuit is the office that may draw it, and is not asked to today."

"Enter it exactly in those words," Mara Venn said. "And I will countersign the words and no others and I will not write one word about what either band is for, and I want it on the record that I have said that in advance and not afterward."

---

The grating came up with it and the grating was the whole of the morning's work and the whole of what four months had been for.

It was an iron frame of bars in a rebate, three foot four by two foot two, and it lifted out of its seat, which is the first thing a grating is for, and it came up with a stowage board in it that was not on the survey and a broken hasp on one corner with the stem still in the iron and the flat of it gone.

And Calder Marr, in eight and a half feet of water with his hand on the plate and the fall on his shoulder, took out a steel rule that was the only instrument in Morrow Quay that anybody owned for the purpose, and measured the clear opening inside the coaming.

He came up with it and he wrote it down himself and he read the figures back to Bevin Rook twice before he would let the boy write them, and then the boy read the whole of it back again.

*Eighth of December, permit of this date, top of the flood, south end beyond the eleventh seam, in about nine feet. Clear opening inside the coaming, measured with a steel rule in the hand at the plate: three foot nine by two foot four. Outside of the coaming, four foot by three foot, measured on the twenty-seventh from a boat. The grating is out of its seat and was in its seat, and the rebate is worn bright on the inboard side only. The grate has a stowage board in it that is not on the survey. The hasp on the north corner is broken at the stem. The survey's ninth page gives number four as eight foot four by six foot nine clear and eleven feet two deep under the grating, and gives the grating in place, and the survey is the ferry's own and was made before she sailed.*

"Read the last three lines of the previous entry back," Nessa Pike said, "and then read those back, and then say the one thing that both of them say, and I want to hear you say it, Bevin, because you are the man who reads this book and if a clerk in Veyr ever asks a question about it he will ask the man who reads it."

Bevin Rook read both entries back and then stood in the middle of a working shed on a Thursday in December and said it slowly, twice, in the flat voice, and it was the plainest thing anybody in that yard had ever said in a room.

"A room eight foot four by six foot nine, with a grating in it, and a hatch in the deck three foot nine by two foot four, and a sealed case seven to eight feet in its longest face. And the case cannot go through the hatch, and if the case is in the room then the case got into the room by the hatch, and it cannot, and so it is not in the room. And a thing that was never in the room cannot have been taken out of the room, and so the room cannot have been emptied, and so a paper that says the room stood empty is not describing the room at that moment whatever else is true, and the yard has wanted for four months to prove the room was full, and what the yard has proved is that the room cannot have been cleared, and a room that cannot be cleared is a room the manifest is not describing at the moment it says the room stood empty, and that is worth more to anybody in Veyr than the thing the yard wanted, because the thing the yard wanted was a sentence and this is a figure."

---

The reason went into the harbor authority's book at eight o'clock in the morning, in the reeve's own barge, with the clerk present and the stove going, and Calder Marr wrote it in his own left hand and Kest read it back and did not argue with a word of it.

*Eighth of December. Reason for an entry, entered before the diver goes in. That this crew has established by measurement at a plate on this office's ground inside the harbor line that the standing space the public manifest describes as standing empty was entered by an opening through which nothing of the sealed case's described size could pass, and that the manifest is therefore unreliable as a description of that space at the moment of the seizure, and that an object of recovered transparent material has been brought up from the same plate carrying a settled witness, and that the yard holds one prior account of the loading space of the seizure, made by a man of the vessel, from one position, limited and contestable, and that a second account from a second position, with its own limits stated, is what turns a contradiction available to any person holding four documents into a comparison anybody may make. The yard offers no view on what was in the space and asks for none. The yard asks for nothing in the custody room. The yard states that it does not know who the witness in the second account is, has not entered a name, and will not be told.*

Kest read it twice and put his own pen down.

"Enter it," he said, "and enter that the office has read it twice and has not edited it, and enter the hour, and Mr Clerk, do not summarise it, because the whole of the value of a reason is that it is a man's own words and a clerk's summary of a man's own words is a different document with the same name on it."

---

The rig went in at the top of the flood in three fathoms over the platform inside the mole, because a working yard with a boy on a sling could not take a bell entry on the mole's face at eight and a half feet with a swell coming round the corner, and Nessa Pike said so out loud and was right, and the reeve's permit for the eighth named the platform and not the mole, and the reason for the platform was entered under it and read out at the slip, and nobody in that yard had to be told twice why.

Nessa Pike read the six conditions out loud, in order, with the book open on the boards and Bevin Rook holding the lamp, and Calder Marr said them with her, because that was the arrangement and had been since the thirteenth of October.

"One. Named tender, and she is Pike, and she has not gone up the slip, and if she goes up the slip this is over. Two. Independent line, and it is not the bell's lead, and it comes up to the cleat on the boards and not to her. Three. Fixed air limit, forty minutes, on this slate, in the hand of Marr and the hand of Rook, and there are three other people in this harbor who know it and it is not forty-five."

He put his hand on the lead block in the seat.

"Four. The weight is seated and it is named, and it is a piece of the stern board of the vessel this room was on, six foot with two foot of it left, rabbetted on the back, three letters under the fouling, taken out of eight and a half feet of water off this office's own ground on the sixth of this month by this crew, and it is not touching the glass, and I will not let it touch the glass until the glass has taken hold of it, and that is the whole of the difference between a room and a stone."

"And why that and not the bell," Mara Venn said from the boards, in the flat voice, with her book shut on her knee.

"Because the bell is my brother's and I will not put it in a public record on the eighth of December, and because if I put it in there then a clerk in Veyr has my family in a file, and because the man whose voice is in this piece of glass stood on that deck when it was new, and the ship was on the deck too, and the ship is the only thing in the world that was in the room and is not a person."

"Write that last sentence down," Nessa Pike said.

"Write it as my words and not as hers," Calder said, "and read it back, and if there is anything in it she has not agreed to then I will take it out."

Mara Venn opened the book and wrote nine lines, which were the anchor described as a physical object with its dimensions and its letters and the place and the depth it was taken from, and under them, in the same hand, two more lines which were that the holder of the anchor states that the association of the witness in this object with the scene of the object is claimed by the holder and not established by any document, instrument or second person, and that the holder states that he does not know where the object was at the time of the scene, and that the conservator has written the holder's statement and not tested it and will not be asked to.

"Read that back to me," she said, "and then read it to him, and then I am going to the far end of the platform and I am not coming back to this end of it until the line is off, and you are not to speak to me while you are in there, and I will not answer you if you do."

---

The glass began to tick at six minutes, in a group and a gap and a group, and the gap was a length of time a man could measure against his own breathing, and he drew the air out of the bell by degrees, and the iron of the seat got louder than the water, and then the water got louder than the iron, and then there was no order between them.

He went in.

The first thing was the cold with a floor in it. He was low, on the deck, with a case high on his right shoulder and his right hand under the near end of it, and the weight went away from him and back again, and he knew without being told that there were four men on it and that he was the smallest and that he was on the near end.

The second thing was that the man with the paper was behind him.

He turned his head. That was the whole of the difference between this and the account the yard already held, and it was the whole of the difference between this and everything else. The man with the paper was behind and above and to the left, and he turned his head and got the underside of the paper and the man's mouth and the man's chin and no letters at all, because a paper held the wrong way up is a shape and not a document.

The numbers came along and away at the same time, the way a note goes along a line. He could hear that numbers were being read. He could hear the shape of a phrase that had one word in it twice and the word was *empty*, and it was read twice, and between the two readings the case went past the man's knees and the man did not look down.

He counted the men on the case. Four, and he was one of them, and the count was right, and he remembered being pleased about that for about two seconds.

There was a tag on the line at his feet where it lay along the deck on the outboard side, a lead tag with a number stamped into it, and he had the first two figures and lost the third because his shoulder had gone from under the corner and the weight had come onto his neck.

Then somebody put a hand on the collar at the back of it, and the hand was not rough and not gentle, and it was a hand that knew exactly how much of a deck apprentice there was and how much of a man carrying a box, and it moved him, and he went, and he went looking at the man with the paper.

That was the whole of the choice and it took about a second and a half, and it was the only one there was, and Calder Marr had wanted for four months to have a brother who would have kept his eyes on the case, and his brother had a choice available to him at that moment and he did not take it, because a deck apprentice with a numb shoulder and a tag he could not read is a boy, and a boy looks at the man who is reading, and the man who is reading turns the paper over.

The frame tore. It came across the scene the way a wet thumb comes across a page, and it was doubled and one of the two was brighter, and there was a hard rim on the bright one, and the pain of it arrived in his teeth and in the two fingers he had on the glass.

He took his hand off it.

The air was warm and smelled of tar and the fish dock and nothing else at all, and Nessa Pike's face was in the hatch upside down, and she had not said anything and was not going to.

"Eleven minutes," she said. "You were in there eleven minutes and you had forty written on a slate and two other men in this harbor know the number, and you are going to say out loud that you have no argument."

"I have no argument."

"Say the rest of it."

"I am coming up and I am not going to be one of the men who argues."

---

It went up the ladder with his hands on the rungs, and on the boards, sitting down, with the lamp on his knees, he said it.

It came out of him low and short with a lift at the end of it, three marks and a flick, in the exact shape of a thing he had last heard on a quay in Morrow Quay when he was eleven years old from a boy who was going to be a licensed diver in a year.

Nessa Pike went white.

She did not say anything. She sat down on the edge of the bell with her hands on her knees and looked at him for about two seconds, and then she got up off the bell, went to the far end of the platform, and put both hands on the mooring post and held on to it with her back to him, and she stayed there for a long time.

Bevin Rook had the book open and the pencil against the page.

"Marr," he said, in the flat voice, with nothing in it at all, "I have got the hour and the state of the water and the limit and the name of the tender, and I have got the line about the frame, and I have got the line about the band, and I have got all of it. I am not going to write a line about what came out of your mouth, and I am not going to ask you what it was, and I am going to say that out loud in the yard tonight so that in about a year there is a person in this shed who knows that a man in this yard had something come out of him in December and did not write it down and was not asked.

"And I am going to write one line, and I read it back before I put my name under it, and it is the same line as the eighth of November and the fourth of last month and every other one since the twenty-sixth of October." He wrote it. "Knock, delayed, in the bottom off the mole. No source. Not written as a voice."

"That is not what came out of me."

"No," Bevin Rook said. "It is not."
