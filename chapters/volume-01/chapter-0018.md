# Chapter 18

*What a Record Serves*

Saturday the 22nd was a market day and the wind had gone round again, and the crew was at the harbor's work board at six because a market day is when the board has anything on it.

What the board had was a Lowfen lighter with her name painted over and her hawser parted, aground on the inner mud where a master in the dark had put too much boat in too little water. The owner wanted her off before the flood made, and he wanted a pump on her hold and a hand on the bottom, and he had already been told by two crews what the bottom would cost him.

"Twenty-four on the board," Bevin said, reading it twice. "Timber found by the owner. Harbor's own share off the top and the rest ours."

"He does not want a diver with a Registry behind him," Calder said.

"He did not ask that either. He asked who was coming and how soon."

Calder put his name under it at ten past six, which was the first name he had put under anything since the gale, and they were rigged on the inner mud by half past.

Their own pump would not have served and they all three knew it, so they hired the harbor's by the tide, three marks, which came out of the twenty-four at the front because that is how a hire works. The ground was soft and the mud had the boat's lee in it, and the first thing they found was that the parted hawser had taken a bight of the mud with it and there was a second bight under the bilge that nobody had known about. Nessa sent Bevin down on the bottom with the tender's cleat in Calder's left hand to look at it, and Calder held the line and gave the time and took the time from the water, and the water was not making yet.

"Six minutes by my slate," Bevin said at the ladder. "You called six and a half."

"Six and a half goes in the book."

"I was under seven."

"Then you were under seven, and the log says six and a half, and next tide you will be under six and a half." Calder held the line while he climbed. "That is the arrangement and it is not going to be argued back into nothing by a man who felt ready."

They pumped her, patched the hawser, shifted three hundredweight of mud out of the bight in baskets that weighed more than the mud, and got her off the ground on the last of the flood with her owner shouting from the mud he had been standing in for two hours, which is the ordinary end of an ordinary job and the reason men keep doing it. The harbor's clerk took the charter's share off the top, three marks, without asking anybody, and the owner paid the rest in the road because that is also how it is done. Twenty-four on the board, three to the harbor, three for the pump, two for rope and the day's food, and sixteen in the bag.

"Sixteen," Nessa said, on the road, with the bag. "That is more money in one bag than this yard has held since the seizure, and it is a lighter with a cut hawser."

Bevin came into the shed at four with a printer's proof under his arm, ink on his nose, and a plan.

"The print shop on Linen Street will copy the duplicate for two marks a copy," he said. "Fifty copies coast-wide. I asked the price before we went out and I have been back since the flood to be sure of it. Bearing sheet and plate drawing with it. If we post them at Ashtide and the Lowfen landing, every crew on the coast will know the *Vigil* lay on our bearing. That is public use, and Kest ruled the duplicate stays public."

Calder looked at Nessa. Nessa looked at the four sheets on the market wall, weather-curling at the corners, with every name on them in Mara's fair copy.

"It is a record of objects," Calder said slowly. "Not of anything inside them. Kest said no office has power over that."

"Then we print fifty," Bevin said. "Mara's name is on it as witness. That will make them read it."

The printer read the sheets twice and named his price, which was the same price in three sizes and got worse as it got larger: two marks a copy for fifty, a hundred marks the yard did not have, or twelve marks for ten with the bearing sheet left out, because the press could not hold the drawing's scale and would have ruined it trying.

"We will take ten of the words," Calder said, "and keep the drawing here."

The type was set by the time Mara found them at the press. She had her coat over her arm and the Registry memorandum of two days before in her pocket, and she read the proof, and her face went white.

"Take my name off it," she said.

Calder stared. "You witnessed it. You made it out loud."

"I witnessed measurements, hours, and hands for a harbor wall, where any hand can answer me." Her voice was low and fast. "Printed and carried to another harbor, my name becomes a Registry warrant that the record is complete and fit for strangers to act on. It is not. It has an aperture sealed and unopened, a possible handling limit, three repair histories with a ceiling and not a date, and a ground band whose purpose is not stated. Strangers will read it as permission. They will read it as permission and they will go, and they will come back with what they took and an argument that I said they could."

The printer waited with his hands on the type.

"This is the consent question," Mara said, "in its first form. What may be published about a wreck, and who does a public record serve. A record serves the living who must work the ground, not the dead who cannot answer, and not a crew that needs a crowd. If you print my name across this coast you spend my ability to witness the next thing truly, and you will not know what you spent until the next thing is the one thing you cannot lose."

Calder thought of the sitting on Monday, of thirty marks and an eighth of share, of how much a printed coast would be worth to them.

He also thought of the shelf above the labor log, where a tin with twelve marks in it was sitting under Nessa's dates.

"Set it without her name," he told the printer. "Crew's hands only, with the line that it is a crew's copy, witnessed in Morrow Quay, and incomplete."

"Twelve marks," the printer said.

"Out of the bag," Calder said. "Not out of the tin."

"The tin is the bond," Nessa said from the door. She had not raised her voice. "The bond is the only money in this yard that has a date on it. The paper is not."

"The paper is what is left after the bond," Calder said, "which is the only reason there is any paper."

The printer reset the type and inked it and worked it while the light was still in the room, and ten copies came off the press at six for twelve marks, which went out of Saturday's wage and left four. They posted two on the market wall beside the original, gave three to Free Keel crews for their own walls, and kept five rolled in oilcloth against the damp in the bench chest. The bearing sheet and the plate drawing stayed in the chest, unrolled, because the crew had a right to a record they had made and not a record somebody else had printed for strangers.

On the way back Mara walked beside him, still angry, and did not soften it by being right.

"You paid twelve marks to make yourselves weaker," she said.

"We paid twelve to keep your word good," Calder said. "A record that spends its witness is not public. It is used."

She did not answer for half the road.

"Keep one copy dry and in the chest," she said at the yard door. "The sitting will want to see what you did not print, and a man who can show what he withheld is a man who can be believed about what he showed."

Nessa wrote that night: *22nd. Tide work, inner mud, lighter off, 24 on the board, harbor's share 3, pump hire 3, rope and food 2, 16 in the bag. Ten copies printed without conservator's name, 12 marks paid out of the wage, 4 left and spent on air. Bearing sheet and plate drawing withheld and kept. Tin untouched at 12.*

Calder signed. The duplicate stayed public, and small, and the cost of keeping it honest was on the page where the man who had to live on it could find it.
