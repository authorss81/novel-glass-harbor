# Chapter 39

*Not Better Than the Length of the Seam*

Nobody in that yard slept on the night of the thirteenth and nobody pretended they had, and at seven in the morning on the fourteenth the stove was lit and the door was shut and the three sheets of three points were on the bench unrolled, and Bevin Rook had the crew's book open and a new stub of pencil, and Calder Marr had a slate with the two distances on it and no answer on it anywhere.

The interval was the whole of it. A mile and two hundred yards from the mole's toe to the shoal's inner edge, and a mile and two hundred and ten yards from there to the sixth station of the office's own course, and a difference of ten yards, and a tolerance of not better than ten yards, and a sentence on the market wall saying the difference was ten yards.

"Right," Nessa Pike said. "Nobody is going to say the word interval in this shed again until somebody has said it in full, and the full of it is that those two numbers are not the same kind of number and we put them on one sheet and let a man subtract one from the other."

"They are a distance and a distance," Bevin said.

"They are a distance taken off a boat on the outer side of a mole with a hand lead and a man at the oars, in a slant tide, entered as the tender's count because the tender's count can be checked by another man on that mole — and a distance taken off this office's own run along its own course with a wire on a lead, corrected by a column nobody has ever read, in the spring, by a man who is not alive any more." She put her finger on the second figure. "And the officer's word for it on the Tuesday was that a difference equal to the tolerance of one of the two numbers is not a measurement. It is an agreement. He was right and he was right in front of a clerk, and if we walk into that room at ten and say *even to ten yards*, then a man in a corner writes *even to ten yards* in a notebook and the next man who reads that notebook reads an interval and not a limit, and in four years there will be a chart in a room in Veyr with a straight line on it that a Free Keel yard drew, and the Free Keel yard will not have drawn it, and the drawing will be on the chart anyway."

"Then what do we say," Calder said. "We cannot say nothing. A sheet with three points on it and no distance on it is three points."

"We say what we have got." She was looking at the first figure and not at him. "Marr, I have been awake since four and I have been doing this in my head because I cannot do it any other way at this hour, and I have got to the bottom of the trouble and it is not the two distances. It is the first one."

"The first one is a mile and two hundred yards and it is Rook's count off a boat with a hand lead and I have the hour and the state of the air on it and two of the harbor's tide gang stood in the water's edge and watched it happen."

"It is a count," Nessa said. "It is a count *to* something, and we have never once said out loud what the far end of that count is, and I have been three weeks not saying it, and the officer said it out loud on the Tuesday without meaning anything by it — *the first of them is not a bearing, it is something Rook found with his hand on a bottom at ten feet* — and he was right, and I did not argue, and I have been carrying it since."

She turned the first sheet round and put her finger on the middle of it.

"Read it. All of it. Slowly, and read what it says about where the seam stops."

Bevin read it. Bedded pale material in the mole's foundation, running north under the rubble to two boat lengths, deeper at the north end. Three keys seen. Counted and stated at the ladder by B. Rook, the twenty-eighth, entered as the tender's count by Marr, three figures in the same figure, bedded, not lying loose.

"There," Nessa said. "It is not a point. It is a seam of a length we gave ourselves, in a rubble foundation, running north under a mole, and we have been using the *south* end of it as a position because the south end is where the boat was, and we have not said so anywhere, not on the sheet, not in the book, not in the hearing room, and the north end is two boat lengths north of the point we have been calling the first point."

"Two boat lengths is about forty feet," Bevin Rook said.

"Two boat lengths is about forty feet in your boat on that tide and it is not better than that, because you went on a slant and you said you could follow it to about two boat lengths and then the rubble was on top of it and you could see the edge of it and no more, and *about* is in the sheet twice." She sat down. "So the first point is a place with forty feet of uncertainty on the end of it that runs north, and the tolerance we published is ten yards, and a yard is three feet, and forty feet is thirteen yards. We published a limit three yards better than the thing we are standing on, and we published it on a wall, and a man took a ruler out of his pocket on the second of this month and did the sum and it worked out to ten yards and everybody in this yard thought that was a triumph."

---

It took two hours to get to the bottom of it, and it was Bevin Rook who got there, at about twenty past eight, in the flat voice, with the pencil behind his ear and his right hand flat on the page.

"There is another thing," he said, "and it is worse than the seam and I have not said it in a fortnight because I did not want to say it and because nobody has asked me for it since the twenty-eighth, and if we are going into a room in two hours with three sheets and a difference of ten yards on them, then it has to be said at this bench, and it has to be said by me, and I have had my arm in a sling for six weeks and this is the one thing in this business that is mine to say and I am not having anybody else say it for me."

"Say it, then," Nessa said.

"It is my number." He did not look up from the book. "On the twenty-eighth I came up the ladder at the third seam and I gave the tender's count, which was nine and ten and a half, and my own count was seven and eleven, and I said my own count out loud on the ladder because the rule says a diver may say his own number and it goes in as his if he does. And Calder did not put it in, and I did not argue, because the rule says it only goes in if the man who took it can be checked, and nobody on that mole could check my count and everybody could check his, and I said *there is no rule that says you have to put a man's number in when the man is standing on the ladder in front of you*, and I have thought about that sentence every day since and I was wrong in a way I did not know I was wrong in.

"My count was two feet out on the first depth and half a foot out on the second, and I read them off a hand lead on a moving bottom with a slant on, and I am not a tender and I have never claimed to be. And there is no tide in this harbor where a hand lead on a rubble toe is a good instrument, and the whole of the first point rests on that lead, and its far end is a seam I could only follow for about two boat lengths.

"So the number on the sheet is my number and the tolerance on the sheet is not a tolerance at all. It is the distance between a boy's two figures when the boat is moving, and that is not ten yards, it is not better than forty feet, and there is no honest figure on that sheet for anybody to subtract."

The stove ticked four times and nobody spoke.

"Say it again in the room," Calder said.

"I will say it in the room if you put it in the book first, because if I say it in a room and it is not in the book then it is a boy contradicting a document in front of a naval officer, and if it is in the book then it is a boy correcting his own record, which is the other thing." He got the pencil. "And I want it in the book in your hand as well as mine, Calder, because you are the man who declined it and not me, and a book that says a boy was listened to is a different book from one that says a man was overruled, and this yard has enough of the second kind."

Calder Marr wrote it out in his own left hand and Bevin Rook read it back twice, and it went into the book on the fourteenth of November in the morning, dated, with the words *diver's own count now given by Rook, 7 and 11 against the tender's 9 and 10½, not entered at the time because the tender's count was the checkable one and is now found to be the tender's and not the diver's, and the interval is withdrawn by the crew at the bench and not by any office.*

---

The answer took another hour and Nessa Pike wrote most of it, and she wrote it in the terms a mason would use and not the terms a lawyer would, and she read it out to the bench three times before anybody was allowed to say anything about it.

"First point. The mole's toe, third and fourth seams, on the twenty-eighth, in a boat on the outer side, with a hand lead and a hand lead's man, and the harbor's tide gang on the wall with two of its men in the water's edge. What was found: a bedded seam of transparent material in the mole's own foundation stone, running north under the rubble to about two boat lengths, deeper at the north end, three keys seen. What was taken as a position is the south end of that seam, being the end at which the boat lay. What the tolerance of the first figure is: not better than the length of the seam, being about two boat lengths, being about forty feet, on a hand lead, on a slant, in a moving boat, by a man of twenty-two with a sling on his arm.

"Second point. The shoal's inner edge at eight fathoms, on the work permit of the twelfth of October, with a keel line and eleven keys in it running west-southwest on a dead straight, no break and no join, measured, drawn, and a measurement entered in a labor log this office countersigned. What the tolerance of the second figure is: not better than the lead on a good bottom in eight fathoms, and the yard does not offer a figure for it, because the yard has one good bottom sounding on that ground and one only.

"Third point. Station six of this office's own annual course over the outer ground, taken in the spring, fifteen fathoms and two corrected, the wire paid out to eighteen fathoms and two, neighbours at sixteen three both sides, firm clay, one figure out of thirty-six on two miles of a course this office has run every spring for forty years, read out of the office's own book in this office's barge on the second of November, in the presence of the clerk, written out in the yard's hand and not copied.

"Distances. First to second, a mile and two hundred yards, being the yard's own count. Second to third, a mile and two hundred and ten yards, being the office's own run. The difference of ten yards is withdrawn by the yard and does not appear in this record. The yard states that the two numbers are of different kinds, that neither is better than the method by which it was taken, that the difference between them is not a figure, and that the crew does not know whether the three places are on a straight within anything better than the length of a seam of about forty feet on a rubble bottom in a moving boat, and does not offer a figure for it.

"What the three points show. Three places on one bearing where the bottom comes up, and the bottom comes up nowhere else on two miles of a course this office sounded. That is the load-bearing fact and it is the office's own figures against the office's own neighbours, and no part of it is the yard's arithmetic.

"What the three points do not show. What is under sixteen fathoms of water at two miles and a quarter from this mole. Whether the thing at the first point and the thing at the second point and the thing at the third point are one thing or three things. Any purpose, and no person in this harbor has been asked, and this crew will not say."

She put the pen down.

"That is the answer to the officer's question," she said, "and the answer is *we do not know, and here is exactly how much we do not know, and here is the one thing in it that is not ours*. And every line of it can be checked by a man in a room with a rule and a lead and a boat and about two hours. And not one line of it says even, and not one line of it says ten yards, and the number that is gone off that wall this morning is the number that the yard has wanted to say since the second of November and the number that would have been the best thing in the room."

---

"It is the best thing in the room," Calder Marr said, "and I want to say it and I am not going to say it, and I would like somebody in this shed to mark the day I did not say it, because I have not kept a single one of those up to now."

"I will mark it," said Bevin Rook, and read the withdrawal back aloud in the yard's own flat voice, and then read the whole of the answer back, twice, the way he read everything.

Then Calder took the three sheets to the market wall and took them down himself, all three, and unhooked the *Vigil's* claim from over the top of them and nailed it back where it had been, and then he came back and wrote on the fresh sheet, in his own left hand, under a rule, the nine words that were the whole of what he had.

*The difference is withdrawn by the yard. See the sheet inside, which says why.*

The officer's man was at the wall when he did it, and had been since half past eight, and he had counted the two crew copies nailed up beside the original and had a note of the three on other crews' walls and the five rolled in oilcloth in the bench chest, which Calder had brought up the quay in the barrow and had not unrolled and had put back in the chest and had strapped, and the man wrote the number ten and the hour on his own slate and put the slate in his satchel and did not ask for any of them and Calder did not offer.

---

At nine o'clock they put the pump and the clamp stock and the cordage and the bench chest into a hand-barrow with a rope round it and took it up the quay to the hearing room, because Kest had said the boy was to be in the room when the tin came open, and because the clerk wipes that hearing room's slate wall every Monday at eight o'clock whatever is on it, and this time the wall was going to be blank again and the three points were in the yard's own hands and not in a reeve's chalk., and the clerk came out at half past nine with the tin in his hand and Bevin Rook signed for it in his right hand and read the clerk's own line back before he signed it, and the pump went up the quay on a barrow with a cracked housing in it and a sleeve packed with the reeve's tallow and no new housing bought and none to be had before the spring.

Nessa Pike put her hand flat on the housing at the yard door at half past six that evening and said, "It has come home and it is exactly as useless as it went away, and I am going to write that on the wall in chalk so that any man who comes into this yard knows that the pound in that chest on the fourteenth is a pound of paper and not a pound of gear."

"And the bond," Calder said. "The twelve marks were spent out of the tin on the twenty-fourth and the gear was held to eighteen and the clerk's balance is the iron's sale, and the iron is worth twenty at a fair sale and a third of it is entered to Brigg, and the claim on the ground is discharged on Monday, and the eighth comes off the top of anything we ever raise from that ground for the rest of the year, and the pledge came out of a clerk's hands at half past nine this morning and there is not one mark behind any of it, and I would like to know what a man has to do to get a pump that works."

"He has to earn a second one," Nessa said, "and he is off the bottom, and there is no charter, and the notice of the thirty-first is nailed to a wall over a market he is barred from, and the pumping work he can take he takes with the diver on the tender's cleat and the tender on the pump, and the harbor may refuse the job, and I wrote that down in October and I would write it again."

They ate on the slip because there was nowhere else, and the tide was making at the top of the water, and out in the road the harbor's bell went three strokes, and nothing came up out of the bottom off the mole under it, and Bevin Rook took the book out and wrote the hour and the state of the water and the line *Knock, delayed, in the bottom off the mole. No source. Not written as a voice*, and read it back, and then he read back the whole of the answer, all of it, twice, and the fourteen marks, and the six marks that have gone on the note since Saturday, and the withdrawal of ten yards, and the last line was the reason Calder Marr would read out in a room in twenty-five minutes, two hundred and forty words with nothing left out of them that a man could check and nothing in them at all about a boy on a quay in 1961 or 1966 or whenever it was, and he read that out too, in the flat voice, twice, and put the book down on a stone.
