# Chapter 29

*The Panel at the Fifth Seam*

The tide was three days past the equinox and making like a different sea, and Tobias Wren went up the mole on the morning of the third with a hand axe and a cold chisel and his own reasons, which were timber.

"There is no timber in this yard," he said, at the top of the steps, in a voice that brooked no argument, "and there is a cradle to be cut in the spring, and a cradle is oak, and every mole from here to the water gate is faced with oak baulks that the sea has been taking out of the wall for sixty years and that nobody has ever been allowed to take up. I have asked the office twice. I am asking a third time with a chisel in my hand so that they understand I am serious, and I would like you three to come because a man on a mole asking for timber is a man somebody will want to explain the law to."

They asked on the second and Kest said yes on a condition, and Kest came up the steps himself at nine o'clock in the morning with his book under his arm, because he had not been up that mole since the harbor lifted its own iron off the third seam on the twentieth, and he stood at the top and looked at the water for a while without saying anything.

"Do not go past the head," he said. "The face and the toe are shut. The top of a mole is not the face and it is not the toe, and I will not have an argument about it at ten o'clock in the morning."

They went down the inner face on the steps that run inside the mole wall, which is the only way a man gets to the water on the harbor side, and at the third step from the bottom the tide was up against the masonry and the slop was going to a sheet of glare, and Tobias went at the wall with his chisel and got a good two feet of sound baulk out of the joint and stopped, because two feet was not a cradle and four feet would be a robbery.

"That is the best of it," he said. "That baulk has been in that wall since before the slip was cut, and somebody is going to take a hundred of them out of this mole in the next ten years for a different kind of boat, and I do not suppose I will be here."

Nessa Pike did not answer him, and Calder understood a half second later why, and understood it a second after that.

They were standing on the mole's inner face at the third step, and the notice of the thirty-first closed that face from the third seam to the eleventh, to all persons, on any work, under any instrument of the harbor authority or any other, and the harbor authority had posted it and enforced it in front of the tide gang with a seal over the nail, and four men had stood in the fish market and read the words *all persons* out loud.

"This is my ground," Kest said, to nobody. "That is my face and my baulk and my notice, and I served it on the thirty-first because I was obliged to serve it, and I have read it aloud to four crews and to a clerk and to a man off a cutter's boat, and I have not read it once to myself, and I have not been down these steps since the day I served it."

"Reeve," Calder said, "we should go up."

"We are going up." Kest did not move. "I have been eleven years in this office and I have never once been the man who put a thing in his own book that he had no right to see, and I have never once been the man who did not. And I am going to look at my own foundation stone this morning because a thing that is a bar on a mole is a bar on a mole, and a man who has never been down to look has not got an opinion, and an opinion is what I am obliged to have by Friday." He took his hands out of his pockets. "Whatever is down there goes in the book, and the book names the four of you as witnesses, and I am aware of exactly where that puts me, and I have put myself there on purpose, and I would thank you not to make a speech about it."

Then Nessa said: "Reeve. Come here a moment. I want you to look at something and I want you to look at it before I have decided what I think I am looking at, because I have been wrong in front of people in this harbor before."

The slop on a falling tide goes to glare, and in the glare you can see the bottom, and what was lying in the glare at the fifth seam, forty yards south of the third, in eleven feet of water against the mole's rubble, was a panel.

Not a rib. A panel — a plate of it, four foot by something, pale, lying at an angle with its upper edge out of the rubble, and along the upper edge three keys, and in the lower corner a rim.

A Crown-shop bolted rim, with the studs standing proud and the seams in a jig's spacing, and the glass inside the rim a shade darker than the glass around it, and the whole of it bedded into the mole's foundation under three feet of the mole's own stone, and it had not been dropped and it had not slid, because a thing that has slid lies on the slope of the rubble and this lay *in* the rubble, halfway up the mound, the way a man sets a stone into a wall he is building.

Kest went down the step.

He did not say anything for a while. He put his hand flat on the chain, which was the thing he did instead of swearing, and he looked at the panel, and then he went back up four steps and got his book out and wrote in it standing up, in the flat voice he used for a hearing, and the three of them stood on the steps and let him.

*3rd. At the top of the tide, from the steps inside the mole wall, the officer of this office and three of the Free Keel yard saw, at the fifth seam, in eleven feet, bedded in the mole's own foundation stone, transparent material in plates, one plate being four foot by some part of four, with three keys along the upper edge and a bolted rim of a Crown-shop pattern in the lower corner, and darker glass inside the rim, and the whole of it bedded in the mound and not lying loose. Seen. Not measured. Not touched. The officer has seen it and enters it in his own book in the presence of three witnesses whose names are below, and the office is not required to send a thing it has merely seen to the circuit office, and is not sending it today, and will send it if the office is asked in writing by an office that has jurisdiction and states its reason.*

*Witnesses: Marr, Pike, Rook.*

He read it back. He made Calder read it back. He made Bevin read it back, twice.

Then he shut the book and said, to nobody, "Twenty-six marks' worth of timber and I get a glass plate."

---

It was after the tide had dropped that Kest produced the instrument, and he produced it out of his own coat, and Calder understood that the reeve had been carrying it since the thirty-first and had not mentioned it.

"You did not show me this on Wednesday."

"I have shown it to nobody, because until an officer of this authority has seen a thing on this ground there is nothing for an instrument to be about." He put it on the top step, flat, and weighted it with his hand. "Read it. I wrote it myself and I have had four days to make it plain and I would like a plain instrument in this harbor before the end of the month, whatever it is about."

It was six clauses. He read them out and Calder read them again after him.

*One. The ground is the mole's inner face at the fifth seam, at a depth of eleven feet, at the top of the tide, and the only work permitted is one supervised look by one diver, without tools, without a lead line, without anything brought up, and without a bell.*

*Two. The look is worked by this office, at this office's cost, with this office's tide-gang on the wall and one of its men on the diver's line from the wall, and the diver's own hand is the only thing in the water.*

*Three. A reason in writing, read out by this officer before the diver goes in, entered in this office's book before the diver goes in.*

*Four. Whatever is seen is entered in this office's book in the same sitting, in the words of the man who saw it, and posted on the wall of the fish market beside the provenance duplicate within the week.*

*Five. The diver is one diver. This office will not supply a second man in the water and will not supply a boat.*

*Six. The date is the eighth of this month, at the top of the tide, being a spring tide, and if the conditions of this instrument cannot be satisfied by that date then it is held open to a later date at this office's pleasure and without a new purpose and without a new bond, and nothing in it lapses with the defense.*

"There is no seventh clause," Calder said.

"There is no seventh clause because I have written this instrument four times and the seventh clause is the one I am not allowed to write." Kest took his hand off the paper. "Clause three of the circuit office's notice of the thirty-first says that any person who has handled such material shall not enter the water within the harbor limits on any work of this authority without a written plan countersigned by a circuit officer of that office. Marr, you handled it. Pike packed it. Rook carried the box. The three of you are the three people in Morrow Quay that clause catches, and I have no power to countersign the plan, and that office has spent the last three days arguing that a line of joined construction is not a scatter and that your crew has handled it."

"So the instrument is void."

"The instrument is *conditional*, and the condition is that a plan countersigned by a circuit officer of that office is produced first, and that office has had eight days to countersign one thing and has not asked about it once. So I have written the best instrument this authority can write, and it is worth precisely nothing, and I would like you to understand three things about when I wrote it, and then I will put the paper away.

"I wrote it on the thirty-first, which was the day the notice came, and I wrote it for the fifth seam and at eleven feet because the fifth seam is the one piece of that face where a man's hand can reach to the bottom off a step and eleven feet is as deep as a man goes down on his own. I had no reason whatever to think there was a thing in that water. I wrote it because a man who has a plain paper in his pocket at eleven o'clock on the morning he finds something is not standing in a shed with his hands up, and because I have been in this office eleven years and I have watched four men find things they could not afterwards prove they had found.

"And I have not shown it to you before today because I had not seen anything to point it at, and because the moment I showed a yard that paper they would have gone and looked for whatever was under it, and I am not having a man of mine out on my mole at night with a punt and a lamp." He folded the sheet in three and put it inside his coat, and the whole of it took about a minute.

Nobody said anything on the steps for a while, and the water came up the wall and went back.

"Reeve," Bevin Rook said. He had his right hand in the sling and his left arm bare to the elbow because it could not go in a sling that fitted, and he was the only one of the three of them who could not get his coat on, and he had not said anything for four days. "If that plan comes, who is the diver."

Kest looked at him.

"You are not the diver," Calder said.

"I have not asked."

"You were going to."

"I was going to," Bevin said. "I would have been faster and I would not have cost the yard any air, and that is a real argument and I would like it written down as a real argument and not as a boy being brave." He put his good hand on the chain. "And the tender's word would have been yours, and you would have had somebody to hold, and that is the whole of it and it is not nothing."

Calder thought about it for about four seconds, which was three seconds longer than he had ever taken.

"No," he said. "Not this one."

"Why not."

"Because if it is your hand in there then the only man in the world who saw it is you, and you are twenty-two and you will be asked about it for the rest of your life, and there is nothing I could say in a room in ten years that would make anybody believe you." He turned round. "And because I have been off the bottom by my own book since the twenty-fifth, and a book is only worth the paper it is written on if the man who wrote it is the first man it stops. You get held to that every tide for the rest of your working life and I do not, and that is the entire difference between us and I am not going to give it up in a fortnight for a plate with a rim on it."

Then he said the other thing, and he said it to Kest and not to the crew.

"I have to ask you for it. At the sitting on the seventh. I have to put in front of that room a request that the bottom be restored to me, in writing, for one tide, at a named seam, with a stated reason, and if the room says no then this ends, and if the room says yes then the seventh clause gets written."

"I told you on the twenty-eighth that I would hold the sitting and that I had no instrument to refuse you on," Kest said. "Inside the line, on my own ground, where the wall wants the entry and where I cannot say no, I have no instrument to refuse you with. I will tell you now, so that you can go into that room on the seventh without a surprise in it: the answer will be yes, and I have thought about it for three days, and I do not enjoy having thought about it." He went up the steps. "And if the answer is yes, the plan is still wanted from the circuit office, and that is not mine either."

---

They could have done it that night, and Calder did not write down that he had thought about it, and everybody in that yard understood that he had, and the reason was on the page the next morning in his own hand.

There was a hired punt on the fish dock that would take a man out for a mark and come back with him, and there was a top of tide at about two in the morning, and eleven feet of water against a mole wall at the fifth seam is not a dive. It is a man kneeling on a gunwale with a hand in the water. There was a hooked lead-line in the yard's locker with a bone toggle on it and it would hold a plate's edge well enough to lead a panel a hundred yards on a good bottom, and there was a lead-line in use on this very ground in November with a rag on a float and nobody had ever been on it.

Nobody in the yard said any of that. It sat in the shed like weather.

At eight in the morning Calder took the book and wrote it in, and Bevin Rook read it back, and it is in this yard's record because it is in the yard's book.

*I have not gone to the fifth seam. I have thought about going, on the punt, at two in the morning, at a depth of eleven feet, with a line on the rim, and I have not gone, and the reasons are as follows and they are not one reason and the second is the real one.*

*The first is the notice of the 31st, clause one, which closes that ground, and the serving of it on the mole, and my own undertaking of the 25th, and any one of the three is enough and I would not go on the strength of an argument with a reeve about the width of his own paper.*

*The second is this. The day I put a mark on that panel in my own book, the wall copy is a copy of a thing four men saw in a slop on a falling tide, and every number that comes after it is a number somebody has to take on trust from me. I have spent two months telling this harbor that the office should not be able to make a man say he was somewhere on the authority's word and nothing else. If I go down there alone tonight and come up with a figure, then on the seventh of this month I stand in the reeve's hearing room and I am a man who put a figure on a piece of glass in the dark with no tender, and every other figure I have ever given this harbor is a figure somebody else can check. I would rather be a man with three checkable numbers and no panel than a man with four numbers and no way to say where one of them came from.*

*And the third, and Wren is right about it and I am writing it down so that it is not only mine: there is no crane on this mole, there is no cradle, there is no timber, there is no boy with a hand, and a plate of glass with a Crown-shop rim in it does not come up in a fish dock punt. I would break it. I would be right that it was worth breaking. Both of those would be true, and I would be the man who did it, and the harbor would be right for the rest of its life.*

*The panel is at the fifth seam in eleven feet and it is not going anywhere. There will be a tide next week and a tide the week after that. It has been there since before this harbor was cut, and it will be there in March, and the yard now knows where it is and has written it down and has not gone, and that is the whole of what we have, and it is more than we had on the twenty-sixth.*

Nessa read that back and did not say one word about it, which she had not said on the sixth of November either, and Calder put the pencil down and got on with the morning.

---

They put the panel on the market wall on the fourth, on the third sheet, under the rule, with a line under it that read: *Seen at the fifth seam at the top of the tide on the 3rd, in the presence of the reeve and three witnesses, and entered in the harbor authority's own book. Not measured. Not touched. Not brought up. No figure of any kind is offered for it, and the yard will not offer one, and the notice of the 31st stands over the top of this sheet and is not removed by anything written on it.*

And at the bottom of that sheet, where the wall meets the boards, three sheets and a notice and a duplicate, a nail went in crooked and the whole arrangement leaned out into the weather by a hand's breadth, and Bevin Rook, standing on an upturned crate with his arm in a sling and his coat buttoned over it with one button, put it straight with the heel of his good hand and stood back and looked at it.

"That is a wall," he said, "with a piece of glass in the water eleven feet under it, and a notice that says we may not go and get it, and a piece of paper that says we have not gone, and a piece of paper that says where it is."

"Yes."

"And in six months there will be a man in this harbor who has never met any of us who can stand here and find out all four of those things without asking a single one of us a question." Bevin got down off the crate carefully with his left arm held out away from his body, in the way a man gets down who has been told not to grip. "Which is the argument, isn't it. That is the whole argument, and it took us two months and a boy's hand."

"Take the crate back to where it came from," Nessa said, "and Calder, the hearing is the day after tomorrow, and you have ten terms in that book to write for the seventh, and you have not written one of them."
