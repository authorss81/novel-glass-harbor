# Chapter 25

*What a Poor Yard Pays*

The bonesetter on the fish dock was a woman of fifty-odd who set a broken wrist twice in the herring season and told people so, and she came at half past eight on Sunday the 30th with a bag of rolled linen and a flat piece of split ashwood and looked at Bevin Rook's hand for a long time without touching it.

"Can it feel the ends?" she said.

"I can feel the ends. I cannot feel the middle."

She looked at Calder, and not at Nessa, and Calder understood that he was in the room because a man with a burn on his forearm had come in a hired boat and the boy's money had come in a hired boat, and she wanted the man who had been in charge of it in the room.

"It was crushed in a bight and the bone went in a way that is very lucky to have gone at all, because I have seen the same thing in a chain and I have had to take a man's arm off at the wrist an hour later." She bound it, and set the ashwood, and put the whole thing back into a sling higher than the bonesetter's first binding had been, "because that boy is going to want to put that hand on a line in about nine days and the higher sling is the only thing that will stop him, and I would rather you had a fight with me about it in a week than have me be the one who was wrong."

"How much," Calder said.

"A mark. I do not take less and I do not charge the boy, and I have told him so and he did not argue, which tells me he is either very well brought up or very frightened, and it is usually the second."

The mark came out of the nine in the bag before the harbor's share of the day's reckoning was even cold, and the bonesetter took it and put it in her own pocket and did not give a receipt, because that is not what that trade does.

The written account cost another mark and took until the middle of the afternoon.

A ship's surgeon had come into the harbor on the Saturday's evening boat off a coaster out of the north, and he was a salaried man with a cabin and a table and no reason in the world to write anything down, and Calder went to him at the fish market and told him plainly what a written account of a crushed hand was worth to a boy with no trade and a guild that had never heard of him, and the surgeon said it was worth a mark to him and a great deal more to the boy's mother, and wrote it on the back of a shipping agent's docket in a bad hand, with the hour and the cause and the name of the man who held the tender's cleat and the name of the man who gave the call.

That was the whole of what the law could be made to do for a hurt hand in Morrow Quay, and it cost two marks out of nine, and it was worth every one of them, and it did not pay a single week's wages.

Bevin's mother came at four and read the surgeon's writing twice, standing in the yard, and then she asked Calder three questions and all three of them were about money.

"How much is owed him."

"Four marks a week while there is work, and there has been no work for nine days, and there is none in this yard and none on the board that he can take with that hand for a month."

"And you are holding the book for a defense to the fourteenth of next month, and there is a mark of mine in it."

"Yes."

"And an eighth off the top of anything that ground ever raises, and a pump in pawn, and a boat on the hard." She folded the writing into her apron. "Then I will tell you what I want and it is not money. I want it written down with the hour and the cause and the man who held the cleat, and I want the copy kept, and I want the original in my apron and not in a shed, and I want to be told the day the defense ends, whichever way it goes, in a room, out loud, by a man in a coat."

"It is written down," Nessa said, and turned the book round so the woman could read the 29th's entry standing up, all of it, including the line about the wages and the masonry and the eighteen of the twenty-six.

The mother read it and gave it back and looked at her son, who was standing in the yard with a sling to his chin because the sling was the only part of the arrangement he had not argued with, and said: "You told him nine."

"I told him nine."

"You had eleven."

"I had eleven."

"You had eleven with the hand already gone and you said nine, and you said it before you were off the ladder, and you are telling me about it a day afterwards, which means you have been sitting in a room for a day working out that you were not brave and were only correct." Her voice did not rise. "I have had four men in that chair since you were born and I have never once known a man to be only correct. I do not know what to do with you."

"I do not either," Bevin said.

---

At half past four, while the mother was still in the yard, Nessa took the harbor's pump back across the wall on a hand-barrow and stood it on the hard beside the *Kittiwake* and looked at it for a while, and then she made Calder and Tobias and Bevin come and stand with her, because she had done an addition and she wanted it done in front of witnesses.

"We had a yard," she said, "on Friday. The yard had a boat, and the boat was ours, and the boat was beached and that was a bad thing and it was survivable. The yard had a bottom man, and he was good at it, and he is not. The yard had a tender's cleat, and the tender's cleat was a job, and the job is now held by a woman who also has to hold the fall, the pump, the board, and the slate." She put her hand on the pump. "The yard had no pump, which was a bad thing and we had learned to live with it. The yard has, at this moment, one borrowed boat that is not ours and one borrowed pump that is not ours, and both of them come back when the man who owns them decides they come back, and one of those men came back at ten on Saturday and will not be asked twice."

"Which leaves what," Tobias said.

"Which leaves a shed, a bench, a set of books, a stack of iron with a kerf in it, and four people." She looked at Bevin. "Three and a half. And on Monday I am going to the board myself, because the board does not want him and does not know it wants me, and when it finds out it will not want me either. And if I go out on a job that needs a bottom man, then I have no cleat, and if I hold the cleat then I have no gauge, and one of us ends up reading a number she cannot hear and the other writing one she cannot check."

"Then we do not take the pumping work," Calder said.

"We take the pumping work and we take it badly, and we say so on the board before we sign, which is a thing you have never once done in your life." She wrote it on the slate and read it back. "Any pumping work this yard takes from here, the crew's cleat is Pike, the pump is hired, and the clerk is told before we start that the diver is the tender and the tender is the pump, and the harbor may refuse the job, and I will not blame it."

Tobias looked at the slate for a while.

"You have just made us a worse yard on purpose," he said, "in writing, in front of the man who lost a hand on Saturday, so that nobody can say afterward that we pretended."

"Yes."

"Then put it in the book," Tobias said, "and add that the yard does not intend to be this way in March."

---

Alden Marr came at six in the evening and stood in the yard door for a while before he came in, the way a man stands outside a shed in winter who has been in the yard all his life.

The sling was wrong. Alden took it off in four movements, read the surgeon's writing off the bench where Calder had laid it, and said nothing at all about the hand, and re-bound it instead, higher, with the linen doubled at the heel of the palm so that the hand could not close.

"Your bonesetter put the wood across the knuckles," he said. "You cannot close a hand around a stick. Whoever she is, she is right about the bone and wrong about the wood, and in eleven days he will have forgotten and he will put a line over that wrist, and it will not be a line that weighs anything and it will be the last thing he does with a whole hand."

"Why higher."

"Because a hand that is set and bound low heals, and a hand that is set and bound low and then used does not heal, and there is nothing in this world that will make the difference for him except that he cannot get at it." He tied it off. "He will be a boat hand again in June. He will not be the same boat hand in June, and the difference is four months and one crushed chain, and that is the ordinary price of the trade and not a thing anybody has to be blamed for."

Then he stood up straight, which took him a while, and looked at Calder, and said the cold thing.

"Now sit down and hear the other half of it, because I have not come here to be the man who says the good part. That boy's wages were four a week and they are now nothing, and they will be nothing for about six weeks, and six weeks at four a week is twenty-four marks you do not have, and you have nine in a bag less two already gone this morning, and you have a pump in pawn and a boat on the hard and a defense that expires in fifteen days, and I have been in this yard since 1961 and I am going to tell you that you cannot carry the boat and the boy at the same time and that one of them is going to be put down to keep the other in the water."

"Say which one."

"I will not say which one, because you will do whichever one I did not name, and you have been doing that since you were nine." He put his hands in his pockets. "I have three repair jobs that keep this roof up and I have one boy who is a good diver with a hand that will not close, and the truth is that you have both and I have three jobs, and the arithmetic is not going to improve because you are brave about it."

Calder sat down on the bench.

"You did not ask me anything," Alden said.

"No."

"You came to this yard at six in the evening to ask me about a hand and you have not asked me about anything since you were thirty-one years old, and you stood in my shed this month with a file of a man's and you asked me nothing at all, and I have noticed, and I would like you to know that I have noticed, and that it is a different thing from the last thirteen years and I do not know what to do with it either."

"Then sit down and hold this," Calder said, and pushed the boy forward on the stool, and his father sat down on the end of the bench and held the hand while it was re-bound a second time, and his hands were oil-blackened in the creases and they were not steady and he did not pretend they were.

At the end of it, on the step, with his hat in his hand, he said one more thing, and it was the only thing he said in the whole of the evening that had not been about a hand.

"There was a man in this yard in sixty-one off the *Kittiwake* whose hand went under a chain, the same as that boy, and it was me that gave him the call and he came up on it." He put the hat on. "He was back in the water in nine weeks and he was a different man and he did not find out for about a year, and by the time he found out he had made four decisions on the strength of being the wrong man." He went out into the road. "I have never told anybody that and I have no idea why I have just told you, and you will not ask me why, because if you ask me why I will not know and we will both be stood in a shed wasting a good evening."

---

Nessa called the crew at eight and reorganized it in front of the boy, which was deliberate, and she did it in a flat voice off a slate.

"Everything inside the line, the tender's cleat is mine, from now on, and I do not care what the harbor's book says about who is what, because the book does not have a line in it about a hand. Marr holds nothing but the pump and the fall. Wren takes the boat and will not be argued out of it. And Rook reads the board and writes this book."

"I am not a clerk," Bevin said.

"You are a man with a hand that cannot close who can read a number twice and say it back to a man who cannot hear it properly, and there is not another man on this coast who can do that in your place." She put the slate down. "And in six months when the wood comes off I want you back in the water, and I want you back in the water rested, and a man who has spent six weeks holding a pencil is a man whose head has gone soft and whose ears have gone soft and who will want to go down too fast. So that is the arrangement, and the arrangement is for your own good, and I will not be arguing about it in April."

He looked at it for a long moment. Then he said: "Then somebody has to read the entries back to him, because that is what he does, and I cannot read them back to myself."

"No," Nessa said. "You will read them back to him. That is the other half of the job. Read it again, Calder, all of it, including the money."

Nessa read it again, all of it, including the money.

*30th. Hand set and rebound by Wren, higher, wood across the heel of the palm, cannot close. Bonesetter, fish dock, one mark. Written account of the injury, hour and cause and the man who held the tender's cleat and the man who gave the call, from a surgeon off the north coaster, one mark, original to the mother. Two marks gone of nine. Rook off the bottom and off the tender's cleat and on the board and this book. Tender's cleat to Pike from this day. Wages to Rook four a week, none, six weeks, twenty-four marks we do not have. And the other thing Wren said out loud on the step and did not say why.*

*First point on the mole's toe, the 28th. Second point out of the shoal book, on Thursday, in the reeve's office, with the clerk in the room. Nothing said about the interval yet.*

Bevin Rook read that back standing up with his arm in a sling, and the room did what rooms do when a man reads out his own ruin, which is to go extremely quiet, and Calder said, "Put the two in the book under the date of the thirtieth and not under mine."

"It is already under the thirtieth," Bevin said.

"Then say it again, because I have not got a mark in it anywhere this week and I have got two."

Bevin said it again. And Calder Marr wrote it out in the crew's book in his own left hand, because the pencil was the wrong way round in a book that has to be signed by a man who cannot hold a pencil, and Nessa countersigned under both of them, and the nine marks in the bag became seven, and there was nothing in the tin, and the chandler's note stood where it had stood since the twenty-fifth at eleven weeks into six with nothing paid on it this month, and the air pot was a third gone, and a third of that pot is a hundred marks a third, and seven marks was all the air in Morrow Quay that the Free Keel yard owned.
