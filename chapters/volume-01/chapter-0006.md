# Chapter 6

*One Witness, One Angle*

The platform at the Free Keel slip was a rotten timber deck on eight legs, three fathoms inside the mole, and the only reason the water under it was workable at night was that the mole took the western swell on its shoulder and passed a fraction of it through.

That fraction was enough to make the bell swing if you let it.

Nessa made the tender's cleat fast twice and then tested it with her whole weight, and when she was satisfied she wrote the time on the slate, which was half past eleven at night, and then she read the six things out loud in the order they had to be done, because reading them out loud was the only way any of them could later be proved to have been said.

"One. Named tender. Nessa Pike. I am not a volunteer and I am not going to be talked out of it, and if I go up the slip, the dive is over."

"Two."

"Independent line. Your bell lead is not your line. Your line is the one over the sheave with the toggle in your left hand, and it comes up to the cleat on the deck and not to me, because I am busy and a line that comes to a busy person is a line with no one on it."

She held up the cutoff wire and let it fall, and Calder felt it come down through the harness and stop at his shoulder where the clip was.

"Three. Fixed air limit. One hundred and ten minutes on the doubled hose, and I have written it on the slate, and you have written it on the slate, and there are two other people in this harbor who know it and it is not a hundred and twenty."

"Four. The weight seated." He turned the bag on the deck so that the lamp went through it. "My brother's bell is in there, seated, and it is not touching the glass because I will not let it touch the glass until the glass has taken hold of it, and that is the whole of the difference between a room and a stone."

"Five. The cutoff comes to me and I pull it and I say the word and you come. Not a knock. The word."

"Enough."

"Once," she said. "Six."

"The reason is in your book," Calder said, "and it is yours, and if anybody asks me what my reason was I am going to tell them I did not have one and that the woman who wrote it down did."

Mara Venn was on the dock wall with the lamp and the slate, and Bevin Rook was beside her with his back to the water because Calder had told him twice, and neither of them said anything when he climbed down into the bell.

---

The bell went over the platform's side and down three fathoms in a swells' time, and the moment the water closed over the crown the world became a small iron room with a hatch in the roof and a glass in the seat at his feet.

He was colder in the first minute than the water was. That was the first thing that had not been in Tobias Wren's forty-five-year-old account, and it was the first thing that told Calder the entry was real and not something he had built out of want.

He waited. He read the gauge. He read it again.

At nine minutes the shard began to tick.

Not to ring. To tick, the way a panel ticks when there is a pressure difference somewhere behind it, a small knock in the glass every second or two, and the knocks came in a group and then a gap and then a group, and the gap was a length of time that a man could measure against his own breathing.

He put his hand on the seat and found the bell through the lead lining, small and hard and shaped like nothing in particular, and he took his hand off and put it against the outside of the shard instead, and the ticking came up into the bone of his fingers.

Then he drew the air out of the bell by degrees, the way you draw water out of a sump, and the glass got louder than the water, and then the water got louder than the glass, and then there was no order between them at all.

The last thing he did before he went in was check that his left hand was on the toggle, and it was, and the toggle was under his thumb.

He went in.

---

The first thing was cold that had weight in it. Not the cold of water. A cold that came from somewhere in front of him and had a floor, and a floor that was up under his boots, and the cold sat on him like the side of a wall.

The second thing was that he was low. He was down at the level of the deck, sitting or crouched or sitting on a coil, and his view was along a deck and not across it, and he understood that he was not a tall man and not a man standing up, and that his right shoulder was under something.

The case was on his shoulder.

That was the whole of what he was, before anything had happened, and it was the first thing he would be able to say out loud on the deck, and it was the thing that undid him for most of the rest of the night: he had not gone anywhere. He had been put inside a man carrying a box.

The case was longer than it was deep, wrapped in oilcloth that had been dark and was now the color of a wet road. He had it high on his shoulder and his hand was under the bottom of it near the forward end, and he could feel the weight going away from him and back again, and he knew without being told that it was four men on it and he was the smallest, and that he had been given the near end because he was the one who could not be seen from the rail.

Ahead along the deck there was a rail, and past the rail a lighter's rail, and the two rails were a foot apart and the case was not going between them. It was going along the deck. It was being carried along the deck past a place where a man was standing with a paper.

The man with the paper was reading.

It was not loud. The man was reading in the flat voice of a man who has read out the same list twice already and will read it out again if he is asked, and Calder heard him clearly and could not make out the words in order, because the words went along and away at the same time, the way a bell goes along a line. What he could make out was the shape. Numbers. A tonnage. A repeated phrase with *standing empty* in it, read twice, and after the second reading the man turned the paper over.

The case crossed the man and the paper while he was turning it. That was all. It went by at the pace of four men walking, and the deck under it was planked and old, and there was a line along the deck on the outboard side, low down, with small tags on it at intervals, and the case went over that line and the men carrying it did not lift, and the man with the paper did not look up.

Three words came from behind and above and out of the frame, loud enough to make the deck jump: *—across, and do not—*

The second word was not a word. The sound came apart in the middle, the way a line parts, and what came out of the two halves did not belong together, and Calder's ears refused it and gave him a piece of each anyway.

Then the hand.

It came in from the low left, below the level he was at, and it was a hand in a thick glove, and it was not his hand and it was not a hand he knew, and it went up onto the tagged line and closed around one of the fittings where the tags were made fast.

The callus was on it. That was the thing he could not get past. A hard ridge of skin on the inside of the two middle fingers where the second joint bends, the ridge that a boy gets from working a caulking iron and never entirely loses, and Calder's own hand had one on the same place and he had had one since he was eleven, and the hand on the line was not a sailor's hand and was not a rating's hand and was not a grown man's hand at all.

It was on the fitting. It had hold of it. It was gripping the way a person grips a thing they intend to move, and the arm went back and down and out of the low edge of the frame, and the fitting came with it, and the tagged line went slack, and everything that was going to happen next happened in a place the man carrying the box was not looking at.

Calder tried to turn his head.

What he got instead was the edge.

It came across the scene the way a wet thumb comes across a page. Everything past it was doubled, and the doubling was not an echo of the same thing at a delay; it was two different things occupying the same water, and one of them was brighter than the other, and the brighter one had a hard rim on it like the rim of a cut plate, and the pain of looking at it arrived in his teeth and in the two fingers he had on the toggle.

He stopped pushing.

The hard rim did not go away because he stopped. It sat where it was, a hand's breadth wide, bright, with something moving in it, and behind it the tagged line lay slack on an old deck and a case four men were carrying went on along a lighter's rail.

He stayed where he was until the cold in the floor stopped being a wall and became water again, and then he took his hand off the glass.

The tide under the platform made a soft noise. He was in the bell. He had his left hand on the toggle and the clip was on his shoulder and the wire went up through the hose and out to a cleat on a deck above him, and the clock in the world was a small brass thing on the front of the panel and the hand on it had gone a long way round since he had last looked at it.

And the burn was on his right forearm, in one place, hot and specific, from a rope he had not held and was not holding.

A voice came down the hose from above. It was not loud and it was not close, because a bell is an iron room and a voice outside an iron room comes from a long way off and through a lot of iron. But there was only one voice in it, and it was above him, and it said one word.

"Enough."

He was not satisfied. He had the tagged line and the doubled edge and the fitting in his hand, and there was a case going along a deck that had gone past the only place he could see, and he had seventy minutes of air left on the slate and his own two hands.

He put his hand on the clip and then he took it off the clip, and he put his hand on the toggle, and he said the word back, because that was the condition, and the condition was the only thing in the world he had that was not made of glass.

"Enough," he said, and went up the line.

---

The hatch came open and Nessa Pike's face was in it, upside down, and the air on it was night air and smelled of tar and the fish dock and nothing else at all.

"Eleven minutes forty," she said. "You were in there eleven minutes forty and I have written it, and you have the look of a man who wanted another hour, and I would like you to say out loud that you have no argument."

"I have no argument."

"Say the rest of it."

"I am coming up and I am not going to be one of the men who argues."

He came up the ladder with his hands on the rungs because his hands were not doing what he told them, and Bevin Rook had the hatch lid and the lamp and a face on him like a boy at a firework, and Mara Venn was sitting down on the platform boards with her back against the mooring post, which was not a thing a woman with a Registry book sat down on, and Calder understood that she had been sitting there for a while.

Then the pain arrived, all of it at once, and he put both forearms on the rim of the hatch and hung there.

The burn on the right forearm was four inches long and it was not a rope burn he had. It was fresh, and it was wet, and it had the exact shape of a line going over a hand, and his skin was doing the whole of the burn at once instead of in order, the way a body does a thing it has already been through.

His ears were going. Not deaf — going. There was a note in them, low and flat, that had not been there that morning, and when he turned his head to find the harbor it was not where it was, and when he turned it back the flat note stayed where it was and the harbor moved. Somewhere under the flat note, a long way off and about a third of a second behind everything, there was the sound of a bell being rung twice in a loading space.

He put his hand flat on the platform boards to hold the world still and the boards were cold and real and stayed where they were.

"Water," Nessa said. "Small cup. You are not going to stand up yet."

The cup was at his mouth and he drank a mouthful and his hand did not shake, which surprised him more than anything, and then the second cup went worse because the first one had been what he was waiting for.

"Four men," he said.

Mara Venn's pen was on the board. "How many were carrying it?"

"Four on the case. I was one of them and I was on the near end and I was the one they put where they couldn't see me." He put his hands on his knees. "There was a man with a paper. He read a list. It had tonnage in it and he read a phrase about the hold standing empty and he read it twice, and then he turned the paper over."

"Twice."

"Twice, and then he turned it over, and while he was turning it the case went past him on the deck and he did not look up." Calder's voice went flat and stayed flat. "I cannot tell you the words. I could hear the shape of them and I could hear the numbers and I could not put the words in order, and I would not want a person to write down words I put in order myself."

"That is the correct answer," Mara said, and wrote it down. "And now the other things, and I want them before you have had time to arrange them. Was there anything on the case itself that you could read?"

"No mark. I was underneath it. I could not have seen a mark if it was painted on the lid."

"Was there any way for you to know the size of the space you were standing in?"

"No. I could see about six feet of deck and a rail and a man with a paper. There was no room in that for knowing anything about the space." He looked up at her. "And I could not see the other half of the deck, and I could not see what was behind the man with the paper, and I could not see where the case came from or where it went, and it went past a rail and out of my sight and I did not see it go into anything."

"Write that last part down exactly," Mara said, "because it is the sentence that keeps every honest man out of trouble and every dishonest man in it."

"Write this too." He held up his right arm. "I have a burn on this arm in the shape of a line I never held, and it is fresh, and it started the minute the hatch opened. If you want to write a medical note, write that, and write the time."

Mara wrote it down, and the writing took a long time because she did it in the flat hand and not fast, and when she had done it she read it back and made him agree to it.

Nessa had not said anything for a while. She was sitting on the bell with her arms round her knees, looking at the water beyond the platform rather than at either of them.

Then Calder turned to her to ask for the slate, and he answered her.

"Give me the s—" he began, and it came out of him in a different voice.

It came out low and short and with a lift at the end of it, three marks and a flick, the way a man calls to somebody over a line when the fog is on the water and the two of them have worked together long enough that the call does not need a name on it. It came out of his mouth before he knew he had made it, in the exact shape of a thing he had last heard on a quay in Morrow Quay when he was eleven years old, from a boy with quick hands who was going to be a licensed diver in a year and had not yet learned to be embarrassed about anything.

Nessa Pike went white.

She did not say anything. She sat on the bell with her arms round her knees and looked at him, and then she got up off the bell in one movement, went to the far end of the platform, and put both hands on the mooring post and held on to it with her back to him.

"Pike," Calder said.

"Give me a minute."

He gave her a minute. The harbor went on being the harbor, and the flat note went on sitting in his ears, and under it the loading bell went on ringing a third of a second behind everything else, and Calder sat on a rotten platform three fathoms inside a mole and understood that he had got what he came for, and that it was not the thing he came for, and that the difference between those two statements was going to cost him a great deal more than the air and the burn and the two days.

Somewhere under the flat note, in the space where a boy had stood with a coil of line in his hands, a voice said *I'll take the near end, I'm smallest* — and the only reason Calder knew the words was that he had been there.
