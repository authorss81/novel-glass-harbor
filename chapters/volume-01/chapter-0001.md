# Chapter 1

*Salt in the Second Pump*

The pump on the second cradle of the public dry dock was pumping bilge into the basin at a slant, and Calder Marr had been watching it for the better part of an hour.

He stood with his hands behind his back so that Rask could not watch them. Rask was a lean man in a coat that had been good on some other coast, and he had been talking for twenty minutes about how the pump looked. Not worked. Looked. There is a difference, and Calder knew what it was worth.

"The hose takes it," Rask said. "Any fool can see the hose takes it."

"The hose takes it until the third stroke, and then it doesn't, and after that you are pumping into the basin and me with it. I want to open the housing."

"You want to open the housing." Rask's mouth did something that was not quite a smile. "I've a market tide in three hours. You'll be into the afternoon and I'll be paying you by the hour for standing in cold water."

"Then carry water by the hour."

Beside him on the cradle a boy of about twenty-two was working a spanner back and forth through four strokes of free play, which was three more than any spanner needed. Bevin Rook made the spanner do a fifth and looked pleased with himself.

"Put that down. You're making the wall shine." Calder crouched and put his fingers on the suction elbow, and then looked up at Bevin. "When I say test, you break the elbow into the trough. Not the basin. If it's the basin we can't tell a good seal from a lucky one, because the basin takes the water either way."

"You told me the test was a formality."

"The test is never a formality. It's the only part anybody can see."

They got the housing open at twenty past nine. Calder said the shoulder was not where the crack was, and then, with his hand still flat over the flange, said that it was. He worked it with a wire brush and his thumbs for an hour and a half, and at half past eleven he stopped and read the chalk line twice, once from each side, the way he read gauges all his life and would go on reading them whether or not they told him anything.

"It's into the web," he said, when Bevin's voice went careful. "It doesn't go through yet."

"Then we tell him we can't do it. He sails at six."

"We grind the web back to sound and seat a sleeve. It'll hold because of the sleeve and not because of my cleverness, and I'll write that on the log so that if it ever goes, nobody has to guess."

They tested at half past one. Calder put his hand on the discharge and felt nothing at the joint where the crack had been, and then felt something: a fine cold seep coming up out of the web and running round the sleeve in a hairline. He watched it for a count of ten.

"Hold her there."

He opened the thread with the chisel and it became a run, and the run became a slow spur about the width of a finger coming out of the iron itself, and he stood in the dock's runoff and looked at the arc for as long as it took to be sure. Then he put the bolts back in and drained the housing onto the stone.

"That's the sound of a pump," Bevin said.

"That's the sound of a housing with a crack in the web longer than the chalk said." He wrote it on the log in full, with the hour and the number of bolts and the fact that he had taken the flange off after all. It took him four minutes and it was the whole of what he knew. "There's a sleeve in it. When you next have the bow off, put a hand under the elbow and feel for cold."

Rask took the pump at a quarter to three and went down the dock without saying anything about it. Then Calder found the chandler's note in his coat and looked at the figure and put it back, because there is no advantage to a debtor in doing sums where the creditor can see them. Eleven weeks into six. The chandler at the sign of the Drowned Bell held his paper without asking anything, which was a kindness and also a habit.

He had meant to do the mole chain too. After the pump he was down something, so when Bevin asked about it he said, "I said I'd look at the chain. It's been fouled since Michaelmas. It can wait a week."

The chain belonged to a small coaster that had lost her mooring in a blow in the autumn, and the harbor authority would pay the Free Keel a fee to raise it, and the fee was worth having, because fees are air you can buy later. The inner face is the working face, and it is lit until about eleven in fair weather, and after that you work by feel. Calder went out along it with Bevin and the hand capstan, and found the fouling exactly where the coaster's master had said it would be, because men who lose iron are honest about where it went.

He got the snook on it and the capstan bar in and the chain half up, and then it came free and the whole weight came over at once and the capstan took him off his feet and put him flat on the wet stone with his shoulder against the handrail.

"I said take it easy," Bevin said, from some distance.

"Say this one. Take it easy."

Calder got up, checked the capstan pawl, found it fine, and took the bruise as the price of showing Bevin nothing. The trick with a capstan was not strength. It was knowing how much iron was coming and getting out of the way before it did.

The chain was up and the fee was made, and it was past two with the light going flat on the west side of the mole. Calder walked the fifty yards back toward the head because it was warmer there, and the swell under the mole came in long slow sets that made the stone work underfoot. He was looking at the toe of the wall where the water came in and broke white, because that is what you look at.

Then he stopped.

There was a shape in the mole's toe at about eight feet down, half in and half out of weed and shell, and it was not a stone, and it was not a broken davit, and it was not the colour of anything the mole was made of.

"Get the boat," he said.

Bevin looked where he was looking and saw water. Calder did not explain, because explaining takes longer than the boat. He unlocked the boathouse and they came out along the mole in the *Kittiwake*, the water eight feet and clear and the swell coming through in long sets that lifted the boat and set it down again.

"There. Off the toe, at the third seam. Don't put the oar down over it."

Calder took the anchor hook and went down on his knees at the gunwale and put the steel into the weed and felt the shape the way you feel a man's ribs through cloth.

Not stone. Thin. Curved, and coming up out of the silt like the shoulder of a thing that had been made rather than grown, and under his hook the weed matted over an edge with a real thickness to it, three fingers at the fat part.

"It's a bottle," Bevin said.

"It's not a bottle."

"It's a pane of something."

"It's a pane of something, and there's a line under it, and the line is seized into it, and there's a whole anchor holding it down."

That was when Bevin Rook, twenty-two years old, said the sentence Calder would think about later, at three in the morning, for a long time.

"How big's the anchor?"

Calder leaned out and looked along the weed with the hook still in the silt. A fluke, a shank, a crown. Four or five feet of it, lying with its shank up and one fluke driven into the toe of the mole the way a thing gets driven when it comes in on a big tide and catches. His hand was already going out for the tape.

"Two fathom of shoal anchor. It came in on an earlier tide, off the inside ground or the old bar, and it's been sitting on the bottom of the mole ever since, and the glass came with it."

"If it's off the bar, it's the harbor's."

"It's nobody's until somebody proves it, and the harbor's tackle is the harbor's whatever it started as." Calder drew the hook back up. "It's bedded too deep for the crane. We can hook the fluke and shift it a foot, maybe two. If we start trying to lift it off the mole's toe with the tackle we have, we put the mole's face out."

"So we leave it."

"We tag it and leave it. It wants a proper lift with a chain and a team, and a tide that isn't this week's." He sat looking at the anchor in eight feet of green water with the glass tucked under it like a thing that had been put there and then covered. "Get me the small hammer and the cold chisel."

They worked on the mole steps in four-inch bites, the way you take a plate out of a fouled hull: tapping, then levering, then chipping the shell away from the edges. The glass was not stuck. It was held by the weed matting over it and a hundred years of silt and the fluke lying on top of it, and the fragments that came off its underside were black with rot and stuck to it like skin.

The glass was the wrong thing. That was the whole of it and he could not have said it better. In thirteen years he had handled window glass, bottle glass, Crown tableware, lantern chimneys, and the flat grey stuff that comes out of a burned hull. This was none of those. It was clear and thick, curved in a way that was not the curve of a bottle and not the curve of a plate, and at the fat edge there was a run of fine lines through the thickness, like the striations in a pane that has been worked rather than blown. When he lifted it out, the color of the basin came up through the glass and stood there in his hand.

And the line was under it. Not beside it. Under it, running from the anchor's crown into a turn at the fragment's edge, seized, the strands flattened and closed over one another where the glass had cut them.

"That's a ground line," Bevin said.

"That's a ground line, and it's seized into the glass, which means the glass was there when somebody tied it on. Somebody's boat, lying on her working ground, with this in her tackle and the anchor in her ground tackle, coming in together on the same tide."

"You could cut it off."

"I could. In a month I couldn't tell anybody whose line it was, and in a year the whole of it would be a shard of glass in a box with a guess on it." He wrapped the line into the fold of the fragment so it could not pull loose, and slid the whole of it inside his coat against his chest, and the cold of it went through his shirt and stayed.

It was half past three and the tide had begun to come up the mole with a slow push, and the anchor was under eight feet and going to nine. Calder had the fragment on the steps and the pry bar in his hand, and he stopped.

He was never sure afterwards that he had meant to do it. His hand was up and the heel of the chisel was coming down on the edge of the glass, going to knock the last of the shell away and get his hand under it, and the chisel went through the shell and hit the glass, and the glass said something.

It was not a loud noise. That was the part that stayed with him. It was the note you get when a piece of glass drops from a hand's height onto a hard bench, except it did not die. It went out across the mole and along the stone and into the harbour and came back, and it came back late. Not echoing. Late, by a hand's width of time, the way a note arrives from the other end of a working line when the line is longer than the man who pulled it.

Bevin looked round. "What was that?"

"Nothing. Somebody's bell."

The harbor's working bells were three streets away, the tow boat was not due, and there was no bell in the yard.

"Calder—"

"Haul the line in. We're off this mole before the set."

They got the boat up the slip and the fragment on the bench in the boathouse under a lamp. There was a lead line and a bone toggle at his belt that he used a hundred times a day, and he put his thumb in the crook of the toggle and pressed, and he waited, because it was possible that a sound like that wanted a weight held against it before it would come again.

It came again. Not louder. Half a beat after the first had finished, and quieter, and he felt it in the two fingers he had on the toggle and not in his ears, and the second time the note did not go out across the harbour at all. It went into him and stopped somewhere behind the bones of his forearm, in the place where a person keeps the sounds that came before they had words.

He put the fragment down and stood with both hands on the bench and waited for his hands to be of some use to him.

The crack in the housing had taken his whole morning, the chain had taken his shoulder, and the note had cost him something he could not name or put on a slate. It was market day, he was eleven weeks into six on the chandler's note, and a crack in a housing is a thing that goes in a log or a thing that ends a career, and only one of those has a bottom to it.

Oren had been in the water thirteen years. Oren had been twenty, with quick hands, and a voice younger and clearer and more sarcastic than Calder remembered it being. There was no sound in any room, any street, or any bell in the Vesper Sea that was going to give Oren back.

He wrote on the slate, in the small figures he kept for records, the number of the seam on the mole's inner face where the fouling started, and the bearing of the toe at that seam, and the depth, and two distances from the mole head. Under it he wrote the word GLASS, and under that a line and the words UNCLAIMED, and he did not write the word that was in his head, which was his brother's name.

Then he got a strip of red rag and went back out onto the mole in the last of the light, and tied a marked lead line to the crown of the drifted anchor at eight feet down, and set a float on it, and knotted the rag to the float with the number of the seam written on it.

That was the whole of what a proper diver could do about a piece of iron four fathoms down in a mole's toe: leave a mark that says somebody knows it is there, so that when a proper lift comes, the proper lift knows what it is for. It did not touch the anchor. It hung above it in the dark water and waited for a tide and a crane and a crew, and Calder left it there and came back up the steps with his coat heavy against his chest.

Kest was on the mole head.

He stood at the rail in a reeve's coat that had not seen weather in three seasons, with his hands held in front of him and the wind coming round the mole and finding nothing on him to take. From the light behind him he had been there long enough to have watched the whole of it, and to have let it finish.

"Marr," he said. "Your boy has been shouting a great deal for a mole that is municipal ground."

Calder stopped on the top step. Below him the harbour lay out flat and grey with the evening in it, the market boats swinging at their moorings, and two streets away somebody ringing the tide bell, and it sounded exactly like itself.

"He shouted once."

"He shouted about a bell."

"There was a sound."

"There is always a sound on a mole." Kest did not move and did not raise his voice, and his voice was trained to be a thing you could not be angry at. "Before I decide what any of it is, I am going to tell you three things, and I would like you to hear all of them before you answer, because you will not get a better set of terms tonight."

"The first. There was a Registry courier on the west basin this afternoon asking after glass recovered on harbor ground. That is not a fishing matter and I am not going to pretend it is. The second. If you hold that thing past morning without a claim of some kind, it is Crown property by default, and I will have to write the word *unclaimed* in my own hand on a form, and after that it is not yours or mine or anybody's in this harbor, and I do not get to argue about it again. The third." He looked at the mole, and the water, and the float standing off the toe in the dark. "The moorings, the tackle, and everything else on this mole are the harbor's. If that iron out there came off a wreck, then somebody's wreck has come to my ground, and I will hold it, and I will hold it carefully, and I will want a paper that says so. Not tonight. Tomorrow, at the office, on the west basin, when the Registry woman has been and looked at it."

"And if I bring it up tonight?"

"Then you bring it up tonight, and it is in your hand, and it is in your hand without a claim on it, and you will not sleep well and I will not be the reason." Kest put one careful hand on the rail and looked at the shape under Calder's coat that was not a coat. "You had it under your coat before I was halfway across the mole, Marr. You did not think of me at all, which is a compliment to how little I have bothered you this year and an insult to the harbor. I would like you to decide which of those you would rather explain."

He turned and went up the steps at the pace a man uses when he wants to be the last one to stop. Calder stood on the mole head in the dark with the wind coming through his coat and the cold of the glass still against his chest, and understood, with the clarity that came to him only when he was tired, that he had about nine hours before any of it was his at all.
