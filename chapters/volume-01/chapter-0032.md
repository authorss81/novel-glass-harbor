# Chapter 32

*The Three Words in the Claim*

The four sheets were read in full at ten past ten on the Tuesday morning, and the room had to be made bigger twice before they were finished.

That was not a joke and the clerk was not smiling when he said it. There were nine people in the hearing room by the end of the first sheet and fourteen by the end of the third, because a hearing room on a quay in a free harbor has a door and a window and a stove, and word travels the length of the fish market in about ninety seconds. The fish market crowd came out into the road and stood in the cold under the window and looked up at the glass at the shapes, and by the time the seal line was read a man at the back of the room said the words *Court of Wreck Claims* to nobody in particular and was told by the man beside him to be quiet, and was not.

Kest did not stop it. Kest read all four sheets at the pace of a man reading a will, and he did not summarize one line of them, and at the end of each sheet he said, "That is the sheet," and the clerk said, "Entered," and Kest said, "Read it again," and the clerk read it again, and that is what a record in this business is.

---

The claim was made in the name of the imperial cutter *Vigil* and it was signed at the bottom of the fourth sheet by her captain, and the clerk read the signature line out loud because the rule in that room was that a name is read, not pointed at.

"Entered at Morrow Quay on the fourteenth of last month by the commanding officer of the imperial cutter *Vigil*, by her own hand, and countersigned by the officer of that vessel ashore."

Nobody in the room said anything. Calder had seen that sentence on the fourth of last month on a different piece of paper, a page long, asking for nothing. He looked at the naval officer in the corner, who had a man's coat on with a cutter's cut and who had read the record of the twenty-fourth without opening his notebook, and understood that the man in the corner was not the man who had brought the one-page request, and that the man in the corner was not going to be the man who carried anything else ashore, and that from this afternoon he was the voice of a piece of paper and nothing else would be asked of him today.

The claim described its ground in a single paragraph on the second sheet, and Calder had to stand and turn round and read it off the wall behind him, because the clerk had it on the table and Calder wanted to see it at a distance.

"The whole of the Gannet Shoal ground, from the inside shoulder of that shoal out past its outer marks, together with the mooring ground recorded in this vessel's own log, **including** the ground thereabouts, upon which is found a **continuous** construction of transparent material, **as it lies and as it was worked**."

He stopped there, because there were three words in it and he had known since half past four the evening before, on the quay, with the case not yet open, that there would be three words in it, and he had gone home and lain in the dark working out which three.

"I object to three words in the second sheet," Calder said, "and I want them read back before the claim is entered, and I want them entered in the record as my words and not the officer's, and I want the claim entered as filed with my objection on the same page and not on a page after it. The words are *continuous*, *including*, and *as it was worked*."

The clerk wrote the three words on the face of the second sheet itself, in his own careful hand, with the time against them, and read them back, and then the officer of the *Vigil* spoke for the first time in the morning, from the corner, without getting up and without opening the notebook.

"Name them," he said.

"They are named," the clerk said.

"I heard him name them," the officer said. "I am asking whether the yard has any other three words, because a party who objects to three words in a naval claim is a party who has read it, and I would like the record to say that the yard read four sheets of the vessel's claim from the first page to the signature line before it found a word of it in the second."

"Enter that too," Kest said. "It is a fair thing to say and it is in the yard's favor, and the yard is not going to be careful about a thing in its favor for the first time in three months." He looked at Calder. "Say the three words again, Marr, and then say them slowly, because you have been in this harbor for thirty years and I have watched you file a purpose with a sentence in it that you wished you had not written, and I would rather hear the words twice than have the second one be a different word."

---

"Take them one at a time," Kest said, "and take the claim itself after them, and Mr Officer, if you have an answer to any of them you may give it now and it will go in the record in your words, and if you have not, I am going to enter the claim as filed and enter the objection, and the two of them will lie on the page together for as long as the page exists."

"Enter the claim as filed," the officer said. "The vessel makes no argument about the ground. It describes ground, the description is its own, and a description of ground is not a thing a party may object to in a municipal hearing room, and the vessel will say more about the ground when the yard has finished with its three words."

"Then I am going to do the thing that has annoyed me all week," Kest said, "which is to rule on a document, and to rule on it in a way that leaves the ruling standing whichever way it goes.

"The claim is entered as filed on the fourteenth of last month, in the words of its own second sheet, with its seal and its signature line, and it is a claim of the imperial cutter *Vigil* over the ground that second sheet describes. That is my ruling and I am not going to narrow it, because I have no power to narrow another office's claim and because a reeve who narrows a naval claim in his own hearing room is a reeve who has been asked to.

"And to that I enter, over my own signature, the three words of the Free Keel yard's objection and the three reasons, and the record of this sitting shows the claim and the objection on one page and the objection dated and the claim dated, and no clerk in this office or in Veyr may read one of them without the other being on the same sheet of paper under it. If you cannot arrange that in your ledger, Mr Clerk, I will write the sheet out again myself and you may copy it."

"It can be arranged," the clerk said.

---

The reasons took until one o'clock, and by the end of them the clerk's hand had gone slow and was being waited on.

"Continuous," Calder said. "Not a word about a join, and I want that noticed, and I want the notice to say that I noticed it and said nothing. But the word is about a thing, and the thing is this. We have three places on a bearing where the bottom comes up and nowhere else on two miles of a course the harbor authority itself sounded. One of the three is a bedded seam under a mole in nine feet of water. One is a transparent keel with a continuous line of its own in it, which is a different use of the word and was never mine. And the third is one figure out of thirty-six on a flat, and the flat is clay, and a foot and an inch in thirty-six is a stone or a spar with the bottom out of it, and I have said all of that in public on the market wall in my own hand and I am saying it again here so that nobody has to guess what I claim.

"Nobody in this room has put a hand on any of it. The word *continuous* describes a thing we have measured the top of, and I would like it entered that no person in Morrow Quay or in Veyr has any instrument that will tell us what is under sixteen fathoms of water at two miles and a quarter from this mole, and that the reason there is no such instrument is a paper that came to this office on the fourteenth of last month with a man in a cutter's coat on my mole. So the word is not a lie. It is worse than a lie. It is a word that costs nothing to write and nothing can be done with it for a year."

The officer said, without opening the notebook, "The vessel makes no claim to the material. The vessel claims the ground."

"I know what the vessel claims," Calder said.

"**Including** the ground thereabouts," the officer said, and read it out again. "Mr Marr, the vessel does not know where its mooring ground is in your own words. It knows where its mooring ground is in its own log, and the log is a public document in the inquest record, and this office has a copy of the mooring entry, and I have put the mooring entry in front of the reeve in this room on the twenty-fourth of last month and he read it aloud. Do you want to hear it read again, or do you want to object to a description of ground that a public document supports?"

"Read it," Calder said, "and I will tell the room what I object to, and it is not the distance."

The clerk read it. It was one line and it was in an officer's hand from thirteen years ago and it gave a distance from the shoal and a bearing off the shoal and the words *over old ground*, and it did not give a position in any unit that a person could go and stand on, because a position is a place and what the log gave was a relationship between the vessel and a place that no longer existed in those terms.

"Thank you," Calder said. "I have got no objection to that line. My objection is to the word *including*, and it is that the only description of the ground in this room is the one the vessel has given of itself, and that if the ground is described by the claimant's own account of the claimant's own position, then there is no description in existence that any other office can test, and the harbor authority's own chart cannot be compared with it, and the harbor authority's own stopped sounding cannot be compared with it, and a clerk in Veyr will have four sheets that cannot be laid beside anything. If the vessel will produce the sheet it used in thirteen years ago — the sheet the log's line was laid down on, with its compass and its hour — then there is a description of the ground that two offices can check and the word *including* is doing the work it is meant to do. I am not asking the vessel for its log. I am asking for the paper under the log."

"Requested," the naval officer said, and wrote it in the notebook, and added without looking up, "and the vessel will consider it."

---

"And *as it was worked*," Calder said, and his voice went slightly wrong on it and he let it, because there was no version of the sentence that did not have Oren's name somewhere underneath it and he was not going to say Oren's name in this room. "That is the hardest of the three, and it is a word about a working history, and I object to it because the *Vigil* did not work that ground. She lay on it. She lay on it for one tide, in a blow, in a cutter's boat, on a wreck that was another party's, and she recorded where she lay and she went away. Nothing on that ground was made by that vessel and nothing on that ground was touched by that vessel, and there is no history of working on that ground that begins with a cutter, and if the claim's phrase is read in a court in Veyr in three years' time by a man who has never been to this harbor, he will read a vessel that laid a line of glass across two miles of a clay flat and worked it, and the only thing that will correct him will be a crew that has been dissolved by then.

"So the yard objects to *as it was worked*, and says in its own words that nothing in this harbor's records shows any working on the ground described, by any person, and that the yard has said in its own book that it does not know what that ground is for, and that no person in this harbor has been asked."

The officer wrote for a while and then said, "Entered, and the vessel notes it."

"It is noted," Kest said, "and I am going to rule, and the ruling is that all three words stand in the claim as filed and all three objections stand in the record over my signature, and that this office makes no finding on any of them, and that the word *noted* in a naval record is not a concession and is not a denial and means only that the vessel has read the words, and if any of the four offices downstream of this one treats it as a concession I will produce this page."

---

Then the officer asked for the market wall, and Calder had to say no to a thing the crew wanted him to say yes to.

"The three sheets went up on the second of this month in the yard's own hands," the officer said. "Three points and two distances and a difference of ten yards, and no line drawn between them by the reeve's ruling, and the yard has stated on them that the purpose is not known to it. The vessel does not object to a statement of ignorance. The vessel asks that the sheets come down, and that the description of the ground in the claim be posted beside them, so that a man in the fish market is not reading three figures off a wall with no document of any kind above them. And the vessel asks — not this office — that the Free Keel yard produce the ten printed copies of its public record, so that the vessel may read them, and enter in its own file what it has read."

"Take them down," Calder said.

Nessa Pike said, "What."

"Take them down." He turned round to the room. "I have been three days deciding that the three points were the yard's only leverage and they are not. They are the only leverage until somebody competent takes them apart, and a man with a notebook in the corner has been sitting in this room for two days taking notes, and when the yard's figures and the vessel's claim are on the same wall in the same town for one week, every man who can read will read them both, and the reading will not be ours. I want the claim on the wall. I want the claim over the top of our sheets, in the vessel's own words, with a seal on the nail, and I want our sheets to stay on the wall underneath it where a man has to lift the corner of the Navy's paper to get at ours."

"That is the worst of both," Nessa said.

"It is the worst of both," Calder agreed, "and it is the only shape of the thing that is honest, and I would rather be the worst of both than the best of one. Rook — the sheets go under it tonight, and you take them down yourself and you put them back yourself, and you do not fold them, and you write on the back of each one the date and the hour and that it was moved by the yard's own request and not by any office, and you do that in your own right hand and you read it back to me before you put the nail in."

"Aye," said Bevin Rook.

"And the ten copies," the officer said.

"No," Calder said.

The room did not expect that from him either.

"The ten copies stay in the ten sheds they are in," he said. "Two on the market wall beside the original, three on other crews' walls, and five rolled in oilcloth in a chest that is pledged to this office and comes back to us on the fourteenth. The yard will not give them to the vessel and the yard will not give them to the circuit office and the yard will not give them to the Court of Wreck Claims, and if the vessel wants to read them it may send a man to the hearing room at a time this office fixes, and he may read all ten here, at this table, one at a time, in daylight, with the clerk, and he may take such notes on them as the law allows a man to take on a document that is not his.

"And I would like the room to understand that this is not me refusing the vessel a document. It is me giving away the only thing this yard owns. There are ten copies and a book and a wall and a chest with a saw kerf in an iron shackle in it, and after tonight the ten copies are in ten sheds where any man in this coast who cares to knock can read them, and there is nothing in this hearing about a place on this coast that has not been told. I would rather be a yard with a public record and no leverage than a yard with leverage and a sealed book, and I would rather say that out loud in a room than be the man who says it in March when somebody wants the copies back."

"Stated in the record," Kest said.

"And one thing more, and then the vessel can read the ten." Calder turned to the clerk. "Put in the record that the third of the three points is station six of this office's own annual course, and that the yard did not have it before the second of this month, and that the reeve let the yard read one figure out of one book in this office's barge on the second of this month, in daylight, with the clerk in the room, and would not let it be copied, and that the figure is now on a sheet on the market wall in the yard's own hand. The vessel is welcome to read that sheet and welcome to say that the figure came out of a book this harbor authority stopped sounding on the fourteenth of last month, and it is true, and the yard will not argue with it, because the figure is the office's and the office gave it and the office is in this room."

"Entered," the clerk said, and the officer wrote nine words and shut the notebook.

---

Just before two o'clock the naval officer of the *Vigil* made two motions, and Kest heard the first of them and did not hear the second, and the reason he did not hear the second was that the clerk had to go and find the harbor authority's own book on the fifth-seam sighting and could not find it in under three minutes, and everything in that room understood that the entry in that book was the reeve's own hand and the date on it was the third of this month and the words in it were *seen, not measured, not touched* and the three names of the witnesses who had been standing on the steps when he wrote it.

Kest took the first motion.

"That the harbor authority strike from its own record the entry made on the third of this month at the fifth seam, on the ground that it was made by the officer of this office in breach of a notice served by this office on the thirty-first, and that the observation of an officer made in his own breach is not the observation of this office." He did not look at Calder while he said it. "I will hear that on Thursday morning at ten, in this room, and I will hear it before anything else, and I am not going to hear it at the end of a day."

"That is two days' notice," the officer said, "on a Tuesday."

"It is a notice and it is a date and you have it," Kest said. "Go and read your vessel's file."

Then he turned round to the room.

"The second motion is not heard, and I want the room to understand the difference, because this office writes down the difference for a living. Not heard is not refused. A thing that is refused is a thing somebody may read in Veyr in a year and enjoy, and the ground of that motion is the same ground as the ground of the first one, and the vessel is not going to put the same argument into this office's record twice, and the book was in the second drawer and not the first. Mr Clerk will have the book on this table at half past two and the vessel may renew the motion if it wants it heard, and I will hear it on Thursday with the other one, and I would rather have both of them in the room on the same morning than in this office's book on two different mornings."

He looked at the clock above the stove.

"This room stands adjourned until half past two. And there is one matter I have not taken and am going to take at the end of the day, and I am telling the room now that it is coming, because the only thing worse than a reeve who surprises a room is a reeve who does it on purpose, and because a yard that has four days to answer an objection is a yard that has four days and I am not going to rob it of an hour of them to satisfy my own curiosity."

The clerk got a hand behind the stove rail to stop it going out, and nobody in the room got up to go out, because a reeve who has told a room in advance that something is coming at the end of the day has just made a market lunch very expensive.
