# Chapter 23

*The Number at the Ladder*

The job came off the board at ten past six on Friday the 28th because Bevin Rook was at it before the ink was dry, which was the fourth morning in a row and which Nessa had stopped pretending not to notice.

"Mole," he said. "Third and fourth seams. Fouled ring and a shackle to clear, and the toe to be looked at where the mole's own lead keeps coming up short."

"Who wrote it." Nessa had her coat on.

"The office. Eight for a morning, on the board, in chalk, with the harbor's own tide-gang on the mole and the wages for them on the office and not out of ours. Inside the line. It is the harbor's own ground and the harbor's own work and there is not a Registry word anywhere in it."

"And a boat." Calder said it because it was the whole of the problem.

"And a boat," Bevin agreed, "and air, and we have neither, and the air has to be bought before the job starts because a pot with a third of it gone has no margin in it whatever, and a man cannot spend air he has not got."

"So the harbor has a fouled ring it cannot clear, a mole lead coming up short, and a crew on its board that cannot get to it," Calder said. "Go and tell Sella Brigg that before the board fills up."

Sella came at half past seven with her own slate under her arm and the second boat of the *Low Lantern* round at the end of the yard, and she did not look pleased and she was not sorry either.

"Six marks of air," she said. "Out of the chandler's, this morning, against the job. And it comes off your share of the eight and not out of the iron and not out of the crew's food, and it goes on my slate in my hand with the hour on it, and if the job goes wrong you will owe me six marks and I will wait for them the way I wait for the third, which is until somebody can pay me and not one week before."

"Agreed, and I will put it in the book tonight and you may read it."

"I have read your book. I have read it four times since the twenty-fourth because Sella Brigg does not lend six marks of air to a yard on a story." She wrote on the slate and turned it round so Nessa could see it and Calder could copy it. "And Marr — the *Low Lantern* is on the water for one tide. Not two. My master will take her out in a southerly forecast on Friday and he will be wrong about it once and he will be right about it about four times a year, and this is one of the four."

That was the whole of it. Nobody thanked anybody, which is how that works.

---

The first thing that went wrong was not the water.

Nessa had the boat's position on the slate before anybody was in the water, because the position was the thing that killed people and not the tide.

"You want the inner face," Calder said.

"I want the inner face and I am not going to have it." She did not soften it. "The inner face at eight feet is a sheet of glass when the flood is on and a man can see a mile off the bottom of it. It is also the only face in this harbor that throws the sea back at a diver. When the west swell has anything on it, it comes in over the mole, hits the rubble, and comes down on the toe as a reflected chop that has no surface and no order to it, and a diver at eight feet against that face has not got a way off the line. He has got a bell, and a lead, and a reflected sea, and about a foot and a half of water over the place where he would want to be."

"Then we lose the look."

"We lose the look. We take it from the outer face in nine and ten feet with a quarter mile of clear water between the diver and the pile heads, which is a look I would bet my own hand on, and we get mud instead of glass, and we come up and I have a diver and a boat and nobody lost." She put the pencil behind her ear. "You have been down on a shoal in eight fathoms with a silt curtain for four minutes and you have decided that clear water is a comfort. Clear water is a comfort *away* from a wall. Against a wall it is a courtesy the wall pays you out of your own lungs."

Bevin Rook was standing at the boat with the slate round his neck and the lantern in his hand and a face on him that Calder had not seen since the sixteenth of September.

"She is right," Bevin said. "You are not going, so I am, and I would rather be told by her than find out in nine feet."

"Outer face," Calder said. "Write it down. I asked for the inner face and I was refused and I am not going to argue with a woman about water in front of the man who is going into it."

Nessa wrote it down and read it back.

---

The *Low Lantern*'s second boat was a fifteen-foot open boat with a bench forward and no more, and she was heavy with another man's gear, and Calder put her on the mole's outer side at half past eight with the harbor's tide-gang standing on the wall above them in blue, because the harbor's own men were on the harbor's own ground and Kest was among them with his book under his arm and his coat buttoned against a north wind.

The fouled ring was at the third seam in eight feet and it took forty minutes to clear, and it came up with a good half fathom of wire weed and a shackle that was not cut and not broken but *opened*, which Bevin noticed on the deck and put in the book before anybody had asked him for it, with the hour, and which Kest came down and wrote into his own.

Then they shifted along to the fourth seam for the toe, because the mole's own lead had been coming up short there for two years and nobody could say why, and a fouled ring is the best job a harbor ever has, because it is a thing that is definitely there.

Calder took the tender's cleat on the bench forward with the line over the sheave and his left hand on the toggle, and the two hundred-pound pump they had hired from the harbor hummed on the mole wall with a man minding it, and Bevin went over the side at nine with the lead line and a slate in a canvas pocket and a hand lamp under his arm.

"Nine on the call," Calder said. "Nine goes in the book and you say it at the ladder every time."

"I know what I say at the ladder."

The water at the top of the flood went to a sheet of glare and stopped, and through it the bottom came up visible in the flat calm under the mole, and Calder could see, at eleven feet, a boy in a canvas pocket picking his way along a rubble toe with a lamp in his teeth, and the pale glare in his eyes.

Bevin was gone for nineteen minutes.

He came up the ladder with weed in the beck of him and the slate wet in his hand and the look of a man who has seen more than he can say and knows the arrangement and has read it in the last fortnight.

"Bottom is rubble and old stone," he said. "Six feet of small stuff over hard stuff, and the hard stuff is the mole's foundation, and there is a seam of it running out from under the toe to the northwest about a foot and a half high, and it is not stone."

He stopped there. He took a breath and went on.

"Four foot of it, maybe five. Pale. The same pale as the shoal, and I am not going to call it anything else because I am on the ladder and you are on the cleat. It runs out from under the mole's toe, and it does not go out to sea. It comes in *under* the mole and runs along it, north and south, under the rubble, and I can follow it north for about two boat lengths and then the rubble is on top of it and I can see the edge of it and no more."

"Keys," Calder said.

"Three that I could see, and the second one is cut, not broken, and I have got my hand on it, and it is going nowhere." Bevin held up his right hand and put it flat and turned it over. "And Calder, it is not lying there. Nothing in this harbor is lying there that pale. That is bedded into the foundation, and there is three feet of the mole's own stone piled on top of it, and whoever put it there did it before they threw the stone."

Calder wrote nothing for a moment. He had the pencil up and the slate on his knee and the boy standing in nine feet of water on a ladder with his hand open in the cold, waiting to be told whether the number was right.

"Bevin," he said. "Depth at the north end of what you followed."

"Ten feet, maybe ten and a half, at the two boat lengths. It was still going deeper and I did not go after it."

"And at the third seam?"

"Nine, near enough, off the same number I gave you at the mole's own mark."

"Ten and a half at the fourth seam and nine at the third, and a mile of nothing out past the outer marks that we are not allowed to sound." Calder wrote it down in the order it had been said, and he wrote the tender's number and not the boy's, and then he wrote under it: *Counted by Rook, stated at the ladder, nine, ten, three keys, bedded, north to two boat lengths and no further. Diver's own count not given, tender's count entered.*

"Read it back," he said, because it was the rule the yard worked by, and because he wanted to hear whether the boy would put his own number in.

Bevin read it back, all of it, word for word, and at the end he said: "And the diver's own count was seven and eleven and there is no rule that says you have to put a man's number in when the man is standing on the ladder in front of you."

"There is now. I have just written it, and if you have a better number than mine you may say it out loud, and if you say it out loud it goes in as yours and I do not get to argue with it after." Calder turned the slate round. "But it goes in as the tender's count unless you say it, because the tender's count can be checked by another man on this mole and yours cannot. That is the whole of the arrangement and it is not about me."

Bevin Rook stood on the ladder in nine feet of water at the bottom of a mole in a free harbor and considered the whole of the arrangement, and then he said: "Seven and eleven."

"It does not go in."

"It does not go in," Bevin agreed, and got his other hand on the ladder, "because you are right, and I have not got a way to make it checkable, and that is the thing I do not like about the job."

---

The mole's chain alarm went at twenty past ten, four bells in a burst, which is the sound the harbor uses for a mooring let go and which Calder knew, and the flat note came up out of the bottom under it a third of a second later and said its three marks and the flick, and Calder's head began to turn before he had decided anything at all.

His hands were busy. Both of them. The left was on the toggle and the right was holding the fall clear of the sheave because the swell had come up and the boat was lifting, and he could not stop the turn, and there was nobody to stop it, because the one person in that boat who could stop it was nine feet down.

Nessa's hand came down flat on his shoulder.

"Alarm," she said. "Four bells, the mole's own chain alarm, from the wall, and it is the second burst since we came and I have been counting them. That is me telling you. I am not asking you whether you heard it."

"That was—" Calder stopped, because the first word that had come was the word that lived at the end of a boy's call in a fog, and it had come out of his mouth in front of the harbor's tide-gang, and the last time that had happened it had made a woman go to the far end of a platform and hold a mooring post with her back to him.

"That was what," Nessa said.

"That was the second burst."

"It was the second burst," she said, "and I have it written on the back of my hand, and if it was anything else you will say so at the bench tonight and not here, and I have not got time now. Water."

He put the pencil down and got the pump's stroke and the sheave and the boat's way on, and he did not turn his head again for the rest of the tide, and it was the hardest working of the morning.

They were clear at eleven with the ring and the opened shackle on the mole wall and the lead line in, and the clerk came down and paid the eight in coin on the mole steps while Kest stood beside him with his own book shut and the fouled weed in a heap and did not look at the figure on the slate that said *seam of pale material, bedded in the mole's foundation, running north under the rubble to two boat lengths.*

"You will not have that entered," Kest said, before Calder had asked.

"I was going to ask you to enter the toe, and the depth, and the fact that the mole's own lead has been short there two years."

"Yes. I will enter the toe, the depth, and my own lead's figure, in my own hand, in my own book, because it is my ground and my own lead." He put one careful hand on the top of the mole wall. "And I will not enter the words pale material, and I will not enter bedded, and I will not enter the words bedded in the mole's foundation, because you have not put a hand on any of it and neither has any man in my office, and because if the *Vigil*'s officer asks me what the Free Keel yard found in my mole on Friday, I am going to have to be able to say that a man looked at a bottom in ten feet of water and told me what he thought he saw, and that is a very different sentence from a finding."

"That is correct."

"I know it is correct. I did not say it because I enjoy your face." He put the book under his arm. "And there is the other thing, and I will say it on the mole because you cannot mishear it on a mole. The office will not sound within four hundred yards of the outer marks, and I do not care that the water in front of me is inside that, because the officer's paper says the ground and the water over the ground, and when the two of those are read in a room in Veyr the room will be in Veyr and not here. If you have taken a bearing or a depth anywhere out past that line this morning, tell me now and I will write it down as a thing reported to me and not adopted by me, and it is the last time I can do that for you without writing something else."

"I have not," Calder said. "Nine and ten feet at the third and fourth seams, inside the line, on our own ground, and every man in your tide-gang watched two of them."

"Good." Kest went up the mole steps. "I will hold the hearing on the seventh. It is not the tenth, it is the seventh, and I have moved it because the office is going to be busy on the tenth and I would rather have you in the room on a day I have got."

It was Bevin who asked the question at the boat, with the lantern hooked over the painter and the slate already dry in his pocket.

"How far's the next one," he said. "You said out past the outer marks there's a straight with eleven keys on it and the permit let you two dates and you used them. So the next one is out there, and out there is the officer's water, and we cannot go."

"The next one is a figure in a book in the reeve's office," Calder said, "and on Thursday morning I am going to stand at that table and read it with the clerk in the room and write it out in our own hand, and Bevin — it is the only number in the whole of this that is not ours, and if the three of them come out a mile apart then there is a line, and if they do not then there is a shoal, and I do not know which and I am not going to guess in front of a man holding a pencil."

The six marks of air went to Sella Brigg at eleven o'clock and the two marks that were left after rope and food went into the tin that was not the bond tin, because there was no bond tin, and Calder wrote the whole of it into the crew's book at the bench with the door open.

*28th. Mole, third and fourth seams, harbor's own work, inside the line, harbor tide-gang on the wall. Fouled ring cleared, shackle opened not broken, entered by Rook and by the office. Toe looked at: bedded pale seam of material, three keys seen, running north under the rubble to two boat lengths, deeper at the north end. Rook on the bottom. Marr on the tender's cleat and did not turn his head for the whole tide and was told the bell by Pike. Tender's count entered, diver's own count declined and not entered. Air bought of the chandler at eight, six marks, against Brigg's slate of six. Eight paid. Two out in rope and food. Six to Brigg at eleven o'clock, her slate read, copied into this book, and no week forgiven. Air pot a third gone and six marks bought today, and the pot is still a third gone.*

*First point.*

And Bevin Rook read that back from the book, standing, both words of it, twice, because that was his job now, and it was the first time in eleven years that anybody in that yard had written the word *first* beside a number.
