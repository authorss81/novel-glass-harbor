# Chapter 49

*What a Record Can Carry*

The sheet went up on the market wall on Saturday the seventeenth of December at four in the afternoon, in three parts, in four hands, with a rule under each part and the limits written inside the rules, and it went up underneath the cutter's claim of the eighth of November with the yard's own corner of it slipped four inches so that a man had to lift a naval document to get at it, which was the arrangement and had been the arrangement since the ninth of November.

Part one was the plate. Not the plate in the custody room — a sheet cannot carry an object — but the permit of the twelfth of October, the labor log of that date with the harbor authority's countersign and the stamp on it, the depth of eight fathoms at the shoal's inner edge, and the naval salvage claim as filed on the fourteenth of October at ten-forty in the morning, described in the yard's own hand as running from the inside shoulder of that shoal out past its outer marks.

Part two was the compartment. The survey's ninth page, eight foot four by six foot nine clear and eleven feet two deep under the grating, number four, port side aft. The coaming in a plate of ferry iron lying in nine feet of water on harbor ground inside the harbor line, measured with a rule in the hand, three foot nine by two foot four clear. The grating out of its seat and in its seat, worn bright on the inboard side only. And the public manifest's phrase, which the sheet quoted because a record that quotes the thing it is arguing with is worth four sheets that only quote itself.

Part three was four lines under a rule, and they were Nessa Pike's, and Bevin Rook read them out to the market twice before they were moved from the bench to the wall.

*This is a record of three places and a plate and a hatch. It is made by a crew with no conservator's name on it and no reeve's name on it and no claim on the ground, and every figure in it can be checked by a person standing in a boat with a lead and a rule and about two hours, and the plate was lifted under a permit this harbor issued and the hatch was measured in the hand and both are entered in the harbor authority's own book with the hour.*

*No office has ruled on any part of it. The harbor authority has made no finding and the circuit office has been asked for none. The office of the circuit has custody of three items and has given a reason for none of them.*

*What this sheet does not show: what was in standing space number four. Whether the object recovered on the eighth of December holds the testimony of the man it appears to hold. What is under sixteen fathoms of water at two miles and a quarter from this quay. Why a transparent mineral of undetermined origin recovered on harbor ground is presumed Crown material.*

---

There was no money in the yard to print it — the five marks in the bag were spoken for before the sheet was dry, and nobody pretended otherwise — and the four crews the copies went to did more with it in a fortnight than a print run would have done in a year.

Elias Voke took a copy down to the herring fleet's skippers in the road and left it with the man he said had the most boats in him, and Harl Dace of the *Grey Widow* took one to four crews at the net loft and read it out loud in a room with coils on the floor and did not read it as an argument, and Sella Brigg read it at the head of her own boat and said two things about it that were not in it, and one of them was that the crew that made it was the crew that took nine marks with a hand already caught, and the other of them was that the *Low Lantern* would want the sheet in her own book and was not going to ask for it, and Calder Marr gave her a copy and she wrote the date on the back of it in her own hand and gave it back to him to write the hour.

Mara Venn came at half past two that afternoon, read the whole of it, and certified the method in a Registry hand on the back of the third part.

"That is a thing I can do and it is the last of the standing I have," she said. "I am not certifying the ground and I am not certifying the objects and I am not certifying the origin of anything. I am certifying that the fouling and the lay and the seizing on the material recovered on the eighth were read under a lamp and written down in the order they were read, and that the depth of the plate was taken by a lead off a boat and the depth inside the coaming was taken with a rule in the hand, and that the hours are the hours.

"And I am doing it for nothing, and I would like that on the back of it in your own hand, Mr Marr, because in about three years a man in a room is going to look at a Registry hand on a sheet nailed to a fish market wall and ask what it cost, and the answer is going to be nothing, and I want the answer to be in your writing and not in mine."

---

The paper from the circuit office came on the Tuesday the twentieth in an inside envelope with the circuit's stamp on it and it was addressed to the harbor authority and marked for the attention of the tide conservator, and it was not a request.

It was an entry, in the office's own hand, dated the twentieth of December, and it re-entered three items in the custody log of the Morrow circuit office from *seized; transparent mineral of undetermined origin; presumed Crown material until a recognized witness or a municipal claimant is entered against it*, to *seized; municipal claim entered by the Free Keel yard of Morrow Quay, of this date; contested; not to be handled, not to be opened, and not to be removed from the custody room of this office*, and the three numbers were on it, one to a line, and under all three of them the same seal line as every other paper this office had sent to this harbor since the fourteenth of October.

*Magister Ilya Serr, senior Registry examiner.*

That is the whole of the paper. It is four lines and a seal line and a date, and it gives no reason, and there is no ground of appeal on it, and it does not ask the harbor authority for anything, and the harbor authority has not asked for anything.

Kest read it out in the hearing room on Wednesday the twenty-first at eleven, which is three weeks after the sitting, and the room was not full, and the naval officer was in his corner, and Bevin Rook had the book.

"I am going to read this paper out and then I am going to say four things about it, and the first three are the reeve's and the fourth is a fact about a file and I would like the room to know which is which.

"One. The order of the fourteenth of October, which took two objects off a Free Keel yard's bench at half past six in the morning, is the only instrument this office has ever been served with out of that room in four months, and I have read its ground four times and it says that a transparent mineral of undetermined origin recovered on harbor ground is presumed Crown material *until a recognized witness or a municipal claimant is entered against it*. The yard entered a municipal claimant on the thirteenth of this month. On the eleventh of November a woman in a Registry coat told three men and a boy with a sling at a bench in a working yard that a recognized witness is not a person, it is a document, and that an office enters one. So the condition in that order has been satisfied, on paper, by a municipal claim, on the day before the office wrote to us saying it has been.

"Two. The order has not been lifted. It stands. It is correct. It is not mine to lift and I have not asked anybody to lift it and I am not going to ask anybody to lift it before I have read the file, and the file is in a drawer in the west of this city and I am not a man in Veyr.

"Three. And this is the reeve's own part and it is the part I have thought about for three days. A conditional ground that has been satisfied and an order that has not been lifted is not a mystery and it is not an abuse. It is a form working exactly as it was drawn. Somebody in that office wrote a condition in October, and the condition has now been met, and the consequence of meeting it was written at the time by somebody who is not in this room, and what the consequence is has not yet arrived, and when it arrives it will not be a decision anybody takes about glass. It will be a piece of a claim that has been sitting in a drawer since the fourteenth of October being allowed to stand.

"Four, and this is not the reeve. This is a fact about a file and I would like a clerk to write it down. There is no paper in this office, or in the circuit office's copying room so far as I know, that states what a municipal claim does to a presumed Crown material. And there is no order in the file that has a ground for anything other than a presumption that expired on the thirteenth of this month. Whatever the reason for the seizure was on the morning of the fourteenth of October, it is not on any paper I have been served with, and I have been served with every paper that office has sent to this harbor, and I have kept them all, and they are in a bundle in a tin in this room with the dates on them in order."

The clerk wrote it down and read it back, and the naval officer said, "Enter the vessel's note, which is that the vessel has heard the fourth and does not comment on it, because the vessel is not the circuit office and the vessel has never seen the ground of the order either, and has never asked for it, and would not know what to do with it if it were given."

"Then the vessel and the harbor authority are two offices in this harbor that have never been told," Elias Voke said from the back.

"Two offices and a fisher," Kest said, "and I intend to keep the third one in the room, Mr Voke, because the third one is the reason this office is in the same position as it is."

---

And then the officer asked for the chart.

He asked for it in the ordinary way, in a room, with a notebook open, and the request was for the harbor authority's own chart of the basin and the inner ground with the yard's figures entered upon it or copied off it, and the reason he gave was that the vessel holds a claim over ground described on a shoal and would like to know what the harbor authority has marked on its own ground within a mile of it.

Kest said no.

He said it in eleven seconds and then he explained it, and the explanation was the fourth time in a month that he had said in a room a thing nobody had asked him to say.

"This office's chart is this office's chart. A party may bring a sheet to a sitting and read it on a table in daylight with the clerk, and did so on the seventh of November, and may not carry it out of the room, and the officer's man counted the sheets and wrote the hour on his own slate, and the vessel asked for none of them. The yard has figures on that chart. The yard did not put them there. This office's own boy put three of them there in chalk on a slate wall and took them off with a cloth in ten seconds on a Monday morning in November and put three of them back in the same places in chalk in eleven minutes and signed the corner. That is what this office has. The yard has a bag with nothing in it and a tin with nothing in it and a boy who is owed forty marks, and this office's chart is the only piece of paper in Morrow Quay that has a mole's face and a shoal and a mooring line and four places on it at once, and this office is not going to hand it to a vessel on a Wednesday because the vessel asked nicely and in the right words.

"And I am aware of three things. I am aware that a reeve who refuses a naval request four times in a year is a reeve who has made an enemy of the only office in this harbor that can put anybody anywhere. I am aware that on the tenth of November I kept a naval officer's sighting in my own book at the cost of nineteen winters of herring dues and I told this room about the price before I took it. And I am aware that the yard has not asked this office for that chart, and has no right to ask for it, and has not asked, and that I have not offered it, and will not ask, because a reeve's request is a pressure and a pressure is a thing an office is entitled to refuse without giving a reason, which is what I told this room on the seventh of November and which I am now using on myself."

"Enter the vessel's note," the officer said, to the clerk, and the clerk wrote it. "The vessel notes that the officer of this office has declined a request made in the ordinary way, has given his reason in his own voice, and has stated on the record that the party to whom the document is refused has no right to ask for it and has not asked. The vessel has no further request on this office today."

---

The other thing that happened in December happened on a mole head at half past three in the afternoon of the twenty-first, with the tide going, and it happened because the bellwright was standing next to the object on its cradle under a sail and arguing with Tobias Wren about the fouling, and Sera Mott came up the steps at her own pace with her basket and her own lamp as she had been doing since the seventh of October.

"There was more of them," she said, and stopped, and let the two men finish what they were saying, and then said it again. "There was more of them out there. I have not said so in this harbor to anybody in forty years and I am saying it to four people and a bellwright and a mole, and I would like the mole to be the reason, because a mole is not a man."

"Sera—"

"Listen to the two halves and do not put them together for me." She was looking at the object on its cradle and not at anybody. "There was one of them at the mole, and the mole has one, and I have been sixty-eight years old and I did not know it until a boy with a sling told the tide gang what came up on the sixth of December, and I am not ashamed of that, and I am not going to be told it is a wonder by anybody, because it is not a wonder. It is a bar of iron on a slope. It looks like the mole. It has always looked like the mole.

"And I have heard this one answered from the westward, and always late, on a bad night with a west in it, and I could not tell you in three hundred years who was answering, and I have asked two men from a city office over the years and both of them wrote it down and both of them came back and asked me the same three questions and I have said no and believed them, and the second one was sorry for me and I could see that he was sorry for me and I told him no anyway.

"What I will say is the other half, and it is the half that matters. The ones that answered it from the westward were kept. Somebody went out in a boat and kept them. And the ones I know about stopped, and the year they stopped is not a year I am going to say out loud on a mole, and the reason they stopped is that a man who goes out in a boat in a weather to keep a thing going is a man who is paid, or is a man whose trade it is, or is a man who has a brother on the far banks — and there was nobody on the far banks by the time anybody asked. Nobody made that up and nobody sat on it. It is a road with nobody on it."

She picked her basket up off the step.

"Somebody has to be looking after them," she said, "and there is nobody doing it, and a bellwright will tell you the same thing for nothing, and I have been asked by archivists and I have said no and I have believed them, and I am going to say no one more time in about two years, and I would like somebody to write down today that I said it and that I was not talking about a mystery."

And she went down the steps at her own pace with her basket, and Tobias Wren stood at the top of them with his hat off, which he did perhaps four times in his life.

Bevin Rook wrote nothing down, on purpose, and said so at the time, and Calder Marr told him not to, and the boy wrote *Sera Mott, twenty-first, at the mole head, at the object, said there was more of them and would not say more, and asked for nothing to be written, and it is not written* into the crew's book in his own right hand, which is the first time in four months that he has entered a thing that a person asked not to be entered.
