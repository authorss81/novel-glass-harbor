# Chapter 46

*Forty-One Minutes*

The clerk's estimate for Thursday the first of December came to four marks a day for the crane and the barge, and the air came to eleven, and Calder Marr did the sum on the bench with the door shut and got the answer he had known he would get.

"We hire the crane and the barge off the harbor at four a day for three days and the harbor takes the hire out of its own money, because the ground is the harbor's ground and the crane is the harbor's crane and the reeve signed the order for it without being asked, and I want that written down as what it is and not as a favor, because a favor is a thing that can be taken back and a hire is a thing that goes on an account."

"That is three days of a crane we are not paying for," Nessa said.

"Three days of a crane we are not paying for and three days of air we are, and the air is eleven marks, and the bag had nineteen on the twenty-eighth and three of that went to the copying room on the twenty-third, so there are sixteen, and sixteen less eleven is five in the bag and nothing in the tin." He put the pencil down. "And I am not going to the chandler for one mark of it on the first, because the note has had six on it in the three days of that job and nothing since, and it is written up in his book as eleven weeks into six and the two weeks I have put on it are mine and not his, and I will take it to him on the second of December and pay it off the first money that comes in and not out of a bag that cannot."

"Then it is five marks in this yard on the first of December and it is five marks on the sixth, and on the sixth we are going to be standing in a boat on a rising tide with a hand lead, and I want the number in the book in your hand and I want it read back before anybody puts a glove on."

---

The bellwright came at two o'clock on the first because Nessa Pike sent for him, and he had been the bellwright on the harbor's own outer buoy since the twentieth of October and had put a brass collar and a new tongue and a cotter through the eye on the clapper, and nobody in that yard had ever asked him a question in his life.

He was about fifty, with a blacksmith's forearms and a cold, and he looked at the plate for eleven minutes without touching it, and then he said, "That's ferry iron, and that's a stores hatch, and three foot nine is right for a stores hatch on a working deck and a crew's hatch is two foot square and a man knows it in the dark," and Calder Marr said, "What is the difference," and the bellwright said, "A stores hatch is a place a man carries things through. A crew's hatch is a place a man lives. You size them for what goes through them and not for what you call them."

Then Nessa said, "And the aperture."

The bellwright crouched at the plate and put his thumb beside the bevel without touching it, and the thumb did not go near it, and he was better at that than any of them.

"Sixteen hundred," he said. "Half-inch, sixteen hundred, four inches on the edge, set out from a template, and somebody with a chisel and a fair hand and no hurry. That is not a hole a man cut to get somebody out. That is a hole a man cut to get something in, and he cut it from the outside, and he had the plate on a beach or a ways and he had a frame, and the frame is gone."

"You can tell he had a frame."

"I can tell he had a frame because the bevel is even all the way round and a man cutting by eye does not get an even bevel on a curve. That is all I can tell and I will not go past it."

He stood up and looked at the mole.

"Which of you three is the one who hears the bell late," he said, and nobody answered, and he took the answer off the air and went on. "Right. Here is the other thing and I am going to say it once and then I am going to go home, and you can do what you like with it.

"A bell is iron. That is the whole of a bell. The sound is the iron and the shape and the thickness and the pitch of the iron, and every bell on this coast is iron because iron is what rings and stays in tune, and if you want a note that will carry three hundred yards and be heard over a wind you want good iron and a heavy wall and you want the note to be *even*, and that is the entire craft of it. There is nothing in a bell that a person can do twice.

"And then there is this." He put his hand out flat. "Set a chamber of worked glass in the waist, leaded in, four pieces, and you have a bell that does not carry three hundred yards. You have a bell that carries *further*, and it carries it *down*, and it will answer another bell that is standing in the same ground with a line between them, and it will answer it late, because the line is longer than the water and the line is what carries it. A glass in the waist is not decoration. A glass in the waist is what makes a bell a *signal* instead of a noise. And I have been asked four times in thirty years why it does that and I have told a Registry man once and a magistrate's clerk once and I told them the truth, which is that the glass is made to be heard in a ground that already has bells in it, and I do not know why that is, and neither do they, and neither of us is going to find out on this coast in our own lifetimes."

"So a bell answers another bell in the same ground."

"A bell answers another bell in the same ground, and it answers it in time, and the whole of the trade is in that, and it is not a mystery. It is a *system*, and a system is the opposite of a mystery, and a system has to be maintained by somebody going out in a boat in the weather, and that somebody is a man, and if the man stops going out then the bell stops answering and starts being a rock, and everybody on that coast stops knowing where the water is." He put his hand down. "Not the making. The going out. That is the whole craft and it is the one nobody wants."

---

Bevin Rook found it on Monday the fifth, at twenty past ten in the morning, from the mole steps at the south end, with the book under his arm, and he found it because the tide bell went and he did not have anywhere else to be looking.

He did not shout. He came down the steps at a fast walk and stopped in the middle of the yard, which is not a thing a boy with a hand in a sling does without a reason, and said, "There is a bar of iron on the slope off the south end, two boat lengths to the seaward of the plate, and it has a ring on the top of it, and it is not the mole, and I have looked at that mole every day for twenty-two years."

Nobody in the shed said anything for a moment.

"Go and get the slate," Nessa Pike said. "Say the hour, say the water, and say what you saw and not what you think it is, and if the word *bell* comes out of your mouth before I ask you a question about it I will take the book out of your hands."

At the top of the flood on the sixth, in the slop, it came up out of the glare as clearly as a plate on a bench.

It was a bar of iron about a hand's breadth and a half long lying along the slope, with a straight bar across the top of it and a ring at the middle of that, and the whole of it standing a foot and a half out of the new mole's rubble with fouled weed on it and a clean pale patch on the seaward side of the yoke where thirteen years of gravel had come off it every tide and had not been allowed to stay.

"A shackle," the bellwright said, from the bank above, and did not go down.

They rigged the crane and the barge at half past nine on the sixth and put Calder Marr over the side at the top of the make, and Nessa Pike had the tender's cleat, and Bevin Rook had the book and the air column, and Tobias Wren had the boat, and Neale's tide gang stood on the south end of the mole in two places and watched, because the reeve had written the date and the hour and the reason into his own book and had sent a man to see it done.

The reason went into the harbor authority's book at half past eight on the morning of the sixth, at the reeve's table, before the diver went in, in Calder Marr's own left hand, and the clerk read it back and Kest read it back and nobody in the room argued with it because there was no room. It said that the crew was going in to shift fouling and to get a chain under an iron object lying on a slope on this office's ground inside the harbor line, at about eight and a half feet, at the top of the flood, under a permit of the sixth of December, with a limit of forty minutes of air on the slate, a tender named who was not the diver, and a cutoff the tender could pull without asking anybody, and that the crew had no reason for wanting the object and had offered none, and that the office had not asked for one.

---

The fouling came off in slabs. That was the thing nobody had expected and it was the reason the whole job worked: the weed on that slope had gone down hard over thirteen years and had set into plates a good deal like the fouling on the *Alder Reach's* ground tackle, and a plate of it lifted off whole when the hook went under it, and underneath the plates the iron was clean and cold and yellow.

Bevin Rook read the air and the depth every ten minutes out loud in the flat voice and did not turn round, and at twenty minutes he read the number and then read the time, and neither of them was the number Calder Marr wanted.

"Twenty-two, and twenty minutes."

"Twenty-two goes in the book."

"Twenty-two is the number I read and it is not the number I would have given you if you had not asked, and you have not asked me twice today and I have not had to say anything about that, and I am writing *no pressure on the tender* in the entry and I am writing it in my own hand and not in yours, because it is my trade and I would like it in the book in the hand of the man who did it."

At twenty-nine minutes the hook went under the crown and Calder Marr got a shackle off with a spanner and put the fall on it, and at thirty-four minutes the barge was in position and the crane's hook was down and the chain was on and they came up together, and the object's own weight did most of it.

"Up," Nessa Pike said, in the flat voice, and nobody argued.

Forty-one minutes. The number was Bevin Rook's and it went in the book as the tender's count and Calder Marr said the same figure on the ladder with air in hand, and the four of them read it back in that order.

And on the way up, at the thirty-ninth minute, with the chain on and the crane hooked and the whole of the harbor standing on the south end of the mole in the cold, the tide bell went in the road.

Three strokes, clear, belonging to the harbor and to nobody else. And under the third of them, out of the bottom off the mole, a third of a second behind, even and very slightly too level to be anything that grows out of a harbor, the delayed note came up with its three marks and its flick.

And the object in the water under Calder Marr's right hand, lying along the slope in eight and a half feet with its shackle in the fall and its glass chamber four inches from his glove, answered it.

The answer came up through the iron and into the bones of his hand and up the chain and into the barge and it arrived a hand's width of a second after the third stroke, and it was even, and it was level, and it was not loud, and it stopped when the bell stopped.

Bevin Rook had the book open on the barge boards and had the pencil against the page and did not write for about four seconds, which was the longest he had ever been late.

"Forty-one minutes," he said. "Top of the flood, south end, the delayed note under the harbor's three and the answer out of the object on the bottom, the same second, the same three marks, no source, not written as a voice, and I want that read back before I put my name to it because it is the first time in four months I have written a line with two things in it instead of one."

"It is a bellwright's core," the bellwright said, from the bank, to nobody. "It is the working part of a bell buoy. It goes in a float and it is hung and it is struck and it is what you hear, and everything else on a buoy is a coat of paint. And it is a signal and it has been answering this harbor's bell every time that bell has gone for however long it has been lying there, and there is not one man on this coast who has ever thought of it as anything but a piece of the mole, because it *looks* like a piece of the mole, and that is the best disguise that ever went to waste."

---

It came up out of the water at eleven minutes past ten and it lay on the barge boards in the sun and it was the first intact thing the Free Keel yard had ever brought up in its life.

It was iron, forged, a hand's breadth and a half long, with a flat foot and a curved back, and a forged yoke laid across the top of it and a shackle in the middle of the yoke, and inside the arch a clapper on a gudgeon with a tongue that was half worn through, and in the waist, leaded in with lead gone white, four pieces of worked glass in a band, and the glass was clear, and it was not bottle glass and it was not window glass, and it was the same wrong thing Calder Marr had been holding under his coat on the eleventh of October and had not been able to put down for a fortnight.

"That is it," Mara Venn said.

She was standing on the south end of the mole with her book against her chest and she had not come into the water and she had not put her hand on it, and she had come because a conservator comes when a thing comes up out of a mole and there was nowhere else for her to be on a Tuesday.

"Mr Marr, I have been in this harbor four months and I have examined one object of transparent mineral in a custody room and one on a bench and I have refused to write a word about either, and I am going to say four things about that object and they are all that I can say.

"One. It is worked. Tideglass, worked, and not worked into a sheet and cut: worked into four pieces to be set in a frame, which is a bellwright's practice and not a yard's, and it is done to a curve that has been thought about. Two. The setting is a mechanical joint and a mechanical joint is not a consent, and I have said in a hearing room that a mark is not a witness and I am going to say it here to four men and a bellwright: this object has a claim seal on it in the ordinary sense of a maker's mark and *the making of it is not anybody's permission to use it.* Three. The glass in the waist is the only part of it that is a mystery and I cannot solve it and neither can he." She nodded at the bellwright. "And four. It is of an age that puts it outside anything I can date, and I am not going to give a ceiling, and I am not going to give a floor, and if the yard wants a number I have not got one and will not invent one to be useful at a hearing."

"What are the yard's limits then," Nessa Pike said.

"Two, and they are both mine and neither is a wall. It is not to be put into any shed in this harbor, and the notice of the thirty-first says that in so many words and the yard is barred from arguing with it. And it is not to be lifted again by any hand that has not been in the water with it, and that one is not the office's and it is the yard's own and I am recommending it and I am not the yard's adviser and I would like that read back to me as the second of the two."

"What is the first of the two in a shed."

"Mr Marr." Mara Venn did not raise her voice. "You have been in this harbor four months and you have wanted a shed since the eleventh of October, and the notice of the thirty-first closes the mole's face and it also says that no person shall take recovered transparent material into any shed in this harbor, and the yard has never argued with that clause and is not going to start this month. So the thing you have just raised is going to be hung, or it is going to sit in the open on a mole head and rust, and those are the two futures it has, and neither of them is a bench with a lamp on it and I would like everybody in this yard to understand that I have not come here to be an obstacle to a good piece of work. I have come to tell you what year the sea is going to be tidied up, and it is not this one."

---

The bellwright would not touch it at the top of the bank with the wind coming off the water and the rain starting, and he said the reason, and the reason was not sentiment.

"I will not open a bell in the wet," he said. "If you get water in a gudgeon you write the whole bell off, and if you get water in the lead between the glass and the iron you have four pieces of glass that go cloudy in a year and there is no way back from that but a furnace two hundred miles off, and that furnace is Crown work and costs more than this yard is worth. So it hangs as it is, with the tongue as it is, and it gets a shackle and a rope and a chain and a good bottom, and the fouling stays on it because fouling is not the enemy, a bell with a clean waist is a bell that has been handled by somebody who did not know what it was."

"And if the tongue is half worn through," Bevin Rook said.

"Then it will go over on its next hard stroke and it will not be a signal any more and it will be a rock," the bellwright said. "So you hang it and you listen to it and you time it, and when the time between the harbor's bell and the answer comes to be a different number than it is today, then the tongue is going, and you come and find me and I fit a tongue, and that is a day's work and it is a day's work every four or five years for as long as that bell hangs. Not the making. The going out."

Calder Marr looked at it lying on the barge boards in the rain, four pieces of worked glass in a band of lead in the waist of a piece of iron that thirteen years of gravel had kept clean on one side, and understood that he had spent four months listening to a sound come up out of the bottom off a mole and that it had been a bell under his own town the whole time, and that the reason no one had ever known was that it looked like a piece of the mole.

"Bevin," he said. "Read the entry back, all of it, and put the bellwright's name in it as a man who has said what it is and has not touched it, and put the hour on the two of us, and then read the twenty-nine lines out loud to the tide gang, because Neale's men were stood on that wall and they are going to have been told by somebody in the fish market by supper what came up out of the water this morning, and I would rather they heard the man with the book say it than a man with a drink."

And the boy of twenty-two, with his left arm across his chest in a sling and his right hand flat on the page, read forty-one minutes and a fouling and a fall and a chain and an answer and the words *not written as a voice* out loud to eleven men of the harbor's tide gang on the south end of the mole, twice, and the harbor authority's own clerk wrote the hour of it in the harbor authority's own book because the reeve told him to and did not tell him why.
