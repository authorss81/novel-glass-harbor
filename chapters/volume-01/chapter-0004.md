# Chapter 4

*What the Anchor Caught*

The bell hose had a split in it two inches above the clamp, on the inside of the curve, the kind you find by putting your thumb along the rubber and pushing.

Nessa had it in her hand before Calder had the helmet off, and she held it up so the last of the daylight went through the split and made it a black line in a grey length of hose.

"Where did you get this?"

"Off the reel. It's the last coil we had and it's been on the reel since the winter."

"Then we have one air supply and a hose with a hole in it." She put it down on the deck and did not look at him. "I can double it. Two lengths, two clamps, a board off the *Kittiwake*'s floor to carry the load, and it will be heavy and it will be awkward and it will work for one cycle. After that the split will be at the clamp and the split will be worse."

"How long has it got?"

"Once. Maybe once with a good clamp and somebody's luck." She got the second length out of the locker before he answered. "Write it on the log that we doubled it and that we ran it doubled."

"I know."

"I know you know. I want it in your hand, because in about an hour one of us is going to want to say the hose was fine."

They rigged it in twenty minutes in the failing light, and it was clumsy work, and it was the best work Calder had seen from anybody in a long while. Nessa ran the board down the length of the doubled hose and lagged it with marline so that the load went into the board and not into the rubber, and she lashed it so that a pull on the hose would not have put a twist into the board, and when Calder said the loop was a hand's width too tight she told him a hand's width was the difference between a hose that turned over and a hose that did not, and he let her have it because she was right.

Bevin took the boat and kept the bell's lead going over the sheave, and he read the air and the depth every ten minutes without being asked, and twice he read them twice because he was unsure and wanted the number to be right.

"Slack water at twenty past five," Nessa said. "You've got until the set makes the bottom run at the inner edge, and after that you will be holding on to a thing with your hands because your feet will do nothing for you."

"How long is that?"

"Fifty-five minutes if the weather holds. Twenty if it doesn't." She put her hand flat on the bell's crown. "Now. Tell me the boundary again."

"Marker line on the anchor shackle, running out along the rib's foot to a sinker at eleven fathoms off the shoal end. Past that line I do not go, and if I go past it you do not come and get me, you write the hour down."

"That last part is new."

"That last part is in your hand as of this morning, and you are going to hold me to it, and I am going to hate you for it." He got the helmet's neck ring and turned it twice. "Anything else?"

"Anything else is that the object came up in this fragment and this plate on the first day it was permitted to come up, and if you go out there and put a hand on the rib's outer end, then whatever the Registry takes off this deck at eight o'clock tomorrow is a piece of glass and no history at all, because there is no history in a piece of glass."

The bell went over at ten past five, and Calder went down inside it, and the harbor went out of the world in the order that it always did: first the color, then the size of things, then the sound, until there was nothing but the hose and the lead and his own breathing and the smell of wet brass.

The *Alder Reach* lay on the bottom at eight fathoms on a silt that took a boot print and kept it, which was the first thing that made him understand the site. Silt that takes a print is not ground you fight with a shovel. It is ground that holds a thing you have put down and gives it back when you pull.

He went along her frames on the lead, hand over hand, and the water was the color of weak tea with a fine cloud in it that would not settle because the least thing raised a fresh layer of it, and after every two minutes of work he had to stop and let it clear, and it never cleared as well the second time as it had the first.

The anchor was where the ground tackle said it ought to be, off her port quarter and a little aft of the frame that carried the repairer's mark, and it was bedded into the silt up to the crown with only the shank showing, laid over on its side, with the fluke down and the stock buried.

And under the anchor, going out to the west across the shoal on a dead straight, was the rib.

It was not iron. It was four feet of something the color of a wet bone, and it was taller than he had thought from the boat, and it ran out of the silt on the shoal's flat with the long smooth run of a thing that had not been dropped. It was not a plate. He could see that from ten feet off and it was the clearest thing he had ever seen in bad water.

The frames on it were not frames. There were fourteen of them along the *Alder Reach* and he counted them because counting is what you do, and the rib had its own, and they ran through it the way ribs run through a thing and not the way frames run through a boat.

He put the chalk on his slate and wrote the number and the time, and then he put the slate away and did the work, because the silt was not going to wait.

The first clamp went on at twenty-five past five, on a length of the rib's foot where the anchor's fluke came up against it and where there was a good shoulder of it. The second went on the shank itself, further out, four feet along.

The second clamp took the load.

The rib moved.

Not far. Two inches, maybe three, and it moved the way a heavy thing moves when the thing underneath it goes — down and slightly inboard, with the whole length of it settling a hand's width as though it were a length of pipe somebody had nudged. A wall of silt came off its upper side in a long slow curtain and stood in the water like a wall of smoke, and the bell's lead came up hard against Calder's shoulder and then slackened.

He held still for a count of ten and took hold of the bell's ring by the join in the casting, which he could find in the dark, and it told him nothing of himself at all, which was the condition for going back up and which was not the condition he was in.

The curl of it came off the silt, and the water went yellow and then went brown, and for four minutes Calder could not see his own boots and did not move, and in that four minutes he did the arithmetic that a diver does in a silt-out: two minutes of working water left before the bell's air would need changing, ten minutes before the bottom would be moving under him, and the work not done, and the plate not measured, and the fact that if the rib went over there would be nothing left of the *Kittiwake*'s people to be angry at.

At the fifth minute the water came back enough to see one hand's length. He took the second clamp off, which was the right thing to do and which cost him the whole of the rib's outer third as a subject, and he put his hand back on the first clamp to find out whether the clamp was still where it had been.

It was. The shoulder had held.

He took his measurements off the plate he could see, because the plate was there and the plate was the reason he had come, and he wrote them in the dark on the slate with a pencil stub in his cold hand, pressing hard enough to feel the point through the back: length along the rib, four foot two, and maybe eleven inches, which is a plate and not a panel; thickness at the near edge, three fingers; thickness at the middle, two; the run of a seam down the long side, and a second seam, and the way the two seams met at the corner with a key in it and a fillet of something grey running the length of the join.

He counted eleven keys along the long seam and wrote the number, and then he wrote under it, because the number meant nothing without it: *keel line, continuous, no break, no join, running west.*

Then he looked up the rib.

It went out into the brown water and faded at about the distance of a long punt's length, and there was a marker line on the bottom out there somewhere with a sinker on the end of it, and beyond that sinker the rib kept going, and the rib was a boat, and the boat was on a straight, and the straight went west.

Eleven fathoms. The number was in the permit and the permit was on the deck of the boat above him, and the four fathoms between the sinker and the point where the rib had gone out of sight were a distance he could have swum. That was the part he understood clearly and could not get past: it was not far. On any ordinary day of his working life he had gone further for less, and he had once walked a line out along a channel floor from a bell on a tank of air that four men had watched with their hands on the pump.

And above him, out of sight, on a boat that was his, Nessa had a hand on a plank and a lead line, and had written nothing down about a man who walked past a sinker, because that was not a thing you wrote on a slate. It was a thing you looked for on the beach afterward.

He took his eyes off the rib and went and got the anchor.

The sacrificial line went on the crown shackle, which was under nine inches of silt, and he cleared it with his hands because a hook in a shackle under silt is a hook that comes out with a piece of shackle in it. He got two turns on it, and hanked it, and brought the line up the short distance to the second clamp that he had taken off, and put the whole load on the one shoulder that had held.

It came.

It came the way bedded iron comes: not at first, then all at once, and with a sound that came up through the silt and through the bell's ring and into Calder's teeth, a grinding of shell and a thud, and then nothing holding it but the line and the water and a hand's worth of work keeping the angle right.

The anchor came up into his lap and fouled the silt, and Calder put both arms round it and let it rest against the bell's ring and felt its weight, which was a great deal more than it had any right to be at eight fathoms, and understood that he had made a mistake about how long he had to work.

He had the anchor out of the silt and the plate off the rib's foot in his other hand when the first current came across the shoal, and it came across the shoal like a hand laid flat on the back of a neck.

He went up the lead.

The bell came up after him and the anchor came up after the bell, and Bevin Rook had the *Kittiwake* round with the tide under her in four minutes flat, and the anchor came over the gunwale onto the deck with a noise like a dropped bell and lay there in the last of the light with its stock bent up like a hook.

Calder came out of the helmet and sat on the gunwale with his hands on his knees and could not get his fingers to do what he wanted them to do for a while. The doubled hose was lying in a heap in the bottom of the boat. The single clamp was bent back about four inches from where the rib's shoulder had tried to roll off it, and the second clamp was somewhere out on the shoal, and the second clamp was a new one, bought in Ash Quay in the spring out of a share Calder did not have.

"Six-twenty," Nessa said. "You had until a quarter past six."

"The current made at six-fifteen." He had the slate in his hand. "The silt-out was at six-oh-four. I wrote the times down."

"Then write the time of the hose down as well, and write that the clamp went, and write that you are going out again tomorrow and that there is no clamp to go out with." She was crouched over the anchor's crown shackle and had the shackle in her hand and a light on it. "Marr, this shackle is cut."

He looked.

"Cut?"

"Cut, and not worn. There is a bright face on one side of it that has no rust on it at all, and there is a saw kerf in the metal, and the kerf is at the same place as the wear from the shank." She turned it so the light went down the kerf. "Somebody sawed this shackle nearly through at some point, and then it was loaded again, and then it came off, and now it is on our deck with a bright cut on it and a mark of the harbor authority's float tied to it."

Calder wrote it down. He wrote it in the line he had made for things that did not belong to him, and under it he wrote a second line, and the second line was: *anchor, shoal, port quarter of the Alder Reach — not the mole iron.*

The plate lay on the deck boards where he had put it, wrapped in the sailcloth, and it was the only thing on the boat that nobody had touched since it came up.

Mara Venn was standing at the head of the slip with her coat buttoned to the throat and her book in her hand, and she had evidently been there some time, because her lamp was lit.

"You are late," she said. "Do not tell me about the tide. I have been standing in a wind on a slip for three-quarters of an hour while a Registry clerk at the circuit office wrote a line in a book that I will have to initial, so you and I are going to be short with each other for a while and it will not improve by anybody apologising." She came down the slip and stopped six feet off the plate. "Is that the second object?"

"That is a piece of the same thing it came out of."

"That is an answer I did not ask for." She did not come closer. "Leave it where it is, and tell me what came up with it, and tell me what came off it, and do not tell me what it means."
