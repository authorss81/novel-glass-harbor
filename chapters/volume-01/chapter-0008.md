# Chapter 8

*The Shard Leaves the Deck*

Bevin Rook came into the shed first, and he came in the way he came into every space, which was backwards and too fast.

"Under the false floor," he said. "There's a space under the bench at the back and the floor's loose and I can have the bag down and the bench back before anybody—"

"No."

"It isn't glass," he said. "It isn't glass, Calder, it's a bag of wet cloth, they'd have to prove it's glass to—"

"They would not have to prove anything." Calder was still standing with his boots in his hand. "Kest will look at the floor because it is loose. There is a Registry woman in this yard tonight with a book, and a courier is coming at eight, and if there is a bag under a loose floor then for the rest of my life there is a bag under a loose floor. Put the bench down where it is."

Bevin put the bench down where it was and stood in front of it with his hands hanging, twenty-two years old, and wrecked about it.

Nessa came in from the slip. She had a chisel in her hand — not a cold chisel. A flat one, four inches of good steel, the kind you put in a fire door.

"Give me that."

"That is my chisel, Nessa."

"It is a chisel." She held it out flat on her palm, not pointing it at anybody. "I am not going to hit anything. I am going to hold it while I say the next part, and then I am going to put it down. There is a seam on the under edge of the plate. It is the old one, the keyed join, and it is the one place on that plate where a man with a good chisel could take four inches off the corner in ninety seconds and leave behind an edge that a Crown examiner would call a damaged edge and not a cut edge."

"You thought about it before you picked it up."

"I have been thinking about it for two days and I picked it up on the way up the slip and I would like you to understand that I was not deciding, I was just holding it." She put it down on the bench and stepped away from it. "And then I understood what it would actually be. It would not be me breaking glass. It would be me making the two of us unable to ever say a word about it, because the next time anybody asks either of us about that plate the first thing that comes up is that the Free Keel yard broke it."

"That is what I would have thought."

"You would have thought of it after." She wiped her hands on her apron, which had oil on it and no blood, and Calder noticed that she had noticed him noticing.

Then the harbor went quiet in the particular way it went quiet when a boat came alongside the mole with a man in it who did not want to be announced.

---

Kest came in with a lantern he did not need, because the lamp was already burning on the bench, and with a courier behind him, a woman of about thirty with a Registry coat and a flat case under one arm.

The courier did not come further than the door. She put the case on the bench's end, opened it, and took out a folded sheet and a small pot of wax, and then she took out a second sheet, and Calder understood that there were two objects coming off this bench tonight and that they were going to be entered separately, in separate lines, in separate hands, and that the distance between the two lines was going to matter for a very long time.

"Sit down, Marr," Kest said. "This is a Registry seizure order. I serve it because I am the harbor authority's officer and this ground is the harbor authority's ground, and I will hold it while it is read and while it is signed, and then it goes where it goes and I have no further part in it. Say that out loud before I read it, so that there is a man in this room who heard me say it."

"Say what out loud?"

"That I cannot seize glass. I can hold it, and I can delay, and I can put it in front of a hearing, and if I could take it off this deck by force of office I would not be permitted to, and if I did it anyway there is a woman from the Court of Wreck Claims in Veyr who would take my coat off me in about nine minutes." He looked at Calder. "Say it."

"You cannot seize glass," Calder said. "You can hold it and delay it and you cannot take it by force of office."

"Good. Now I will read it."

He read it. He read it slowly, in the voice he used for a hearing, and the room heard it in the order it was written.

It was addressed to the Morrow circuit office of the Lantern Registry. It cited, in its third line, a report from that office on an object of transparent mineral recovered on harbor ground within the harbor line, and it stated that the object's origin was undetermined and that the origin of a transparent mineral was a question of provenance and therefore of the Registry and not of the harbor authority. It did not mention glass. It did not mention the *Aster Gannet*. It did not mention a boat, or a case, or a ferry, or a storm.

At the foot of the second page there was a seal line, and Kest slowed down for it, not because he was uncertain but because it was the part that mattered in a room with a courier in it.

"Sealed and entered at the Morrow circuit office by order of Magister Ilya Serr, senior Registry examiner, on the ground that a transparent mineral of undetermined origin recovered on harbor ground is presumed Crown material until a recognized witness or a municipal claimant is entered against it."

He stopped and looked up.

"Magister," Mara Venn said, from the stool.

"That is the word on the paper." Kest turned the sheet round so she could see it. "It is the Registry's word for a senior examiner. There is a man in Veyr with that word in front of his name and I have not met him and neither have you, and I would point out to everyone in this shed that a name and a word on a piece of paper is not a reason and is not a charge and is not an explanation. It is a signature."

"I have not met him either," Mara said. "I have read his examinations for three years."

"And what does he write like?"

"That he is very careful." She did not take her eyes off the seal line. "That is not an opinion, Mr Kest. It is an observation made by an employee in his own office, and I will not put it in any record of mine."

Calder looked at the word for a while. It was a form of address, set in the same size of type as everything else on the page, in a line that had a space at each end of it, and it meant nothing to him except that the paper had come from a long way off on account of a word.

"Three days ago," he said, "a courier came onto this quay and asked after glass on harbor ground, and a woman with a book stood across a table from me in a barge with a stove in it and asked me what I recognized, and I lied to her. And it has taken three days to arrive on this bench, and the man who signed it was not in my yard and did not hear me lie." He turned to Kest. "Where is the order going?"

"Both objects go to the Morrow circuit custody room, by the courier, this morning, and they are entered there as two items under two numbers, and the reeve's office will have a copy of the entry and you will have a copy of the entry, and the numbers are the whole of what either of us will have to argue with later."

"Two numbers."

"Two numbers, and the courier will initial both, and I will witness the loading of both, and if I am honest with you here, Marr, that is the only place where the two numbers can be kept apart, and it is the last place anybody with a boat can keep them apart."

---

They signed for a long time, and Calder thought about his father for the whole of it.

The surrender was four lines. It said the Free Keel yard held one fragment of transparent mineral recovered on harbor ground and one plate of transparent mineral recovered on the inner edge of Gannet Shoal, and that both were surrendered without prejudice to the Free Keel's claim to such share as the harbor authority might allow, and that the yard would retain its log, its measurements, its drawings, and its names, and that nothing in the surrender was an admission that either object had any particular origin.

He read it twice. He read the words *without prejudice* and did not know what they meant and decided that it did not matter because there was no better version.

Then he read the last line, which was the line Kest had put in himself, and Calder understood that Kest had put it in himself because nobody else in any office in the Veyr chain was ever going to.

"The moorings, tackle, and iron of the Morrow Quay mole remain the property of the harbor authority, including the shoal anchor now on this deck, which is iron and is municipal, and including a two-fathom storm anchor tagged and bedged in the mole's toe off the third seam, which the authority will lift at its own cost on a proper tide, and the Free Keel waives all claim to it."

"That last one is yours," Calder said.

"That last one is mine," Kest said, "and I have written it in because in about eleven years somebody is going to look at that float in the mole and read a tag, and I would like the tag to agree with a line in a document. I cannot do anything about the glass in your hands. I can do that."

Calder signed. Kest witnessed. The courier initialed both entry lines and wrote the two numbers on the back of the second sheet in a small square hand, and gave one copy to the yard and kept the other.

Mara worked while they were packing.

She did it in one sitting, and she did it out loud at intervals so that the yard could hear what a public provenance duplicate was, on the grounds that a thing that is public ought to be made in public. It was not a copy of anything. It was a witnessed record of measurements, seams, custody facts, and named hands, in a form that would survive the loss of the two objects, and every one of its lines was a line that could be checked by somebody standing on a different day in a different place with a rule and a steel.

"Every measurement from the slate, in the order he took it, with the hour. The three repair histories on the plate, with the iron's ceiling on the second one, described as a ceiling and not a date. The keyed keel seam, the eleven keys, the fillet, the Crown-shop flange over the corner, the aperture described as sealed and not opened, and the builder's mark of nine joined lines on the underside, described as a builder's mark, of pre-Accord construction, with the conservator's written note that a mark is not a witness and records no consent. The fouled line, its lay, its seizing, the district it points to, and the fact that it is a material comparison and not a chain of custody. The shard: one object, undetermined origin, its lay and its seizing, and a band ground along its lower cut face whose purpose is not stated — which is not a claim and not a fault, and is written as a measurement and nothing else. And I am writing *undetermined* and I will not write it softer because the entry is going to Veyr."

"And my names," Nessa said. "All of ours. Whoever put a hand on it."

"Every name that touched either object, in the order, with the job. Marr, Rook, Pike, Wren, Mott. And you as independent witness, with your office and your book, because a duplicate made by the crew and witnessed by nobody is a note in a drawer."

"And the sounding lead line from the shoal," Calder said, "and the bearing of the anchorage when we took it, and the sounding of the pale line, and the fact that I would not let the boat over it. The name list is nothing if the position is not on it. A record of a thing that was found somewhere is a story."

Mara wrote it. The yard made four copies and kept one, and the duplicate went into the flat case and out to the courier, and the measurements went with it, and the drawing of the plate went with it, scaled, with the eleven keys marked on it in Calder's own hand and a note beside them that said *continuous west, no break, no join.*

At twenty past six they brought the two objects out.

The shard went into a padded box with the fouled line folded under it exactly as it had come up, and the plate went into a second box, longer, with a board on each side of it and straw packing at the corners, and Nessa packed the second box herself and would not let the courier's hands touch it, and when the courier said that it was Registry procedure, Nessa said that it was a plate with a three-century-old join in it and that she would be responsible for the packing in her own hand or not at all.

The shard was carried down the slip by Bevin Rook, who had carried nothing all night and who was going to be told all his life by people who did not know any of this that the last thing he ever carried for Calder Marr was a box of glass out of a Free Keel yard at half past six in the morning, and that he had not been trusted to hide it.

The plate was carried by Nessa. The moor iron they had freed came out and went up the yard to the stack against the wall, and it lay on the iron with a float line's weight on it, and Calder stood in the yard and looked at a piece of iron that nobody wanted any more and could not make himself feel anything about it at all.

At twenty to seven the courier closed the second case and put the flat case under her arm and got into the boat.

Then Calder went and got the slate.

He had written the position of the drifted storm anchor on that slate on the first evening, with the seam number and the bearing and the depth, and it was the only record in Morrow Quay that connected a piece of iron in a mole to a shoal outside the harbor line, and it was in his hand, and it was on a board in a shed.

Kest was at the gate, and the courier's boat was pulling off, and Nessa had a hand on Calder's arm, and he was already turning for the shed.

"Leave it," Kest said.

"It is the only—"

"It is a lead line, a bone toggle and a rag on a float," Kest said, "and I put my own mark on that float at five o'clock on the first evening, and I hold that iron and I will lift it, and when I lift it I will lift it with two men off the town and a proper chain and a slip's crew, and there will be an hour written against it and a man on the wall, and if I do not do that then I will not have it done, because I have seen a mole in this harbor with a fouled holding in it since I was thirty years old."

"Four days."

"Four days is an eternity, and you are not to go out there with a hook and a long line, and if I catch you on that ground in a boat I will hold you under the harbor charter and I will win, and I will not enjoy the letter." Kest's hands were behind his back and his voice was exactly as neutral as it had been on the mole. "And it will be a real iron with a real tag on it and my name on the tag, and the harbor will have lifted municipal tackle off its own ground in front of two witnesses, in daylight, in a record. That is not nothing, Marr. That is the only thing left in Morrow Quay that is a thing anybody can check."

Calder put the slate down on the bench in the shed, in the open, where anybody who walked into the yard could read it, and wrote the position out again for a second board, and gave the first to Bevin Rook to take to the market, where the fish market crowd would read it because it was not a salvage notice and it was not interesting to them and it was therefore on the wall.

He watched the courier's boat go out past the mole and round into the channel.

Then he stood in the yard in the early light with nothing on the bench and an iron on the ground, and worked out for the first time in three days what the day was going to be.

It was going to be a day with no glass on it. And the claim window on the shard closed when the courier took the box down the slip, whatever the paper said about four days, because a window on an object a man no longer holds is a window with nothing in it; and the object at the end of it would be sitting in a custody room in the west of the city under a number he had written down once and would see again, if he ever saw it, in a list of other numbers.

He had wanted a piece of his brother for thirteen years. He now had a slate, a drawing, a fouled line described in a Registry hand, a woman's note about an edge he had not looked at, and a bell in his head that was a third of a second late, and the morning was coming up out of the water over the mole with the tide, and there was nothing else in the harbor that he could do about any of it.

So he went to get the drawing of the shoal off the wall where Nessa had put it up on the first evening, with the position, the sounding, and the anchorage in her figures, and he stood in front of it with the tide coming in and turned it sideways, and turned it again, and put his finger on the corner of it where the *Alder Reach* lay and the line of the pale thing on the bottom ran out to the west.

"You have not got a chart of that," he said.

Mara Venn had come out of the shed behind him, with her book shut under her arm, and she came and looked at the drawing for a while, and then she took a folded paper out of her coat and opened it on the drawing, and Calder did not know what it was, and she did not tell him, because she was busy.

"It is a copy of a page from the inquest record," she said. "It is a public document. The public pressure log and the ferry survey, copied this morning in the circuit office's copying room, in the presence of a clerk, at my request and at my office's expense, and I have copied it because the copy is free and the reading of it in the room is not."

"What is it?"

"It is the *Vigil*'s own record of where she lay on the morning of the Gannet Cut." She put her finger on a line on the copied sheet and slid it across the drawing until the two lines of paper lay together on the yard's bench. "Her mooring. Three-quarters of a mile out, on a westerly bearing from the shoal, in water over old ground."

The finger moved along the copied sheet to a second line, a surveyor's, and then along the yard's drawing to where Calder had drawn, three days ago, in the middle of an afternoon, with a sounding lead in Nessa's hand, a pale line of something on the bottom of the Gannet Shoal that ran out to the west on a dead straight.

The two lines ran the same way. The distance was right to within a boat's length.

"Well," Mara said. "That is not a route. It is two lines of ink on two pieces of paper made by two different offices on two different coasts, and one of them is a public record of where a naval cutter anchored thirteen years ago and the other is a fisher's sounding lead and a boatman's eye. It does not show a way in. It shows a direction, and it shows that a piece of glass with a fouled ground line in it was lying on the same bearing as the cutter, and I would like to sit down."
