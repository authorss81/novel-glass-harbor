# Chapter 26

*The Office Reads Her Back*

The paper came in on the Monday the 31st at ten minutes past nine and it was three sheets, and the reeve's office had a fire in it that had not been lit since the middle of September, which told Calder more about the morning than anything on the paper did.

Kest had it flat on the table with a weight on it. The clerk stood at the counter with his sleeves rolled and would not look at any of them. And there was a fourth sheet, thinner, that had come in with it, folded twice, with a seal on the fold that was not the Registry's.

"Serve it," Kest said, to the room in general. "Marr, you are here because you are named in it, which I did not know until I had it in my hand, and which I would have told you before I opened it if I had been able to find you. Read it."

Nobody read it. Kest read it.

It was a memorandum of the Morrow circuit office of the Lantern Registry, directed to the harbor authority at Morrow Quay, and it began by citing a document.

It cited *the conservator's note of the fourteenth*, and then it quoted it, in the circuit office's own type, and the quotation was exact.

*Object held separated. No keel-line contact without a written plan. No bell entry without a conservator present and a stated reason. The edge observed in the entry of the fourteenth is a possible join, and a join upon a panel still joined to a keel-line travels along the line to the next panel, and the next panel's witness is not asked whether it consents to the arrival.*

Kest read that twice and stopped on it.

"That is her," he said. "That is her hand's sentence with her hand's punctuation, and it is seventeen days old, and it is in a book in her office, and it is on the top of a list of a document she wrote for the infirmary of a Free Keel yard." He turned the sheet round and pushed it at Calder. "Read the rest. I have not got the voice to do it and I have to serve it in a minute."

Calder read.

The notice had four clauses. The first took the mole. No keel-line, panel, or vessel of transparent construction lying in harbor or coastal water should be approached, sounded, entered, or worked between the **third and the eleventh seams of the mole's toe and inner face**, by any person, on any work, under any instrument of the harbor authority or any other; and the harbor authority was requested to post the same on the mole and to enforce it against all persons.

The second took the sheds. No transparent mineral recovered on or beneath harbor ground within this harbor should be brought into any shed, workshop, store, or dwelling inside the harbor limits, save the Morrow circuit custody room and the place of its entry; and the mineral in the custody room was not to be moved.

The third took the crew. Any person who has handled such material should not handle such material again, and should not enter the water within the harbor limits on any work of the harbor authority, without a written plan countersigned by a circuit officer of this office.

The fourth was the argument, and Calder read it three times and then put his hand flat on the table to keep it from moving.

The circuit office stated that the object in its custody room was entered as *held separated*, and that the conservator who had made that entry was the officer of this office. It stated that a join of the kind described travels along a joined line. It stated that the Free Keel yard had filed a written purpose with the harbor authority on the twenty-seventh of this month, of which the office held a copy, and that in that purpose the yard asserted that the ground described in its own bearing sheet and in the harbor authority's own shoal book lay on a single line running west-southwest, and that the bottom came up on that line at stated intervals and nowhere else. It did not reproduce the closing sentence of that purpose, which had been that the crew had no measurement of the assertion it was making. It stated that the office was therefore satisfied that a continuous line of joined transparent construction exists on the ground claimed by the *Vigil*, within four hundred yards of which no person may already approach. And it stated, in its last line, that in the opinion of this office a line of joined transparent construction is not a scatter of material, and that material which travels is not material which may be approached by a crew that has handled it.

Kest let the fire tick four times.

"That is a well-made paper," he said. "I have served worse and I have served better. Every word of it is drawn from a document that exists, and I have read the document, and it is a correct document, and I have no power to say one word of it is not correct." He squared the three sheets. "And the last line of it is a sentence about a join, and the office has applied it to a position, and those are two different things, and a join travels along a joined line, and a line lying on a bottom is not a joined line, and there is not one word in that woman's note which says that a line of glass lying in a row is a line of glass that will carry anything from one end to the other."

He stopped and looked up.

"Which is the whole of what I have, and it is not enough, and I am going to serve the paper."

"You are going to serve it," Calder said.

"I am going to serve it at eleven o'clock on the mole, out loud, with the tide gang standing there, and I am going to post it on the market wall beside your duplicate where the fish market can read it, and I am going to enter the four clauses in my book in the order they are written." Kest picked up the three sheets. "And you are going to be standing on the mole at eleven, because I have told you twice that you are not to be on this ground and I meant it the first time and I mean it now, and I would rather bar a man from a mole in front of witnesses than have a man say afterward that nobody told him."

"Whose paper is that?" Calder nodded at the fourth sheet with the seal on the fold.

"That is a request, and it came in on the *Vigil's* boat at seven this morning, and it is signed by her captain, and it is one page, and it says that this office having formed an opinion upon the ground claimed by that vessel, the vessel does not ask the harbor authority for anything and has no authority in this harbor, and merely requests that the harbor authority be assisted." Kest's mouth did not move at all. "I have read that sentence about nine times since eight o'clock and I have not worked out whether it is a courtesy or a hand on the back of my neck, and I intend to work it out after I have served the other one."

---

They served it at eleven.

The mole wall is only a hundred and forty feet long and there is nowhere on it to stand out of the wind, and the harbor's tide gang had to stand in a line to hear it, and the fish market crowd came out because there was a man reading aloud on the mole, which is the only entertainment a mole has.

Kest read all four clauses. Then he read the three of them that touched the mole and the crew again, because the crowd had been late, and he did it slowly and without dramatizing it in the way a man dramatizes something when he wants it resented. He gave the date and the office and the ground of it, which was the conservator's note of the fourteenth, and he named the conservator, because her name was in it and because he was not a man who read a woman's name out of one of her own sentences and left it off the wall.

Then he said the enforcement, in his own words, and Calder wrote it down afterwards from memory and checked it against Kest's own book in the yard that afternoon, word for word.

*The notice is served and the notice stands. The seam of the mole's toe and inner face from the third seam to the eleventh seam is closed to all work and all approach by all persons, which includes this office, this yard, and any other crew in this harbor. The office's own moorings and fenders on that ground are not to be worked, and I will find another way to do them, and I will tell the tide gang so myself. No person who has handled the seized material goes into the water inside this harbor on the authority's work without a plan countersigned by a circuit officer, and I will not sign that plan and I have no power to sign it. Any boat found lying inside that ground will be reported to this office and to the circuit office, and I will write the report. The bar at four hundred yards off the outer marks was not mine and is not altered by this and stands.*

Nothing in that last paragraph had been in the notice. Calder noticed it and did not trust it.

The notice went on the market wall at half past eleven, on the same board as the duplicate, nailed at the top with the circuit's seal over the nail-head, and the four sheets of the crew's own public record of objects went on below it with their names on them, and for about ten minutes in the fish market there was a small crowd standing in front of a wall reading two documents written by two offices about the same six feet of water in this harbor.

The duplicate said what a plate measured, what a seam looked like, what a mark was, and what a record was for.

The notice said the crew must keep off the water.

---

Bevin Rook copied it out that afternoon, in his right hand, on a board, in a fair print, because his right hand was the one that worked and he said he would rather it were his own board than a hired one, and he stood at the end of the slip with the board on his knee and the sling on the other arm and copied four clauses and the argument and the seal line without leaving out one word of it, which took him until the light went.

"You cannot put that on the market wall," Nessa said, when she saw it. "That is an office paper and we are not an office."

"He is not putting it on the wall," Calder said. "He is putting it on our own board so that a man working in this yard can read what has closed the water without walking to the fish market to find out. That is not publication. That is a yard telling itself the rules."

"Write the difference on the board," Nessa said, "so that nobody in four years thinks we published it."

So Calder wrote at the foot of it, in his own left hand: *Copied into the yard on the 31st, from the notice as served on the mole. This board is a copy for working hands. It is not a publication and it is not a claim and it is not the office's paper. Nobody has taken it down.*

And then he sat on the end of the bench with the day gone out of the window and understood the shape of the whole of the month entire, and it took him a while to get it right.

The thing that had kept him off the water was a bench, a cloth, a lamp, a woman writing down what she could not make sense of, and a line that said a thing travels. It had cost them nothing except eleven minutes and a burn on a forearm, and it had kept the shard and the plate off the market and the *Vigil*'s claim at arm's length for six weeks, and every word of it had been true.

And now it was a notice with a seal on it, and it had closed the third seam and the fourth seam and the fifth seam, and the place where a boy had put his hand on a bedded thing in ten feet of water on Friday was inside it, and there was no longer any argument in the world between Calder Marr and that water that was not an argument he had already lost once.

The yard door was open. There was nobody in the yard.

Mara Venn was standing in the road with her book shut under her arm and her coat buttoned to the throat.

"I wrote it," she said, before he got to her. "I wrote the four sentences they have quoted, in my own book, in my own hand, on the fourteenth, for a fragment lying on a bench, and every word of it is correct, and I am not going to be argued with about the correct part of it today or any other day."

"I was not going to argue with the correct part."

"You were going to argue with all of it, because you have wanted to since the fifteenth." She did not come into the yard. "Mr Marr, I have three *not stated* entries against you in a book and I am now quoted in a notice that will be read in Veyr by a man who does not know what I wrote it about. That is the cost of writing a thing down in an office and it is the first time in three years that I have been quoted at this length by my own office, and I have had four hours to find out whether I can live with it, and the answer is yes, and it took four hours, and I would have preferred three."

"Then come in and write something."

"No." She said it flatly. "If I write anything at all now, it goes up to a circuit officer to be countersigned, and it will not reach anybody in three weeks, and if in the meantime somebody in this harbor uses it to argue that a woman in a Registry coat has taken a side, then the correct part of what I wrote goes into the ground with me. So I am going to go back and I am going to write nothing, and I am going to let my own sentence stand in that notice in exactly the words you have all just read out loud on the mole, which is the only thing I have that they cannot get round."

She went up the road. Calder stood in the yard door with the board on the slip and the notice on the fish market wall and the fourth seam of the mole closed to him by a sentence written on a bench by a woman he had lied to in September, and he did not follow her, and it was the hardest thing he did on the thirty-first.
