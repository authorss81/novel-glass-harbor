# Chapter 36

*Six Clauses and a Reason*

The plan had to be written before anything could happen to it, and it had to be written on the yard's own paper, in the yard's own hand, without the harbor authority anywhere in it, and that last condition was the reason Calder sat down at seven in the morning on the twelfth of November with a blank slate and a cold stove and had to start with the reason rather than with the work.

The reeve's instrument of the third of November, in his coat, on the mole steps, in front of three witnesses, had six clauses, and clause three was that a reason in writing be read out by the officer of the harbor authority before the diver went in, and entered in the harbor authority's book before the diver went in. Calder could not satisfy that clause and never would, because the harbor authority's instrument was conditional and void until a circuit officer countersigned a plan, and the plan was the thing he was writing. So he wrote two reasons: one for the instrument, to be entered if the plan ever came, and one for the plan, to go to the circuit office with it.

He wrote the second one first, because it was the harder of the two, and he wrote it four times.

The first attempt said the ground at the fifth seam had been seen and not measured, and that the yard asked to measure it. That was a request for a survey and it would have been refused in a line, because the office is not asked to survey ground and does not survey ground.

The second attempt said that a plate of transparent material with a bolted rim of a Crown-shop pattern had been seen at the fifth seam, and that a plate of transparent material with a bolted flange of a Crown-shop pattern over a sealed aperture was entered in the Morrow circuit custody room under one of two numbers, and that the yard asked that the two be compared by a person entitled to compare them.

He read that one out to Nessa Pike and she made him take a sentence out of it, and then she made him put one in, and the putting-in was worse than the taking-out.

"You have written that the two are the same work," she said. "You have not written it and you have written it in the shape of the sentence, and if that goes up to Veyr with your name on it, then in four months a man in a Registry coat is going to read two rims described in the same words and take them for the same object, and he will be doing it on a paper of yours, and you will have written it on the thirteenth of October and dated it and cannot get it back."

"They have got the same rim on them," Calder said.

"They have got rims on them that have been described in the same words by a Registry woman and by a reeve, and the two descriptions are eleven feet and two hundred yards apart and one of them is under three feet of the mole's own stone, and neither of them has been measured, and the description of the one in the custody room is mine and was written by Mara out of her own hand and the description of the other is a reeve's and four men saw it in a slop. That is not the same work. That is the same kind of thing." He squared the slate. "Write this and I will sign it. Two descriptions. Four words in each of them. A bolted rim, a Crown-shop pattern, studs in a jig spacing, and darker material inside it. And then a line that says the two descriptions are close and that no person in this harbor has compared the objects and that this office is asked to compare them and not the yard, because the objects are in its custody and the yard has one of them in a wall and is barred by the notice of the thirty-first from the other."

"And what does the office answer, and if the objects are the same work, what then?"

"Then the yard has a second thing with a Crown-shop rim on it inside the harbor line, inside the authority's own mole, bedded in the authority's own foundation, and it is a different object from the two in custody and it is a third of the same run, and that is a finding and not a fragment of glass lying on a bed of rubble with nobody's name on it." He put the pencil down. "And if the objects are not the same work, then we have wasted a week and the fifth seam is a piece of a boat's skylight and the whole of this is a wall and a tide and a boy's eyes, and I would rather be refused in a room than be wrong on a wall."

Nessa read it back, and then Bevin Rook read it back, and then Calder signed it left-handed and Nessa countersigned and Bevin read the whole of it once more with the hour against it, because that was the job and he was the only man in the yard who could do it with a right hand and a sling on the other arm.

---

The Morrow circuit office copying room was a long cold box with a counter down the middle of it and four stools, and Calder sat on the far side of it with the plan in front of him and a clerk opposite who had ink on his cuffs and who was not the harbor authority's clerk and who had read the plan twice before he asked for the fee.

"Three marks for the entering of a plan and the fee of the countersign, and the fee comes back if the plan is refused, and the plan is not refused until it is countersigned, so the three marks is the cost of not being refused for nothing."

"I have not got three marks," Calder said.

"You have got five in a bag and they are spoken for."

"They are spoken for and I am aware of it, and I am telling you because the office's own rule is that a document is not left with a man he cannot pay, and I would rather say it at the counter than have it discovered in Veyr." He put his hand flat on the five marks. "There is no money in this harbor that is not a debt. Take the three and take it out of the five and put the two on the note at the chandler's, and the note is eleven weeks into six and it has not been touched since the twenty-fifth of last month, and I would like two marks on it this week, and the office can have the reason in writing if the office wants a reason."

The clerk wrote the two marks on the note into a small book of his own, and gave him a slip for the three, and read the plan in and stamped it, and then he said, in the flat voice of a man filling in a box on a form, that the countersign went to a circuit officer and that a circuit officer was not in the building on a Saturday because of the mid-month returns, and that the plan would go up and would come back down either with a countersign on it or with nothing on it, and Calder said that if it came back with nothing on it he would want a line saying that it had been considered, and the clerk said that a plan that is not countersigned returns without a line, because a line would be a decision, and a decision is made by the officer and not by the room.

That is the whole of what the circuit office did for Calder Marr on the twelfth of November, and it took twenty minutes, and it was not the answer.

---

The answer came back at half past two the same day, on the same paper, with nothing on it, and under the stamp there was a second sheet, and the second sheet was four paragraphs long and it was in the Registry's hand and it began with the words *the circuit office has considered the plan lodged this day and returns it uncountersigned, and the grounds are these*, and Calder read it standing at the counter and the clerk read it over his shoulder because a man may read his own paper alone but a man who is not sure may have it read, and Calder said "read that one, please," and the clerk read it.

*One. The plan is for a supervised look, by one diver, without tools and without a lead line, at a depth of eleven feet, at a place on the face of a mole which the notice of the thirty-first of last month closes to all persons on any work under any instrument of the harbor authority or any other. The office has issued that notice. The plan asks the office to countersign an instrument which would put a person inside the closure its own notice created.*

*Two. The office will not, and the ground is not a question of the office's convenience. A notice which closes ground is a notice about ground. A countersignature which authorises one named person to enter that ground is a statement by this office that the closure is negotiable on application. If it is negotiable on application then it was not a notice, it was a first refusal, and every person in this harbor and on this coast will read it as a first refusal within the year, and will make his application, and the office will be answering applications instead of holding ground.*

*Three. The plan also asks that the material be compared with the objects in the custody room of this office. This office will not compare a thing in its custody with a thing in a wall, on a plan produced by a crew which is barred by the notice of the thirty-first, and it will not do so in a room in which the yard's account of the wall is the only account, and it will not do so in a shed.*

*Four. The office notes that the conservator of this circuit has this day declined a certificate of the ground, and the office notes that the plan and the certificate ask for the same thing by two different roads, and the office declines both, and the office does not treat that as any comment on the conservator or on the yard. Grounds of refusal are not a judgment on a person and are not to be read as one in any other office.*

Calder stood at the counter with the two sheets in his hand and did not move for a while.

"That is a correct refusal," he said at last. "All four of it. And the fourth one is written by a woman who read it and knew what it would be used for, and she has put the sentence in the end of it on purpose, and she has put it in the last paragraph where an office puts the thing it does not want read out."

"I am not permitted to say who wrote it."

"You did not say. I am saying. There is a difference and I have spent four months learning it from a person who taught me it on purpose and would be furious with me for spending four months on it." He put the two sheets in order and squared them on the counter. "Book it as a refusal on the day and enter the fee back to me, and I want the refusal dated the twelfth and I want the four grounds in the book in the order they are written and I want the word *negotiable* in it, because in a year somebody is going to tell a room that the circuit office refused a plan because it was inconvenient, and there is a word in paragraph two that stops that happening, and I want it in the record in your hand."

The clerk booked it. The fee came back. Three marks out and three marks in, and Calder walked out of the copying room with two marks that belonged to the chandler and a refusal that belonged to the yard's book and nothing else in either hand.

---

Nessa Pike was at the mole gate when he got back, with a slate and a capstan bar under her arm, and she had been waiting since one o'clock, and she said the thing she had come to say before he had got the gate shut.

"You have spent this morning in a copying room and this afternoon is a wall job, and you have three and a half people."

"I know what I have got."

"Then say what you are going to do with them, because you have not said it and I have." She turned the slate round. "Elias Voke's *Anna Ray* wants her mooring ring and shackle shifted at the second seam and the ring is under forty pound of weed and a shackle that has been in the water since Michaelmas, and it is a capstan job off the wall with a tide, and it is fourteen marks for two days, and the second seam is two seams landward of the closure and it is the only work in this harbor that has been offered to this yard in three weeks."

"I cannot do the second seam and write a purpose for the twenty-first—"

"You can do the second seam and you cannot do the second seam and walk to the circuit office and back and stand in a hearing room and write a purpose and sit with a book in this yard until dark." She did not soften it, because she never did, and because a man with a hand that cannot close needs a woman who does not. "The plan came back refused this afternoon. I do not know what it says and I am not going to ask you to tell me, because if you tell me what it says then I am in it. I know two things. I know the thing at the fifth seam is not going to be looked at in November or December and probably not in March, and I know that a yard that goes into the room on the fourteenth with no work done in three weeks is a yard that is heard, and a yard that goes in with a mole wall cleared and fourteen marks in a book is a yard that is listened to.

"That is not politics. That is a wall. Eight hours on the second seam and the harbor's tide gang is stood on the wall above you for two days and every man on it will read the three sheets on the market wall from where he is standing, and you will not have said a word to any of them."

---

They went down on the second seam at eight on the Saturday morning and the ring came up in about an hour and a half in a foot and a half of water, and it was eight seams of wall and two of them were inside a closure and the other six were not, and the difference between those two facts and the difference between the two marks that will go to the chandler tomorrow night was that one of them was a matter of law and the other was a matter of having a man who could do a day's work with one hand.

At the top of the make on the Saturday evening, out on the mole steps above the second seam, the harbor's tide bell went three strokes and Calder's head began to turn and Nessa Pike's hand came down flat on his shoulder without her looking up from the shackle.

"Harbor bell," she said. "To starboard. In front of you, and you have your back to the basin, and it is not a voice and you did not hear one and nobody is going to write one down tonight, and if you want it written then Rook has the book and you may tell him and he will tell you the truth about it, which is that he has got the hour and the state of the water and nothing else and that is what the line will say."

"Then that is what the line will say," Calder said.

"That is what the line will say," Nessa said, "and in March, when somebody tells you that you can hear a bell in the bottom of this harbor as often as you like, you and I and that boy will be able to produce a book with a hundred and forty entries in it in which there is a knock, delayed, no source, not written as a voice, and no line anywhere in it that says a man heard his brother." She let go of his shoulder and picked up the hot bar again. "And that is not nothing, Calder, and I want you to hear me say it once, because you have spent four months believing that keeping a bell out of a book is the same as keeping a man out of a book, and it is not, and the one we are keeping out is you."

Then the work went on until dark, and they cleared the fouling off the second seam's stone and stacked the weed on the mole head where the tide would take it, and Calder could not help Bevin Rook with the shackle because Nessa told him not to and he obeyed, and it was the fourth time in five weeks that a woman in that yard had told him not to do a thing for a boy's sake and the third time in five weeks that he had obeyed.
