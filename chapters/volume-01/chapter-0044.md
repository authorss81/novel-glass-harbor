# Chapter 44

*Where the Ferry Is Broken*

The place was a mole face nobody had ever had a reason to look at, and it took them four days to find it, and the finding was not a discovery of anything. It was a discovery of where they had been standing.

Tobias Wren put it on it at the bench on the Saturday, in the middle of the day, without being asked, in the middle of a conversation about a shackle.

"The mole was carried out to the south," he said. "Not long. Forty yards, fifty, in my time, and I have never seen a line of it on a chart with a year against it, and I asked once, about nineteen sixty-five, and a clerk in that barge told me it was done before he was born and would I like the file. And the stone came off the beach. And the beach was not all beach."

"What is the rest of it," Nessa said.

"Glass," Tobias said. "I have never had a line on it. There is a length of that mole's face, south of where the notice is nailed, where the rubble goes down into the water on a slope instead of going down on a wall, and the tide gang call it the dump, and there is stone in it that is not stone, and I have been in this yard since 1961 and I have told nobody, because a man of sixty-one who says a mole is built on something else is a man who is told to go home."

---

Kest gave them the harbor authority's own account of its own works on the Sunday, out of a fourth drawer, read in his barge with the clerk in the room and not copied, and it took four minutes and it said three things.

The mole was carried out to the southward in the spring of the year of the *Gannet* Cut, by about fifty yards, of stone quarried at the north and of material taken off the foreshore at the south, and the account of it set out in a surveyor's hand named a quantity of rubble in cubic yards and a line that read, *the foreshore at the south end being mixed, containing iron and timber*.

"That was in October," Kest said, before Calder could say anything. "The tenth of October, four days before a cutter came into this harbor and lodged a claim over a shoal, and it is in a municipal book, and it says that a man building a mole in the month a ferry went down put iron and timber into the foot of it, and did not say what iron and did not say what timber, and did not have to, because a mole is a mole."

"Somebody knew," Bevin Rook said.

"Somebody wrote *mixed, containing iron and timber* in a surveyor's hand in a municipal book and got on with his month," Kest said. "That is the whole of what this harbor knows about it and it has known it for thirteen years and has never once been asked, and I have been in this office fifteen years and I have never read that page, and I am telling you that as a fact about me and not as a confession, because there is a difference and I have had four weeks to work out where the difference is."

He put his hand on the page.

"Now. What you have found is a mole face at the south end, beyond the eleventh seam, on ground this office owns, in about nine feet, and the notice of the thirty-first of last month closes the mole's toe and inner face from the third seam to the eleventh. It does not reach the twelfth. It does not reach the dump. And it does not reach water, and no notice has ever closed water, and a man may stand in a boat in this harbor with a hand lead at any hour of any tide and nobody has ever had a paper to stop him."

"That is the whole of the answer," Nessa said, "and it is a form."

"It is a claim window," Kest said, "which I can grant in nine minutes and which costs the yard nothing, and it is a look and not a permit, and a look may be made from a boat with a lead and a slate and a rule, and it is not the water. If you want a man in the water with his hand on something, that is a work permit, and a permit names a site and dates and a bond, and I have not got a bond instrument open, and I am not going to pretend to the room on Monday that a claim window is a work permit, because in nine days I have watched a plan go round the front of four clauses and be refused on four correct grounds and I am not going to be the office that does the same thing to a boat."

---

They went out on the Sunday the twenty-seventh with a claim window Kest had written at half past nine and the clerk had stamped at ten, naming the mole's face at the south end beyond the eleventh seam and the water off it inside the harbor line, for one date, the twenty-seventh, for a look only, with a hand lead and a slate.

The top of the flood came up at a quarter to eleven and the slop went to a sheet of glare the way it always did in this harbor, and Calder took the *Kittiwake* out with Bevin Rook on the oars and himself on the lead, because the *Kittiwake* was on the hard and had been on the hard since the twenty-fifth of October with her bilge blocked in and no cradle under her, and the only reason she was in the water at all was that Nessa Pike had made three men shift her down to the slip on the Friday before with a hand-barrow and two lengths of marline and had written the hour in the book.

At the south end the mole wall stopped being a wall and became a slope, and the slope went into about nine feet of water and kept going, and on the slope there was stone that was not stone, and in the glare at the top of the make you could see the bottom as clearly as you could see a plate on a bench.

It took them forty minutes and it was not dramatic.

There was a plate. Ferry iron, three-eighths, lying at an angle on the slope with the new mole's rubble packed against its upper side and the old beach under it, and it was the size of a good table and it had a coaming standing three foot off it, and the coaming was a hatch coaming, and the coaming was **four foot by three foot** on the outside, and the crew wrote that down with the hour on it and Bevin Rook read it back, and neither of them said anything for about a minute.

Then Calder put the lead over the shoulder and sounded inside the coaming the way you sound a thing you cannot reach, and brought the lead up under the foot of it and read the depth inside and the depth outside, and wrote both figures down, and they were two foot one, one and a half, and the difference between them was the wall of the coaming and it checked.

"Nine and a quarter at the toe of the mole face," he said. "Eight and a quarter inside the coaming, by a lead off a boat on a slant, and I will not give you better than a foot on either and you may have it because you have earned it."

"That is a man with his hand on it," Nessa said later. "Not you. A lead off a boat is not a man with his hand on it."

"I know."

"And the plate is three-eighths, and it is ferry iron, and it is on a mole this authority built on top of her in the month she went down, and there is a hatch standing in it in nine feet of water off the south end of this town's own mole." Calder put the slate on his knee. "Bevin. Read the last line back before I write it."

Bevin read it back. *Twenty-seventh, top of the flood, south end beyond the eleventh seam, water inside the harbor line, a plate of iron three-eighths lying on the slope in the glare with a coaming standing in it, the coaming four foot by three foot on the outside, the depth nine and a quarter at the toe and eight and a quarter inside by a lead from a boat, the plate not touched, not measured with a rule, nothing brought up. Claimed window of the twenty-seventh, one date, a look only.*

And under it, in Calder's own left hand, four lines, and Bevin read them twice before he was allowed to stop.

*The survey's ninth page gives standing space number four, port side aft, as eight foot four by six foot nine clear and eleven feet two deep under the grating. The coaming standing in this plate is four foot by three foot on the outside. The yard has not measured the clear opening in it, and cannot, from a boat, and will not give a figure for it until a person has a hand on it. The yard states that a room eight foot four by six foot nine and an opening four foot by three foot on the outside are not the same measurement and that the comparison between them cannot be made by anybody who has not stood in the coaming, and the yard has therefore made no finding and offers none and is not going to.*

"That last sentence is the best thing you have written in four months," Nessa Pike said, "and you wrote it because a man on a mole could not measure a thing and said so, and if you had said anything else I would have cut it out of the book and you would not have known until the twenty-eighth."

---

They found the aperture at four o'clock, in the falling water, with the glare gone, and it was the reason the whole month was worth having.

It was low on the plate's outboard side, below the coaming, a hand's breadth and a half across, with the edges dressed — not torn and not burst, dressed, with a cold chisel's bevel on both sides of it — and a circle of small holes round it, and the holes were at a spacing that had been set, not set by eye, and every one of them was full of thirteen years of fouling.

"Fourteen holes," Bevin said. "It is a bolt circle and it is fourteen and it is not square and it is not a diamond, so somebody counted."

"Somebody counted," Calder said. "Or somebody cut to a template, and a template is a shop's thing, and this is a hole in a hull cut by somebody who was not in a hurry and was not in a shop."

He wrote it down and he wrote the limit under it, because the limit was on the page before the finding was, and it was the limit that made the finding survivable.

*An aperture of a hand's breadth and a half, edges dressed with a bevel on both sides, a circle of fourteen small holes at an even spacing, the fouling in the holes and on the bevel continuous with the fouling on the plate outside, and the fouling gives no hour and no man and no direction. Nobody in this crew has touched it and nobody in this crew can say who cut it or when or from where. It may have been cut from the water and it may have been cut from the deck and this crew has no instrument that would tell it and is not going to pretend to one.*

"They will say it was cut from the deck," Nessa said.

"They will say it was cut from the water, and they will say the fouling is the same inside and out and therefore it has been open the whole of the time and therefore the hour is unknowable, and they will be right about all three and it will not matter, because what it does and does not do is the same either way." Calder put the pencil down. "Bevin, read that last sentence back twice, and then we are going to the reeve with it tonight, and we are going to ask him for a work permit for one diver on one date in December, and I already know what he is going to say, and I want you both to hear me say it before we get there."

"That he has no bond instrument open."

"That he has no bond instrument open, and that a work permit costs a bond, and that I have a tin with nothing in it and nineteen in a bag and no mark behind either, and that the man who lifts the undertaking of the twenty-fifth of October is him and not me, and that none of the four of us can get a hand on that plate before the twenty-eighth of November without something in this yard that does not exist." He looked at the water. "And I am going to tell you the other half of it now so that it is in this boat and not in a room.

"That plate has been in nine feet of water off the south end of this mole for thirteen years, and it was not the notice that hid it and it was not the *Vigil* and it was not the four hundred yards. It was that four men stood at a wall for six weeks and looked at a wall. And the answer to a question we have been carrying since the twenty-seventh of October turned out to be a form from a man in a barge, and the form is nine minutes long and costs the yard nothing, and it took us four months and a woman's four days at a bench."

---

They went up to the reeve's barge at half past six on the evening of the twenty-seventh and Calder Marr asked for a work permit and Kest told him, in about nine seconds, in the flattest voice he had, that he had no bond instrument open and could not open one before Monday and would not open one on the strength of a man in a boat who had found a hole in a plate.

And then he did the thing that Calder Marr had not expected and had no way to have prepared for, and it was the largest thing anybody in that harbor authority had done all month.

"Mr Clerk, get a sheet out. This is entered in this office's book tonight, at the hour, in this office's own hand, and the yard is to have a copy of it before the clerk shuts the tin, and the entry is as follows."

*Twenty-seventh of November, this office. A claim window was granted at half past nine in the morning of this date, for one date, for a look, on this office's own ground beyond the eleventh seam, and the look was made at the top of the flood in nine feet of water with a hand lead, and from a boat, and the office entered in its own book, at the yard's request and not at the office's suggestion, that the reason the look was made in a boat and not in a bell is that the office has no open bond instrument and could not open one before the twenty-eighth, and that the yard applied to this office for a work permit on the evening of this date and the office refused it for want of a bond and not for any other reason, and that the yard states in the office's hearing record of the twenty-eighth that the purpose filed by it on the twenty-first does not ask to be put in the water, and the office has entered that statement tonight so that it is in this office's hand before this office opens its book on Monday.*

"It is Sunday," Bevin Rook said, from the doorway.

"It is Sunday," Kest said, "and this office keeps no Sunday hours, and I have never in fifteen years written a Sunday entry, and I am writing one, and Mr Clerk is going to read it back and I am going to initial the read-back, and Mr Marr is going to take a copy of it up the quay in his own hand tonight and put it in with his purpose, and it is going to be the first thing this office has written about the Free Keel yard since the fourteenth that the yard has not had to argue for."

"Why," Calder said.

"Because on Monday I am going to read six pages into a record and a naval officer is going to hear me do it," Kest said, "and in about two years a man in a room in Veyr is going to read that record and work out that the yard's own purpose did not ask for the one thing it wanted, and he is going to assume the yard hid it, because that is what a party does when it has had four months and a hearing room. And I have sat in this office for fifteen years and watched four documents get built on top of a fourth *not stated* this month, and I am not going to be the man who builds a fifth and then wonders in two years what it was standing on."

He squared the sheet.

"File a supplementary paper in the morning," he said. "In your own hand, on your own paper, and it must be in this office by ten o'clock on the morning of the twenty-eighth, an hour before the room fills, and it must say three things. That the purpose of the twenty-first is complete as to what the yard asks for and is silent as to the ground, and that the silence is not a withholding. That the yard asks to be put in the water inside the harbor line, on this office's own ground, on a permit, on a bond, on named dates. And that the yard says now, on the twenty-eighth, in advance and of its own motion, that it did not say it on the twenty-first, and that a person who files a purpose in six pages on a Monday and asks for the bottom on a Saturday six days later is a person who should be asked about it, and I will be asking, and the room may hear it."

"You are entering my own incompleteness into your book before I have written it down myself."

"I am entering it into my book on Sunday night," Kest said, "and you are going to write it on Monday morning, and I have made a copy, and you may read my copy before you write yours, and I would like you to notice that I have not made two copies and I have not sent one to the circuit office, and that the *Vigil's* officer will not know about this until Monday morning, at the same time as everybody else, because there is one version of a thing and it is the yard's."

---

They filed it at ten past ten on the morning of the twenty-eighth of November, an hour before the room filled, and the air pot was a third gone, and the bag had nineteen marks in it of which six was already spoken for, and the chandler's note was at eleven weeks into six in a book in the fish market with three marks of the yard's own count not written down anywhere, and a boy of twenty-two was owed forty marks, and there was a plate in nine feet of water off the south end of the town's own mole with a hatch standing in it that no man had a hand on, and a bell of some kind under the same water, and a reeve's Sunday entry in a book in a barge, and a copy of it in Calder Marr's coat.

Bevin Rook read the whole of it back at the yard at four o'clock that afternoon, twice, and then said the thing he said on the fourth of November and did not say on any other day of that month.

"A purpose, a supplement, a bell nobody can strike, a hole in a plate, and a reeve's hand in his own book on a Sunday night, and I have not been paid one mark of any of it and I am not going to ask again before the tenth of December."
