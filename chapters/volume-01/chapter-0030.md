# Chapter 30

*The Bill for the Interval*

The clerk read Kest's notation out twice on the morning of the fourth, and then read it out again because he wanted to be sure that a man standing in the room understood it, and the reason he wanted to be sure was that in about four years somebody was going to come in and ask whether the harbor authority had quietly taken the mole's inner face, and the answer was going to have to be no.

It was on the authority's own chart, in the reeve's own hand, at the mole's inner face between the third and the eleventh seams.

*Ground claimed by the harbor authority, pending examination. No other claim may be entered on this ground while the harbor authority's defense of the twenty-fourth of last month runs, and afterward until a further instrument of this office. The claim is the authority's. It is not the Free Keel yard's. The Free Keel yard has asked for nothing, has been given nothing, and has declined to ask. The thing at the fifth seam, seen and not measured, is entered by this office in its own book and is not the property of any person in this harbor.*

"That is the whole of the favor," Kest said, "and I will tell you what it cost, because the clerk has told me what it cost and I am not going to have a thing in my book that the town finds out about in March.

"The third to the eleventh seams of the inner face is where this harbor keeps its timber fenders and four sets of moorings and the two berths that the herring boats use in a northeast blow, and I have just claimed all of it. That is the cost. The fenders come out of service this month. The moorings come out. The berths go. The office will have to find another face for the herring fleet or tell the herring fleet to go elsewhere, and neither of those is a thing I have to enjoy on the first of March when forty boats come in with nowhere to lie." He squared the chart. "And it is the only piece of paper in this harbor this month that protects the thing at the fifth seam, and it protects it from the *Vigil* and from the circuit office and from me, and I do not want it, and it is the correct instrument, and I have written it."

"You could have written nothing," Calder said.

"I could have written nothing and then in nine months a naval officer would have written a claim over a mole, and then a circuit office would have written a quarantine over a mole, and then a glass plate in a mole's foundation would have been in a custody room in Veyr before somebody had put a lead on it." Kest put the chart away. "That is not a favor, Marr. It is a fence, and I have put it round my own ground, and you are inside it where you cannot get at anything and neither can anybody else, and if you are inside a fence and complain about it, you are complaining about the fence."

---

They spent the whole of the fourth in the shed writing down what had happened in the four days since the notice, because the fourth was a Friday and the hearing was on the seventh and there were ten terms to go into the room and none of them could be written on the morning.

The count was done first, on the bench, with the door shut, and it is in the book in Calder's own hand because the book is the only place in Morrow Quay where this yard's money has ever been true.

*Fourth. Nine marks of air bought this month: six on the 28th against Brigg's slate, one on the 29th, two on the second. The pot was a third gone on the fifteenth of last month and is a third gone today, and a third of that pot is a hundred marks a third, and nine marks has not put a mark back into it. Work taken: the mole, third and fourth seams, eight; the mole, ninth seam, eighteen of twenty-six. Twenty-six marks of work in ten days. Twenty-one marks of it gone: six back to Brigg, three for the harbor's share, three for the harbor's pump, four in rope and food, three in air, a bonesetter's mark, and a surgeon's mark for a written account of a crushed hand. Nothing paid on the chandler's note, which stands where it stood on the twenty-fifth at eleven weeks into six. Nothing paid on the certificate, which is forty marks and is first after food. Five marks in the bag. Six marks owed to Brigg and paid and gone. The stove in the Low Lantern's boat is Brigg's own cost and the clerk has been told so in the book so that nobody in this yard can quietly put it under a line of ours.*

*We have in this yard: a shed, a bench, a book, a chest of drawings held against a bond to the fourteenth, a stack of iron with a saw kerf in the crown shackle of one piece of it, a third of that iron's sale entered to another crew, and four people, one of whom is a woman holding the tender's cleat and one of whom is a man with a hand that cannot close.*

*We do not have: a boat. A pump. A bottom man. A tender. A permit of any kind. Glass. A place in the water on the authority's work, which is every work there is, by clause three of the notice of the thirty-first, and the clause is correct, and it is served, and it catches the three of us.*

Nessa read that back and put the pen down and said, "That is the first honest balance sheet this yard has ever had, and it should be the last page of a book and not the first, and I am not going to say which order a man should read it in."

Then they wrote the ten terms for the seventh, and Calder wrote them and Bevin read them and Nessa cut one of them, and the nine that went in are these, and they are in the book in the order they are to be said.

*One. That the harbor authority find the labor of the Free Keel yard upon the two dates of the permit of the twelfth of last month proved, as it has already been found and countersigned, and that the finding be not disturbed by anything said after it.*

*Two. That the fourth hundred yard bar be found to stand, the yard not having asked for it to be lifted and not intending to go within it.*

*Three. That the anchor-bearing sheet be not used and not asked about, being a drawing and not a finding of where the Vigil lay, and that the yard withdraws its own filed suggestion that the sheet and the mooring line be laid together in a room.*

*Four. That the three points of the second of this month be received as a record of the crew's own figures and of one figure out of a book the harbor authority wrote, with the tolerance of the method stated by the men who took it, and that the yard be not asked to say what any of them is.*

*Five. That the thing at the fifth seam, seen and not measured, be received as seen and not measured, and that the yard be not asked to produce a figure for it, and that the yard states plainly, if it is asked, that it has declined two lawful routes to it and one unlawful one and has written the reasons in its own book where anybody may read them.*

*Six. That the notice of the thirty-first be received as served and as correct, and that the yard say in one sentence that it has said all it means to say about it in the conservator's second note, which is with a circuit officer and bears the date of the twenty-second, and that the yard asks the room to note the date and not to wait for it.*

*Seven. That the crew's ability to work be restored under the harbor charter in the ordinary way, the yard not asking for the third clause of the notice to be withdrawn but asking the room to hear the yard say that a clause which stops three named people from earning a living inside their own harbor line has been read in Veyr as a measure about glass and is not one.*

*Eight. That the bottom be restored to the holder of the crew's book for the term, in writing, with a stated reason entered before he goes in, a tender named who is not the diver, and a cutoff. The yard does not ask for a date and does not ask for a place.*

*Nine. That the authority's own instrument of the third of this month, in six clauses, be received, and that the authority be asked in the room to say in its own voice what it needs, so that the thing wanted is written in a man's mouth by the man who wants it, and not inferred afterwards by a lawyer from a document in a coat.*

Then Calder wrote the last line and it is the one Nessa did not cut.

*Ten. That if this defense falls on the fourteenth, the Free Keel yard will say in this room on the fourteenth what it has, and how it came, and what it does not know, and it will not go to another forum with the same figures, and it will not put another crew's numbers into this book to make this one look better.*

And a heading over the whole thing, which he wrote and which is the one he read out to the crew that night.

*Terms of the Free Keel yard for the sitting of the seventh of this month, written on the fourth, three days before, and not to be added to after the sixth.*

---

He read the whole of it aloud at eight that night to the yard, standing, with the stove going and the door open because the notice on the market wall was four clauses long and there was no point in being careful about who heard a thing that was already nailed up in the fish market.

Bevin Rook read it back. All of it. Both times. He read it with his coat on and his left arm across his chest in a sling, and the pencil was in his right hand and his right hand had been his right hand all along, and when he had finished he did not put the book down.

"There is no term in there about my hand."

"There is no term in there about your hand," Calder agreed, "and I have thought about putting one in every night since Saturday, and here is why there is not. If I put a term in about your hand, then on the seventh I am a man asking a room for something on account of an injury in his own crew, and the room will give it to him, because a reeve would rather give a man his own grief than argue with him, and then that is what that hearing is, and in six months when the fifth clause of somebody else's notice comes up there is no hearing left for the three points, because the hearing was about the boy."

"So it is not on the paper."

"It is on the paper," Calder said, and turned the book round, "in the entry for the twenty-ninth, in the line that says *Rook's wages from this day until the bonesetter says otherwise, and the bonesetter has not said*, and in the entry for the thirtieth, in the line that says the surgeon's writing has the hour and the cause and the man who held the cleat in it, and his mother has the original in her apron. And the seventh of this month is a hearing about paper, and paper is the only thing I have got, and if I spend it on the one thing in it I am sure of, then I have got nothing to say when somebody in Veyr asks me why a line of joined glass is a different thing from a line of glass."

Bevin Rook looked at the open book for a while.

"Read me the last line again," he said, "and read me the heading, and then put your own name under it and let me put mine under yours, because for the first time since the eleventh of this month the two names in this book are going to be read out in a room by a man with a coat, and I would like mine to be one of them and not to be read afterwards."

He read the heading. He read the last term. Calder signed left-handed at the foot of the page and pushed the pencil across, and Bevin Rook signed under him with his right hand, and Nessa countersigned under both, and the page went into the bench chest with the bearing sheet and the plate drawing and the five rolled copies of the duplicate, in a chest that is pledged to a clerk's generous eighteen and comes back to them on the fourteenth of next month whether they can pay the balance or not.

---

At the top of the make they went out onto the slip, all four of them, because a tide at the end of a month is worth standing on, and there was nothing on the board and nothing in the bag and nothing in the tin and the pot was a third gone.

Out in the road, a quarter of a mile off the mole on the bearing of the pale run, the *Vigil*'s tender lay where she had lain since the fourteenth of last month, with her oars shipped and her floats run out and a line of marks going away from her into the west-southwest, and she had not moved in twenty days, and a boat that holds a station that long in a free harbor is either doing something or waiting to, and nobody in Morrow Quay had worked out which, and both of those were a reason to have the tender's end of the line in a book.

The harbor's tide bell went at the top of the water, three strokes, out in the road where it had always hung, and the strokes arrived, and under them, a third of a second late and much too even to be anything, the note came up out of the bottom off the mole and said its three marks and the flick.

Bevin Rook took out the book.

"Half past nine," he said. "Top of the make, west-northwest two, water making, the delayed note under the harbor's own three. No source. Not written as a voice."

And Calder Marr, standing on the slip of a yard with no boat and no pump and no permit and no glass and a boy with a crushed hand holding a book open on a stone, did not write it, and did not say what he thought it was, and did not go and look, and that was the arrangement, and it had been the arrangement for a month, and it was going to be the arrangement on the seventh and on the fourteenth and in March, and there is no office in this harbor or in Veyr that can ever put in a book what a delayed note under a tide bell is, because there is no instrument on this coast for it, and the man who hears it has four people around him who say a number back at him whether or not it is the number he would have said, and the last of them has a hand that cannot close and is holding the pencil anyway.

He took the book back off him at the door and read the whole of the day's entry, and the whole of the terms for the seventh, and put the pencil down on top of it, and said the four words that were in the margin on the fourteenth of last month and at the foot of the twenty-fifth.

*Note entered by Marr. Stated to the crew.*

And on the fish market wall there were five documents: a public record of two objects, made in a shed and witnessed by a woman in a coat and refused a line by two men; ten crew's copies on other crews' walls; a notice of four clauses with a seal over its nail; three sheets of three points with no line drawn between them; and one sheet, under a rule, that said a plate had been seen at the fifth seam in eleven feet and had not been measured and had not been touched and was under a bar. And the tide was making behind all of it, and a quarter of a mile off the mole a naval tender held a station she had held for twenty days, and a mile and a quarter off the mole the outer marks stood where they had always stood with nobody's name on them, and about two and a half miles out on the same bearing, in water no boat in this harbor had any right to enter, the pale run lay on a dead straight with a continuous keel line and eleven keys in it, going west-southwest, toward a place that was seventy fathoms down and that one old man on a quay had named once in a gale and had told them was not any good.

Ten days of it had cost them a tender, a boy's hand, a boat on the hard, a quarter of a ton of air, and every mark of hope they had of putting a steel on a shoal.

None of it had bought a claim. All of it was on the wall, and it would be there in the morning.
