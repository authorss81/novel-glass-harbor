# Chapter 7

*What the Bell Borrowed*

The infirmary was a corner of the Free Keel shed with a curtain on a wire, two cots, a tin of hot water on a brazier, and a smell of iodine and boiled rope that Calder had been able to smell from the yard gate since he was seventeen.

He sat on the edge of the cot with his boots off and the burn on his right forearm dressed in a wet cloth, and Mara Venn was on a three-legged stool with the lamp between her feet and her book open on her knee, and she had been writing for a while before she said anything at all.

"You will hold still while I test the ear, and you will tell me when you are going to do something stupid, and if you do not tell me I will notice and I will write that down too."

"Go on."

She put a finger on the wall and moved it slowly to her left, and Calder's eyes did not follow it, and then he moved his head and found it late, and she wrote the hour and what he had done.

"Now the bell."

She went out to the yard door and put her head round the frame.

"Pike."

Nessa's voice came back from the slip. "I am not leaving the platform."

"Then Wren."

Tobias Wren's whistle went two short, and a moment later the yard's working bell went, three strokes of the evening tide call, out in the road where the tide bell was hung.

Calder had it. He had it exactly, and it was in the right place, and it was the right bell.

Mara wrote something. Then she said, "Again."

The whistle. The bell. Three strokes.

And this time Calder turned to the left, because the harbor's tide bell was in the road to the right and the sound had a shadow under it about a third of a second deep, and the shadow was a loading space with a bell in it, and his body put the two on top of each other and sent him looking for the source of the one that was further away.

He stopped himself halfway round and turned back and found the bell in the road, and the third stroke of the shadow was still arriving after the third stroke of the real bell had stopped.

"That," Mara said, "is the thing I am going to write down, and I want you to be in the room while I write it, because it is the only sentence in the whole examination that is going to matter most to you and you are the only person alive who can contradict me about it."

"Write it."

She wrote. It took a while.

"Your ordinary hearing is now carrying a second bell," she said, "which is not in the room, and which is not going to be in the room, and which you will carry for some hours. It arrived with the entry and it did not leave with it. I can test that you hear both. I cannot test which one is the harbor's, and neither can you, and if anybody tells you that in a week you will know which one is the harbor's, they are selling something." She looked up. "How long is it going to last?"

"A night. A day."

"That is what a man says when he has a hearing in the morning and a funeral in the afternoon." She put the pen down. "You will not dive again for a day and a night, and that is not a medical opinion, it is a limit I am putting in the record, and it is a limit I will enforce by not certifying you as fit for a bell entry until two clean days have gone by."

"I am not going back in a bell."

"You said the same thing at four o'clock yesterday about a hose, and the hose held once." Mara's voice did not rise. "Mr Marr, I have watched a great many men say very sensible things about what they are not going to do yet. I do not need to be convinced. I need it written down before the next person comes along who is better at arguing than you are."

Calder did not answer that, because she was right, and because there was nothing in the argument that would improve the position of either of them.

The curtain moved. Nessa Pike came in from the slip with her hair wet to the shoulders and a slate in her hand, which was the six conditions in Calder's own writing, with a line at the bottom under it.

She put the slate on the bench by the lamp and turned it so it faced him.

"Sign at the bottom," she said. "And read it first, because I have added a line and you will want to argue with the line and you are not to argue with the line."

The line said: *The tender's decision stands without discussion for the whole of the night of the entry and until a physician or the conservator has looked at the diver in daylight. The diver may not overrule the tender while the diver is attached. Where the diver and the tender disagree, the tender is right about the going up and the diver is right about the going down.*

"She has already signed it," Nessa said. "I made her sign it at the platform and I made her say it out loud so that if you argued with me in the morning, one of us would have a witness."

"That is not how a crew works."

"That is exactly how a crew works, and you have never once in your life had a crew, you have had a set of men who were afraid of you and were good at their jobs, and I have watched you for two days." She put her hand flat on the slate. "Sign it, Marr. Because you are going to be tempted, and not for days. You are going to hear that bell again in about four days on a Monday, and you are going to be standing on a boat, and some part of you is going to say that a bell in the ear is a thing that gets better and a bell in the water is a thing that gets paid. And I want a piece of paper with your name on it from this night, so that when that Monday comes you have got to put your hand on something before you go."

He read it twice. He read the clause about the diver and the tender and the part where each of them was right about one half of it, and that was the part that would have stopped him, because it was true, and because nobody had ever written it down for him before.

He signed.

"Right," Nessa said. "Now the other thing, and I am going to say it once. You are off the water. Not for a day. Off the water until the burn on your arm has closed enough that I can put a glove on it without you making a noise, and that is my measurement and not anybody's else. You are not to touch that plate, you are not to touch that shard, you are not to put your hands in that bell again, and if anybody in this harbor offers you money to look at glass I am going to be asked to build the thing that lets you do it and I will not build it for a man with a burn like that and a bell in his ears."

"Understood."

"Say it back with a number in it, because you are a man who understands numbers better than words."

"Twenty-four hours on the water minimum. The burn. And no bell entry until two clean days, in daylight, in front of you or in front of her."

"Twenty-four hours on the water, and the bell is hers."

Nessa went out. Calder looked at the slate, and then at the burn on his arm, and then at his hands, which were perfectly steady, and which were the one thing about him that had not changed at all.

Mara Venn had not moved from the stool.

"Mr Marr," she said. "Two things and then I will let you sleep, and one of them is a gift and one of them is not."

"Say the bad one."

"The bad one. The edge in that scene that would not resolve — the doubled part, the bright rim, the thing you stopped pushing against." She turned a page back in her own book and did not show it to him. "It is not a fault in the shard's material. A panel that has been broken out of a hull carries a broken edge; that is ordinary and I can test for it and it tells me nothing. What I could see on your account of the last four minutes of that entry is an edge with a different quality on it, hard-rimmed, and bright, and with something moving behind it, and you described it as two different things occupying the same water."

"Yes."

"That is the description of a seam. A seam is where two records that do not agree have been forced into one panel and made to hold. It is not a lie and it is not a truth. It is a join." She put the pen down. "And the practical consequence is this. A seam on a loose fragment is a nuisance. A seam on a panel that is still joined to a keel-line is a splinter, and it travels along the line to the next panel, and the next panel's witness is not asked whether it consents to the arrival. That is how a record that was made by one person in one room with one anchor ends up being carried by a vessel that a hundred people have to live with."

"Has it got a keel-line?"

"Unknown, and that is the answer I am going to write down, because I will not write *uncontaminated* and I will not write *contaminated* on a fragment I have never seen in a connected hull." She picked the pen up. "What I will write is this. Object held separated. No keel-line contact without a written plan. No bell entry without a conservator present and a stated reason. And if this object is ever found in a connected hull, this note is the first thing anybody reads, and it will say that a man went into it once and found a hard edge on it, and that we knew, and that we went in anyway, once, for a stated reason, with a tender and a cutoff."

She wrote it, and turned the book so he could read it upside down, which was the second time she had done that, and which was not careless either time.

"And the gift?" he said.

"The gift." She closed the book. "You gave me your numbers before I asked for them, and you gave me your arm when it was dressed and not when it was convenient, and you told me about the burn and the time it started, which is a thing men hide for a year. And I have been three years in a circuit office writing down what men found, and in three years I have had eleven accounts given to me as they happened and no more than three of them have been worth writing down. I am going to remember that, when there is a decision to be made about who gets to look at this."

"And the question I am not going to answer?"

"You are not going to tell me what your hand was doing when the panel ticked." She said it evenly, without any weight on it at all. "That is the third time tonight. I have written *not stated* against it in three places and I am going to keep writing it, and at some point one of those three places is going to be the reason a man's evidence is set aside, and I want you to know that I have told you which of my instruments it will be."

"I know."

"Go to sleep, Mr Marr. I will sit here until the light."

A boy came down the slip at twenty past three with a folded paper in his hand and did not come into the shed, and stood at the curtain and said that there was a message at the reeve's office and that it was for the yard and not for a person, and that the reeve's boy had said the words *before the inspection*.

Calder got up off the cot with his boots in his hand.

"What kind of message?"

The boy was nine or ten and he was frightened of the yard and wanted to be gone.

"I don't know the words. He said before the inspection and he said Registry and he said the glass is to be brought out to the yard, and he said it is not to go to the office." The boy's eyes went past Calder to the bench, to the lamp, and to the two books on the stool. "He said at the yard, though. Not the office. That's what he said."

Calder stood in the dark of his own shed with a wet cloth on his arm and a burn that had not started bleeding again, and a bell in his head a third of a second behind the real one, and the hour hand of a brass clock going round a long way in a place that was not the shed.

Then he said, to nobody in particular, "Nessa."

She was already on the slip. He had not heard her come back in.
