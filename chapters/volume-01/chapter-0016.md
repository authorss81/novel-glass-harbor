# Chapter 16

*Honest Work*

Thursday dawned fair and cold with the wind round to the north, and the harbor lay still in a way it rarely does this close to the equinox tides. The harbor's own work came to the Free Keel yard because nobody else would take it cheap.

The buoy master came up the slip on foot instead of sending a boy, which was how Calder knew the job was real. The outer harbor bell buoy had to come up, be scraped, and be hung again before the equinox tides, with a new bellwright's fitting on the clapper and a white cord spliced through the ring for a mark the harbor's own list still required and nobody in the office could explain. He had asked three crews. The first wanted a week of notice. The second wanted the harbor's diver, the harbor's crane, and a clause about the weather. The third told him to go to the harbor yard, which is what a crew says when it has settled what a job is worth.

"It is inside the line," the buoy master said. "It is harbor money under the harbor charter, and there is no claim on it whatever, which is the whole of why nobody wants it. One day, slack water to dark. Twenty-six marks for the crew, with cord and fitting found by us, paid at the end of the day on my count and the clerk's slip. I want a diver for half a tide to clear the shackle and hands aloft for the lift. I do not want a Registry man on my buoy."

"You will not have one," Calder said.

Nessa had the rest of it on the slate before he could make a speech about owning it.

*20th. Diver off the bottom till burn closed. Rook on the bottom under tender. Air read and written by the tender. Nobody on the bottom without a tender on the fall. Any man who wants to argue with the slate argues with me.*

Bevin read it twice. He was twenty-two and had been on the bottom four times, three of them on a beach with his boots on and a hand on his belt. He said as much, and then he asked the question that was actually in him, which was what happened if the tender called the time and he was not ready.

"Then you come up," Calder said, "and you come up angry, and you come up because the word was said. Nothing in that water is worth a man making a habit of coming up late, and there is nothing on that buoy but a bell."

"That is what you said on the *Merrit*, and the *Merrit*'s patch is still half seated."

"Yes. So you have the rule and you have the man who broke it once, and you know exactly what it costs." Calder put his good hand flat on the crown of the helmet where it lay on the boards. "Get your weights."

They rigged on the buoy punt with harbor gear. Their own pump was in the yard with its cracked housing and the sleeve weeping, so the harbor's pump came out on the harbor's account with a harbor man minding it who was careful not to touch anything Calder's crew had rigged. Bevin went over in nine feet with the tender's cleat in Calder's left hand and Nessa on the fall, and Calder found in the first minute that he could not tell the water's own noise from the pump's, and would have turned twice if there had been anything to turn toward.

Down there was a foot of green milk and a shackle gone bad with three seasons of rope and a nest of bait fish that came away in handfuls. Bevin put the light under his arm where he could not lose it, found the pin with the wrench, and came up at the ladder with the pin and the shackle in a canvas bag and a face the color of the basin.

"Nine in the water," Bevin said at the ladder. "You called eight."

"Eight on the call. Eight goes in the book."

"Eight is nothing."

"Eight is a number you can say back," Calder said. "Say it at the ladder every time. Then the log has the number whatever you were doing, and the log does not have to have an opinion about you."

Bevin said it back to him anyway, out loud, twice.

The buoy came up out of the water streaming weed and green water, and they set her on the punt's midships and scraped her from the waterline up in the cold with wire brushes and a flat bar until the lead showed. Tobias worked a hand's breadth at a time and swore at the weed in three languages. Calder spliced the white cord through the ring with his left hand doing the work his right could not, and the new fitting went on the clapper — a brass collar, a new tongue, grease in the pivot, a cotter through the eye — and the bellwright tested it by hand and then by ear, and said it would do.

At noon the buoy went back over. She came down hard, took the swell, and the bell rang with it: three clear strokes that belonged to the harbor and to nobody else, rolling out along the wall and off the roofs of the fish market. Under them Calder heard the other thing come, a third of a second late, and his head began to turn.

Bevin's hand came down flat on his shoulder.

"Harbor bell. To starboard."

Calder stopped where he was. "Say it again."

"Harbor bell, to starboard," Bevin said, and wrote the hour and the water on his slate, and Calder stood in the cold with his hands open and let the boy keep the fact for him.

They ate on the punt because there was no room on shore and nobody wanted to stop. Bread, cold fish, onions, and coffee out of the buoy master's flask, and Bevin ate like a boy after his first full tide on the bottom, and Tobias told a story about a bell off Ashtide that rang for a week after it was condemned and could not be got to stop, and Nessa laughed with grease to her elbows and then looked at her own hands and wiped them, which was the nearest thing to a holiday the yard had seen in a month.

The buoy master counted out twenty-six marks in the open on the quay at half past two, the harbor's share first off the top and the crew's share after it, in coin and in small silver, and the clerk wrote the receipt on the buoy's own card and signed it, and the buoy master read the line before he put the money in Calder's hand. It was the first time in three weeks that money had changed hands in that yard with nobody arguing about what it was for.

The rope and the day's food came out of it, and the tallow did not, because Kest's order had put the tallow on the reeve's own repair account and the chandler had taken it straight off that. Eight marks gone, eighteen in the bag.

Nessa took the bag inside and counted it on the bench under the lamp with the door shut, because it is a different thing to be trusted with a yard's money and a different thing again to be told what to do with it.

"Four to the air," she said. "Two to the chandler, which buys a fortnight and not a week. Twelve in the tin for the bond, and the tin is not to be touched for anything that is not the bond."

"That is all of it."

"That is all of it, and it is twelve, and the office wants thirty." She wrote the split in the book and put the tin on the shelf above the labor log with the date on the lid. "Bevin, read me the air line."

Bevin found the page and read it out in the flat voice he had begun using for numbers, because a number said twice is a number and a number said how he felt is not.

"A third gone on the fifteenth. Seven marks of air Monday, four this morning, which is eleven bought, and the book puts the whole pot at a hundred marks to the third. So it is a third gone, less a finger's width, and there is no margin in it at all. A third of that pot does one morning of somebody else's hull and then the morning is gone."

"Write that under the split," Nessa said.

"Write *a third gone, less a little*," Calder said, "and that is enough. Nobody needs the rest of it in the yard's book."

But Bevin wrote the whole of it anyway, in his own hand, at the bottom of the page, with the hour against it.

That afternoon they watched the harbor's lift at the mole from the rail of the fish dock, all of them, and none of them on the mole. A crane on a lighter, a chain, a slip's crew in blue, Kest on the wall with a book and his coat open in the wind. The drifted storm anchor came up out of eight feet off the third seam streaming weed — two fathoms of old iron, Calder's lead line off it where it had parted, and Kest's rag float still on it and still carrying Kest's mark, because nobody had ever been allowed to take that off it. The harbor's men on the chain cheered it when it broke water, and one of them took his hat off, which was not for Calder.

Calder stood back from the rail with his sling on and did not go, because he had been told not to, and let the harbor's tackle go up on the harbor's time at the harbor's cost with the whole of it written in the harbor's book. The *Vigil*'s tender lay a quarter of a mile off on the pale bearing with her oars shipped and did not signal once, and Bevin watched her do it and then watched the anchor go down onto the harbor's own lorry.

"It is theirs," Bevin said beside him. "Proper."

"It is theirs," Calder said. "And it is up, which is more than it was, and nobody in this harbor will ever hear about the two men who found it."

They walked back up the road with the eighteen marks in a bag between Nessa and Tobias and no one talking, the way men walk when there has been a good day and they are afraid to say so.

At the bench Nessa wrote: *20th. Buoy work inside line, harbor charter, one day, paid in full on the master's count and the clerk's slip. Diver tended, did not dive. Rook on the bottom twice and up on the tender's call both times, numbers called back. Hearing still double, crew compensating. Split: 4 air, 2 chandler, 12 tin for the bond. Air pot a third gone, less a little. Crew ate.*

Calder added underneath it, left-handed: *Work going right is still work.*

And under that, because it was true and because the book was read by everybody who worked in that yard: *The air pot is not a third rebuilt. It is a third gone, and it will be a third gone in the spring.*

That night he slept the whole night through without waking, which was the first time in a month, and he did not hear the harbor bell at all.
