# Chapter 2

*The Claim Window*

The reeve's office was a barge with a stove in it, moored inside the west basin against the authority's own stone, and to get to it you had to cross a gangway that had been built for a shorter man and repaired by a taller one.

Calder had the fragment in a sailcloth bundle under his arm, and he had not slept, and there were two people in the office when he came in. One of them was Ors Kest, sitting behind a table that had been planed so often it had gone soft at the edges. The other was a woman in a Registry coat, standing at the window with her back to the stove heat, turning a piece of glass over in her hands.

She had narrow hands with two of them stained at the second joint, the way people get when they have spent years in ink and never quite got it out. On the table beside her lay a pair of brass calipers, a steel rule, a small leather book, and a folded sheet of paper. She was holding the glass by its edges, and she was holding it in a way Calder had seen exactly twice in his life, both times on the seabed, both times with a person who knew the price of what they were touching.

She did not put it down when he came in. She waited until he had crossed the gangway, and she looked at his hands first, then at his face, then at what was under his arm.

"Mr Marr," Kest said. "Sit down. You look like a man who has been up all night with a decision and no money."

"Then I can save you the decision."

"You can save me the decision and give me the money. Sit down."

Calder sat. The stove was going and the office smelled of wet wool, ink, and the particular sourness of a room where men come to argue about paper and are given tea they do not want.

"Now." Kest put his own hands flat on the soft edge of the table, palms down, fingers spread, the way a man holds a thing he has decided not to hit. "Before we go any further I am going to read you what I have written, and you are going to tell me if it is wrong, and I would rather you told me now than in front of a courier."

He read from a sheet.

"You are Calder Marr, of the Free Keel yard, of Morrow Quay. On the day past you raised a fouled mooring chain from the inner face of the mole in the course of paid work, and while doing so you brought up an unclaimed object of a kind the harbor authority has not had a form for. The object was not in your possession before that work began. You hold no permit for it. You hold no claim window. You hold no license of any kind that covers it. The chain you raised is municipal tackle and is now on the authority's stack in the yard, and I have had it valued at eleven marks, and the valuation is yours whether you like it or not."

"Eleven marks is a generous valuation."

"It is a generous valuation and I have written it down, and generosity that is written down is the only kind that survives a hearing." Kest turned the sheet around and pushed it across the table. "Sign that. It says you have read it and that you accept the chain's value and that the glass is held by the Free Keel at the yard pending a claim. Then we can go on for another hour without either of us being accused of something."

Calder read it. He read it twice, the way he read gauges, and he signed the chain's valuation line and the pending-claim line and initialled the margin, and then he put the pen down and did not sign the last paragraph.

"I don't sign that one," he said.

"The last paragraph says that the object is held at the authority's pleasure and may be held for inspection."

"That's not true. It's held in my yard because I put it in my coat and carried it up a mole, and if your courier takes it off me at eight o'clock this morning because I didn't like the word *pleasure* on a piece of paper, then it's held at nobody's pleasure. It's held at a courier's convenience." He pushed the paper back. "I'll sign that the object is on the Free Keel deck and that I'll produce it when a Registry examiner asks to see it, and I'll sign that the harbor authority may inspect it. I won't sign that it can be lifted off me on your word without a form."

Kest looked at the paragraph. He did not look annoyed. He looked, if anything, briefly satisfied, in the way a man is satisfied when the thing he was braced for does not happen.

"The Registry woman will tell you what the Registry wants," he said. "I have no authority to seize glass. I can hold it, and I can delay, and I can embarrass the Registry in my own hearing room, and I cannot take it off you and I could not keep it if I did."

"Then the last paragraph is a courtesy that costs me nothing and you everything," Calder said. "Which is why I won't sign it."

The woman at the window had not moved, and now she turned round.

"Mr Marr," she said. "May I look at it again before we discuss the form."

Her voice was flat and quick and entirely without performance, and she had said *again*, which told him she had looked at it for eleven minutes before he came in the room. Calder got the bundle open on the table, and the sailcloth went damp and stiff where the water had got into it, and the fouled line was still seized in the fold of the glass where he had wrapped it, and he did not offer to explain that.

She did not touch it. She put her hand flat on the table on either side of it, and looked down, and was still for a while.

"May I?"

"You may."

She turned the fragment a quarter at a time. The calipers came out of her coat and she took three measurements — the curve, the thickness at the fat edge, the length of the worked striations — and wrote them down in the small book in a hand like a row of nails. Twice she stopped and put her thumb on a place and left it there while she counted something to herself, and once she said, "There is a second edge," and Calder said, "I know," and she looked up at him sharply and wrote that down too.

"It is not a window," she said. "It is not a bottle. It is not a lantern chimney and it is not Crown tableware, and I have written all of those out already this week for other people." She put the calipers down. "May I ask what you think it is?"

"I think it's a piece off a ship."

"I think it is a piece off a hull that was built, and worked, and then broken. I cannot tell you how old it is. I cannot tell you where it came from, or what it was carrying, or who was near it. I cannot tell you that from this." She set her hand flat on the table on the other side of the glass. "That is the entire content of my examination, and I am obliged to read it into your log, and when I do, your claim goes on the record as a claim over an object of undetermined origin and undetermined age."

"Read it in."

"It will make your claim weaker."

"My claim is a sheet of paper with my name on it. It is already as weak as a thing can be. Make it honest."

She looked at him for a moment with the expression of a woman revising an estimate upward.

"I am Mara Venn," she said. "I am a tide conservator with the Lantern Registry. I examine material, and I settle provenance marks, and I decide whether a piece of glass is physically fit to be moved or handled by a person. I do not decide whether it is true, and I could not if I were asked, and anybody who tells you a Registry examiner decides whether a record is true is telling you about a different office in a different century." She turned to Kest. "Reeve, I have to state for the record that the harbor's referral reached this office before I was assigned, which is irregular, and that I have read the object before I have read the crew's account of where it was found, which is more irregular, and that I have done both because a courier was on the west basin yesterday asking after glass on harbor ground and I would rather look at the object than at the form."

"You looked at the object before the account," Kest said. "Do you want it again?"

"I want the account next, and I want it from the man who took it out of the water, and I want it before I read the form." She looked at Calder. "Not from the log. From you. And I want to know what you did not see, because that is usually the more useful half."

So he told her. The inner face, the third seam, the anchor hook going in and finding a real edge with a thickness to it, the fouling, the four feet of ground line seized into the glass, the drifted anchor of ordinary shoal iron with one fluke driven into the mole's toe. He told it in order because she had asked him to and because there was no version of it in which saying less would be shorter.

"And the line," she said. "You left it on."

"I left it on."

"Why?"

"Because if I cut it off, then in a month I cannot tell you whose line it was, and in a year it is a piece of glass in a box with a guess on it." He shifted his hand on the table. "And because it was the only reason I had for going back down. I could not say where the glass came from. I could say that the glass was in the ground tackle of some boat, lying on her working ground somewhere out past the bar, and that the ground line would say which boat if the line could be read."

"Read how?"

"By the lay of it. And by the seizing. And by the seizing wire." He looked at the fouled line where it lay folded in the cloth. "I have not done that work yet. I would do it with a hand lens and a good bench and a week, and I have a bench and half a week, and I have a chandler's note."

Mara Venn turned the fragment so the fouled line lay uppermost, and bent over it, and did not touch it, and breathed on the strands once so that they stood up in the cold room the way grass stands up in wet weather.

"Lay's right-hand. Tarred hemp, four-strand, laid close." She came up out of the bend. "Morrow Quay cooperage did not lay right-hand. Ash Quay and the Lowfen banks do. A Morrow Quay boat would have left-hand, and if the lay is right-hand then the ground tackle was made east of the marsh, or bought east of the marsh, or — " and she stopped, and her finger went along the seizing.

"Or replaced," Calder said.

"Or replaced. And the seizing wire." She straightened up. "Mr Marr, I am going to say something and I would like you to hear it as what it is, which is a woman reading rope, and not as a woman handing you a place to dive. If this line is made in the Lowfen fashion and it is in the ground tackle of a boat that lost an anchor in a Morrow Quay tide, then that boat was not working off the inside ground this season. She was somewhere along the old shoal, to the south and west, where the ground is bad and the anchorage is old and nobody has gone for anything in a long time."

"You have a bearing."

"I have a rope. The bearing is yours." She wrote for a while, and then, without looking up, said: "Now. The other question."

"I have not got to it yet."

"You are going to." She put the pen down. "You struck this object with a chisel, and you have not told me why you struck it with a chisel, and I know why, because the object is held in cloth and not in a box, and because a man who wanted a piece of glass out of a mole does not hold it against his chest on a cold walk back to a boathouse. Something happened to you on those steps, and it happened through your hand, and it is not on the form, and it is not in the log, and I am the conservator who will have to certify this object for legal handling."

"I chipped a shell off it," Calder said. "The chisel went through the shell and hit the glass. It made a noise."

"What noise?"

"A noise like a bell, coming back late."

"Coming back how late?"

"A hand's width. The width a note takes to run the length of a working line from the far end."

Mara Venn wrote that down. She wrote it down word for word, in the nail hand, on a fresh line, and she did not put a question mark after it, and she did not put a note in the margin either, and that was somehow worse.

She looked up.

"What did you recognize?"

"Nothing."

"Mr Marr."

"I heard a sound. I have been on this water thirty years and I know what a working bell does and I know what a bottle does, and that was neither."

"That is not what I asked." She had not raised her voice and she had not moved, and the room got smaller. "I asked what you recognized. Those are different questions and you have just shown me which one you prefer, which is useful to me, and I want you to know that it is useful, and that it is also the answer I was expecting, and that the answer I was expecting is still an answer."

Calder looked at the stove.

"Someone taught me to recognize it," he said. "No. Not taught. Somebody I knew used to make noises with his mouth, and this is a family thing, and it is nothing, and it is in my ears and not in the object."

Mara Venn looked at him for four or five seconds. Then she wrote one more line, and turned the book so that he could read it upside down, which was either careless or a deliberate and unkind test.

It said: *Crew reports a personal association with the material, stated as unverified, not material to provenance. Conservator has not been told its basis.*

"If you want that changed," she said, "you will have to tell me its basis. If you do not, it stands, and I will read it into the log exactly as written, and it will be the only sentence in the log that nobody can check."

"Leave it."

"I am going to leave it. And I want you to notice that I left it, because the day comes when you need a Registry examiner to have written down that you told her something, and she did not, and she chose that."

She turned the book back, and picked up the pen, and read the whole of her own examination out loud in the flat voice, and it was nine lines long, and none of the nine lines said anything Calder did not already know. Then she signed it, and wrote the office and the hour, and gave it to Kest, and Kest read it and countersigned it in his careful hand.

"There," he said. "Now there is a document. On the strength of that document you have a claim window of four days, from the hour it is stamped, over one object of undetermined origin lifted from harbor ground in the course of paid work, and the object stays on your deck and comes to me when I ask." He laid his hand flat on the paper. "Understand what you have, Marr. You do not have a claim. You have four days in which to turn that word *undetermined* into something a person can be paid for, and if you cannot, the window closes and the object goes to the Registry as unsettled, and after that nobody in this harbor will ever see it again, including me."

"What happens to the drifted anchor?"

"That is municipal tackle and it stays where it is, tagged, on my ground, until somebody brings me a crane and a tide." He picked up the chain valuation. "And the float you put on it last night has my mark on it, so it is my mark and not yours, and if you touch that iron before I bring a lift, I will hold you under the harbor charter and I will win, and it will not be close."

The woman at the window put her calipers in her coat and buttoned it.

"I will be at the yard this evening," she said. "I want the glass laid out on a bench with a lamp and a cloth, and I want the fouled line wet and slack, and I want whoever cut the shell off it to stop cutting anything off it. And I want the position mark, in your hand, with the time on it."

"It's on a slate."

"Then bring the slate." She was already at the door. "And Mr Marr — if you go and look at the shoal, you will not find the boat by the rope. You will find her by what is lying next to her, and what is lying next to her may be considerably older than she is."
