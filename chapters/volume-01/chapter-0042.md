# Chapter 42

*The Door Shut on the Twenty-Second*

The reeve's barge had the stove going and the window steamed over and the door shut, and the clerk was not in it, which Calder had not known was possible and thought about for a day afterward.

Kest stood with the purpose in both hands and read all six pages into his own book, out loud, in the order they were written, and he read slowly and he did not summarize a clause or skip a line, and it took twenty-two minutes, and the only other sound in the barge was the stove and the water under the floorboards shifting against the quay.

At the end of the fourth page he stopped for about a second, which was a thing he had never done while reading, and then went on.

When he had finished he closed the book, and put his hands flat on it, and looked at Calder Marr across a table with a soft edge.

"Now I am going to ask you one question and I told you on Monday I was not going to be kind about it."

"Ask it."

"You have told this office in your fourth part what the ground is. Three places on one bearing where the bottom comes up, and the first of them is a bedded seam in my mole's own foundation, and the second of them is a transparent keel at eight fathoms on a permit of the twelfth of October, and the third of them is a figure out of my own book at about two miles and a quarter from this quay. That is the ground, and you have told me all of it correctly and I have read it twice and there is not one word in that page I would have written differently."

"Then the question is not about the ground."

"The question is about the *place*." Kest took his hand off the book. "Marr, I have read a six-page purpose of which nine lines ask for nothing, and a fourth page that describes a piece of coast from a mole to two miles and a quarter out, and a sixth page that promises a stated reason before every bottom, and a named tender, and a cutoff, and a book four men can read. And there is not one sentence in six pages that says where this crew intends to be when it is working under the paper it is asking for."

The stove ticked.

"Name it," Calder said.

"That is the question. I am not going to name it for you."

"Then here is the answer, and it is the true one and it is not an answer you will like. The second of the three places is on ground the *Vigil* has claimed since the fourteenth of October and inside a bar of four hundred yards off the outer marks that the yard has sworn twice in a room not to go near. The third of them is the same water, a mile further out, and the yard has no instrument that reaches it and never had. The first of them is under three feet of my mole's own stone at the third and fourth seams, in water I closed on the thirty-first of October, on a face my office has a plan countersignable only by the office that wrote the notice, and my plan was returned uncountersigned on the twelfth with four grounds, the first of which was correct."

"So the ground of this purpose is four feet of stone and a mile and a half of water and a reeve's word."

"The ground of this purpose is *nowhere*," Kest said, "and the holder of it is standing in my office telling me that in the order a man admits a thing he has got wrong, which I notice and am not going to thank you for, because a purpose that does not know its own ground is not a purpose that is cautious, it is a purpose that has not been written yet, and if you file it and I read it into my book and the naval officer reads my book in December, then in a year there is a document in a room in Veyr with six pages on it and the words *the crew does not know what the three places are* in the fourth of them, and a man will read those two sentences next to each other and he will be right, and he will be right about a thing that is not what you are frightened of, and I will not be able to tell him otherwise because the paper will be in a file and I will not be in the room."

"I did not know it on Friday."

"You did not know it on Friday and you filed it on Monday and you are here on the twenty-second at ten in the morning and it has taken you a day to find out, and I am going to write that in my book in these words: *The purpose of the twenty-first does not name the ground on which the holder proposes to work, and the holder states that he does not know it.* Enter it."

The clerk was not in the barge. Kest wrote it in his own hand in his own book, in the margin, with the hour against it, and turned the book round so that Calder Marr could read a reeve's own handwriting upside down, which was a thing he had never done and which was not an accident.

"Will that be read in Veyr," Calder said.

"It will be read in Veyr on the twenty-eighth before anybody leaves the room, because I am not going to read a purpose into a hearing record on the twenty-eighth that I have not read into the record before, and the twenty-eighth is the fourth time this office has read your words in a room with a naval officer in it and I have made my own position on that plain on the seventh of November and I am not going to take it back on the twenty-eighth." Kest shut the book. "And I will tell you the thing about it that nobody will ever ask me, because nobody will ever think of it. I have written in my own book this morning that the Free Keel yard does not know where it means to work, and I have written it in the margin, which is the part of a reeve's book that the clerk does not copy. In a year a man in a court in Veyr will read the body of my entry and he will not see the margin, and the record will say the yard filed a purpose, and the record will not say it was a hole. That is not a trick and I am not proud of it, and I am telling you I have done it, because if I am going to do it I would rather the man it is done for knew."

Calder Marr stood in a barge on the west basin with the stove behind him and understood that he had been given something by a man who had no reason to give it, and that the something was not a favor, because favors can be refused and this could not.

---

He walked back up the row with the envelope under his arm and did not open the door of the shed until he had stood in the yard for a minute, and when he came in Bevin Rook was at the bench with the book open and a stub of pencil in his right hand and Tobias Wren was at the far end with his hat on his knee.

"Read it in," Calder said.

"Which of it."

"The entry. Say what he wrote and then read back the margin, because I am going to tell you what is in the margin and I am not going to have you read it out of his book without knowing."

Bevin Rook looked at the book on the bench and did not touch it, because it was the harbor authority's book and it was in Kest's barge and it was not here, and the whole of what he said in the next four seconds was the difference between a reader and a clerk.

"You have got it in your head, then," he said. "Say it and I will write it in ours."

---

The mole work came on the Thursday, the Friday and the Saturday and it paid thirty-four marks, and Calder did not go near the copying room for a week.

It was the first and second seams and it was a capstan job off the wall with a tide and no diver and no boat and no pump, and the six-seam capstan and its pawl and forty foot of chain came off a coaster's ring that had been under forty pound of weed since Michaelmas, and a Lowfen owner paid it in the road on the Saturday afternoon in coin and small silver and read the line before he put the money in Calder's hand, which is what everybody in this trade does and which no man in a fish market thinks is worth mentioning.

Three days, thirty-four on the board, paid in the road on the Saturday afternoon in coin and small silver. The harbor's eighth three and the harbor's share three off the top, rope and the day's food three, five marks of air into the pot, and six on the chandler's note, which came to six on the note in those three days and brought it, by the yard's own count, to thirteen weeks into six, and which the chandler at the sign of the Drowned Bell had not written a new figure against because nobody had asked him to and he was not going to be the man who offered.

The bag closed at nineteen and Bevin Rook read the line out twice.

"Thirty-four in and twenty out and nineteen in the bag, and the note at thirteen weeks by us and eleven in his book, and the whole of that nineteen is spoken for to the last mark except one, and I would like somebody to tell me what the one is for before the first of December, because I am the reader of this book and I would like to read one entry where a mark is not spoken for."

"That is a good question and I do not have an answer to it," Calder said.

---

Mara Venn came at half past four on the twenty-second with her book shut under her arm and her coat buttoned to the throat and nothing in her hands at all, which was the first time in a month Calder Marr had seen her come into that yard with nothing in her hands.

"I am not bringing you a document," she said. "I am bringing you a date, and I have walked down here in the cold to say it out loud to four people instead of writing it, because a date that is only written is a date that a clerk owns."

"The twenty-second," Nessa said.

"The twenty-second. It is dated today, and it is dated today because a circuit officer countersigned my refusal of the ground on the document this morning, and my refusal has left my office, and the office has entered the date and put its hand under mine, and it is now a paper in a circuit office in this city in a drawer with a man's name on the drawer, and there is not one person in Morrow Quay who can put a hand on it."

"What is in it," Calder said.

"Four grounds, in the order I wrote them, and the office date under them, and my hand, and the officer's initials, and nothing else, and it is eight pages of a file in which the *Vigil*'s claim is a lodging and my name is a query." She put her hands in her coat pockets. "And the reason I am here is not the refusal. The reason I am here is that the certificate the circuit office asked for on the eleventh cannot now be produced by anybody, at any price, in any time before your sitting, and therefore the ground description of the seventh and the eighth of this month is going into your hearing on Monday uncertified and is going to stay uncertified through Monday, and if it is going to be turned on in Veyr in February then it will be turned on with a yard's own figures and a fourth *not stated* and no hand in a Registry coat anywhere near any of it."

"I have not asked you for it," Calder said.

"You asked her for it on the eleventh at six in the morning before you had thought about it and she refused you in front of the room and you did not take the asking back, and that is the single most useful thing that has happened to this yard since the first of October and I have never once said so and I am not going to say it now." Mara Venn looked at the three of them in turn. "I have about a quarter of the standing that lets me refuse anything, and by Christmas I will have none of it, and I will then be a conservator whose certificates take three weeks and who cannot say no to a clerk. I would like that written down in your book with the date on it, not because it helps you, which it does not, and not because it helps me, which it does not, but because in about four years a man in a room is going to read what this office did to a woman who said no four times and he is going to be looking for a place where it began, and it began on the eleventh and it will end before the year does and there is going to be nobody in the file who wrote either of those dates down but her."

Bevin Rook wrote it down. He wrote the date and the hour and the whole of it, and read it back twice, and Nessa countersigned, and Calder Marr signed it left-handed at the foot with the three names above him, and the four of them stood in a shed on the twenty-second of November with a purpose in an envelope that named no ground and a certificate in a drawer in the west of the city that could not be produced, and neither of those two facts had ever been in the same room before and both of them were now on a page in a book that four men could reach.

"That is the whole of what I have," Mara Venn said at the door. "I am not thanked and I do not want to be, and I am going to say one more thing and then I am going home in the cold, and it is a thing about Monday and not about the certificate.

"On Monday there will be a man in a corner with a notebook who has read every paper this yard owns except the one that has not been written, and he is going to hear a reeve read six pages into a record and then ask a man the question that the six pages do not answer, and the man is going to have to stand in a room and say *I do not know where I mean to work*, and then the room is going to have nothing to do on the twenty-eighth except decide whether to give a paper to a crew that cannot say what it will do with it.

"You need the ground before Monday. Not the answer. The ground. And if you cannot find it, then write down that you could not find it, and file that, and let the reeve read it with the door shut on Saturday the twenty-sixth, because a yard that says on Saturday what it could not find is worth more in that room on Monday than a yard that has found something overnight and cannot say how."

She went up the road. Tobias Wren put his hat on the bench.

"That woman has been in this business eight years," he said, "and she came down here in the cold with nothing in her hands to hand us the worst news of the month, and she did it because she thought we would do something with it by Monday. Which is more than the reeve will have done, and he is the one with a stove."

"Then we find the ground," Nessa Pike said.
