# Chapter 28

*Three Points and One Line*

The shoal book came up out of the stern locker at ten past nine on the second of the month, in two parts, in boards, in a tin, exactly as it had on the twenty-sixth, and the clerk wrote the date and the hour and the words *one book, read in room, not copied* and turned it round and stood back.

It was a Wednesday and not the Thursday the reeve had named, and nobody in the yard asked about it, because the Thursday had gone on a purpose filed in a hearing room, and the Friday and the Saturday on a chain that came tight, and the Monday on a notice with a seal over the nail, and by the time there was a morning to give the figure away in, the yard wanted it five days before a hearing and not two.

Kest had agreed the third station himself, in his own hand, at the bottom of a sheet on the twenty-eighth, and the sheet said: *For the purposes of a bearing only, taken from the top of the mole, by any person, at any hour, off the office's own compass, the office having no objection to a station on its own ground that does not approach his own ground.* It was signed *O. Kest* and Calder had read it nine times and had not been able to make it into a favor and had stopped trying.

"Read it yourself," Kest said. "I have told the clerk once and if I tell him twice he will write twice."

Calder found the entry and read it aloud.

*The course run this spring for the outer ground. Course as marked. Lead on a wire paid over the port bow and marked at the staff every ten fathoms of wire. Station taken at the time the wire was paid out and corrected for wire and for the hour of the tide, and the corrected figure is the figure of this office and the uncorrected figure is not.*

Station six. The wire was paid out to eighteen fathoms and two, and the corrected figure is fifteen fathoms and two. And the stations before and after it — five and seven — were sixteen fathoms and three, both of them, both sides.

"Sixteen three against fifteen two," Calder said. He did it in his head twice, the way he read a gauge. "That is a foot and an inch. In two miles of course."

"A foot and an inch of bottom on a firm clay flat, at one figure, out of thirty-six figures on two miles of course, in water that no person in this harbor has any right to sound." Kest turned the book a quarter turn so the whole of the line was visible. "Read the neighbours again. Five is sixteen three. Seven is sixteen three. Eight is sixteen four. Nine is sixteen three. That is a flat, and the flat is clay, and the office has put *firm clay* against every figure on that course for sixty years because a man sounding a course once a year can tell the difference between a bottom that gives a lead a chance to sink and one that does not, and this one does not."

"And there is a second thing and it is the whole of why that figure has sat in a book for sixty years being nothing," Kest said. "A wire on a lead pays out longer than the water is deep. That course pays out between two fathoms and three fathoms more wire than there is water at every station on it, and this office has corrected for it, every figure, since before I came in, and it is in the second column and nobody has ever read the second column. An uncorrected line on that course puts the sixth station within two fathoms of its neighbours, and two fathoms is a boat. A foot and an inch is a mast. The number has been sitting here in a column nobody reads, and the reason nobody has read it is that there has never been a reason to compare a station to its neighbours on a course that is flat, because a man does not doubt a flat."

---

"Nessa says clay does not make sand waves."

"Nessa Pike does not need to be in this office for that, and she is right, and it is the only good sentence anybody has said about that figure in three years and I have said it to two clerks and neither of them wrote it down." He squared the book. "Now you have your number and I want three things said in the room before you take your hand off it.

"One. I will hear a figure out of a book this office wrote, in this room, in your own hand, and the figure is fifteen fathoms and two at the sixth station of a course run in the spring. I will not hear a bearing taken toward that figure, and if you have taken one I will strike it, and I will not tell you which.

"Two. The figure is a rise in a bottom. It is not an object. It is not glass, and it is not iron, and it is not a wreck, and it is not a seam, and no office in this harbor or in Veyr has any instrument that will tell you what is under sixteen fathoms of water at two miles and a quarter from the mole, and the reason there is no such instrument is a paper that came on the fourteenth of last month with a man in a cutter's coat on my mole. So the number is a number and you may write it down and you may not say what it is.

"Three. You may write the three points as three points. You may not draw a line between them."

"I was not going to draw a line between them."

"You were going to lay your bearing sheet on the table and put a nib between the mole and the shoal and run it out west, and then you were going to say the words *one line*, and every word of that is fine, and none of it can go on a piece of paper in this harbor, because a line drawn between three points on a chart is not an observation. It is a route. And a route is a claim about where a man should put a boat, and the ground that route is drawn across is claimed by a vessel of the Crown Navy, and I am not going to hold in my own book a route to ground that a naval officer can read as an invitation."

"It does not have to be an invitation."

"It is an invitation with three points on it and no opinion attached, which is the kind of invitation that gets a man's boat turned over." Kest's hands went behind his back. "Write three points and a mile and two hundred yards. If a man in the fish market wants to know whether they are in a row he can take a ruler out of his own pocket and find out for himself, and then it is his ruler and his finding and not my book. And the reason I want it that way is not the officer. It is that a thing four people can check is worth something and a thing one man can draw is worth nothing at all."

---

The arithmetic was done on the bench with four men round it, which it had not been since the twelfth.

Nessa had the crew's book open at the entry of the twenty-eighth and Bevin Rook had a slate and a new stub of pencil, and Tobias stood behind them saying nothing at all, which in a yard with a boy's hand in a sling is the most valuable thing in the room.

"A to B," Bevin said. "Mole's toe, third and fourth seams, the twenty-eighth, from the boat on the outer side with the office's compass and a hand lead, ten feet and ten and a half at the north end. B is the shoal's inner edge where the run was sighted on the twelfth, eight fathoms, on the three bearings that Wren, Rook and Marr took inside four degrees. A to B by those three bearings is a mile and two hundred yards."

"Again."

"A mile and two hundred yards. And B to C is the sixth station of the office's course, fifteen fathoms and two corrected, on the same bearing west-southwest, and the distance out from the shoal's inner edge by the office's own run is a mile and two hundred and ten yards."

"There is your ten yards."

"There is my ten yards." Bevin put the pencil down. "And I have been sitting here for an hour and I want to say the thing that is going to be said about it and I would rather it was said at this bench by me than at the hearing by a naval officer. A foot and an inch of bottom is nothing. A foot and an inch is a stone. A foot and an inch is a spar with the bottom out of it. A mile and two hundred yards is a long way, and it is a long way whatever else it is, and if you had gone out and put a lead on that course yourself you would have found the same inch, because a bottom does that."

"Yes," Nessa said.

"So what is the thing that is not nothing," Bevin said.

Calder looked at the book, at the two entries, and at the third figure that was not in either book because it was in a tin in a barge on the other side of the harbor.

"Thirty-six figures," he said. "On two miles of course. Thirty-five of them are sixteen three, sixteen four, sixteen three, and the thirty-sixth is fifteen two. That is not a rise in a bottom. That is one figure in thirty-six. And it is a mile and two hundred and ten yards from a rib we put a hand on in ten feet of water, and a mile and two hundred yards from a run of transparent keel with eleven keys in it that we sounded on the twelfth with a lead and a line, and a bright run, no break, no join, running west, that I wrote in the dark with a pencil stub and pressed hard enough to feel the point through the back of the slate."

"And it is a mile between three things that are each a mile apart," Tobias said, from behind them. "A wrecks field does not do that, Marr. I have seen the *Kittiwake*'s own ground after a bad blow and there was iron on that bottom the way there is weed on a stone, and it was where the water had put it. Water does not sort a field into a mile."

"Water did not lay it," Calder said. "Somebody laid it."

"Somebody laid something," Nessa said. "I am not going to let that sentence out of this shed in that form and I want you to hear why, and I want to hear it now, in front of Wren and Rook, so that there is a date on it and not only an opinion in your head.

"Three things a mile apart, on a straight, on a bottom that is flat, is order. Order is what a man sees. Order is not what it is *for*, and it is not even that it was *made*, because a line of stone on a bottom is also a line, and a wreck that broke up along a shoal's edge can put three pieces a mile apart, and if you write *somebody laid it* you have written a conclusion, and a conclusion can be taken apart in a room in eleven minutes by a man with a ruler, and when a conclusion goes, everything under it goes with it, and what will be left on the market wall will be a piece of paper with three numbers and no numbers at all." She capped the pen. "Write the numbers. Write the foot and the inch. Write *one figure in thirty-six*. Write *even to ten yards*. And then write *what it is for is not known to this crew, and no person in this harbor has been asked.*"

"And struck out is what I said on the twenty-seventh," Calder said, "that the ground is placed and not scattered."

"Struck out of the yard's copy, and left standing in the hearing record where the reeve read it in, because you filed it and it is in his book and it cannot come out." He had the slate in his hand. "Marr, I do not want to be the man who told this yard to take the word *placed* out of a paper. The word is in the record. If a clerk reads the record in January he will read a purpose that says the ground is placed and not scattered and eleven pages later he will read a yard's book that says nobody knows what it is for, and he will not know which one the yard believes, and that is a good thing, and it is the most honest shape a record can be in."

Calder looked at the book for a while.

"Struck out in ours," he said. "And not one word changed on the hearing record, and I will write a line under it saying I have struck it out of ours and not out of the reeve's, with the date on it, and anybody who wants to know when the yard stopped believing it can put the two pages side by side and see it was the second of this month."

Nessa wrote it, and read it back, and Bevin read it again, and Tobias put his hand flat on the bench on the last line and said, "That is the first thing you have written in six weeks that I would put my name to."

---

The bearing was taken off the top of the mole at half past four, with the office's compass out of the office and the sun gone down behind the fish market roof, and Calder went up the mole steps alone on the strength of the sheet the reeve had signed on the twenty-eighth and of nothing else that was written, because the top of a mole is not the toe and not the face, and a man may stand on it.

The mole is a hundred and forty feet of stone and it is eight feet high at the head, and from the head at the top of the flood a man can see the bottom in the slop, because the surface of the slop goes to a sheet of glare, and there is no other water in this harbor where a man can see what is lying on the bottom.

He did not look for the rib. He took the bearing and wrote it and came down.

And on the way down, at the fourth step from the top, the tide bell went out in the road, three strokes, and the strokes arrived, and under them, three marks and a flick, a third of a second late and too even to be a bell, the note came up out of the bottom off the mole, and Calder's hands stayed exactly where they were on the stone.

He stood on the mole steps and let it come and go, twice, and then went down off the mole and up the road to the yard at a fast walk with the compass in the bag, and Bevin Rook was standing at the bench with the book open and the pencil ready before Calder had got the gate.

"Half past four," Calder said. "Top of the make, west-northwest light air, slop on the mole face. I heard the harbor's three strokes and I heard the delayed note under it, and I did not turn round and I did not go back up. There is no source and I am not writing what it was."

Bevin wrote it, and read it back, and it went in under the hour and the state of the water with the line: *Knock, delayed, in the bottom off the mole. No source. Not written as a voice. Heard by Marr at the mole head, top of the mole, not the toe and not the face, with the office's station in his hand and a man waiting in the yard.*

And Calder did not see that line until the end of the day, when Nessa read the entry out over his shoulder the way she read everything, and read it a second time before she put the book down, and neither of them said one word about it, because the standing rule was that the man who hears a knock in a bottom does not get to look at the pencil first, and that rule had now cost him something four times and given him something once, and he was not going to argue with a rule that had kept its side of the bargain while he had not.

---

They put the three points on the market wall on the third of the month, in the yard's own hands, without a conservator's name and without the reeve's name, on three sheets, and there was no line drawn between them anywhere on the three sheets.

*First point. Mole's toe, third and fourth seams. Bedded pale material in the mole's foundation, running north under the rubble to two boat lengths, deeper at the north end. Three keys seen. Counted and stated at the ladder by B. Rook, 28th, entered as the tender's count by Marr, three figures in the same figure, bedded, not lying loose. Inside the line. Harbor tide-gang present and two of its men in the water's edge.*

*Second point. Gannet Shoal, inner edge, eight fathoms, on the work permit of the 12th of last month. A transparent keel rib with a continuous keel line and eleven keys, running west-southwest on a dead straight, no break and no join. Measured, drawn, and a measurement entered in the labor log, Kest countersigned. Bearing taken by three men, agreeing within four degrees, hour and man's name against each.*

*Third point. The sixth station of the harbor authority's own annual course over the outer ground, taken in the spring of this year. Fifteen fathoms and two corrected, the wire having been paid out to eighteen fathoms and two. The stations either side of it, and the other thirty-four, are sixteen fathoms and three and sixteen fathoms and four. Firm clay. The figure read in the reeve's office on the 2nd of this month, in the presence of the clerk, out of the harbor authority's own book, written out here in the yard's own hand and not copied.*

*Distance, first to second, a mile and two hundred yards. Second to third, a mile and two hundred and ten yards. The difference is ten yards. The figures are the tender's count and the office's corrected count and are not the same kind of number, and the tolerance of the method is stated by the men who took them and is not better than ten yards.*

*What this shows. Three places on one straight where the bottom comes up, and the bottom comes up nowhere else on two miles of a sounded course. It does not show what is under sixteen fathoms of water at two miles and a quarter from the mole. It does not show that the thing at the first point and the thing at the second point and the thing at the third point are one thing, or three things, or anything at all. It does not show a purpose, and no person in this harbor has been asked, and this crew will not say.*

Then, at the foot of the third sheet, in Calder's own left hand, four lines under a rule.

*The word placed is struck out of this yard's copy of the purpose filed with the harbor authority on the 27th of last month, and it stands uncorrected in the hearing record where the reeve read it in, and the yard does not ask for that to be altered. The yard believed it on the 27th and the yard does not know it on the 2nd, and both are in the record and both are dated. The reeve's ruling of the 2nd is that no line may be drawn between the three points, and the yard has not drawn one, and the distance between them is a mile and two hundred yards, and any man who wants to know whether three things on a straight are in a row may take a ruler out of his own pocket and find out for himself.*

And the slate stayed on its own shelf where it had been since the fourteenth of last month, with its own heading on it, headed as the evidence heard by Calder Marr, unverified, from himself, and not corroborated by any document, witness, instrument, or second person, and the one false line in it, and the shelf it stood on a hand's breadth from the bench chest and four feet from the book, and not one word added to it on the second of this month, because a man's own account of what he heard in the bottom of a bell is a different kind of document from a figure that four people can go and check with a lead, and the yard keeps the two apart for that reason and for no other reason at all.

And the seals, and the names, and the five rolled copies in oilcloth in the bench chest and three of them on other crews' walls, and the wall in the fish market with a Registry notice nailed over the top of it and a rule under both of them.
