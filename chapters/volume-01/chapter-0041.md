# Chapter 41

*What the Purpose Leaves Out*

The purpose had to be in Calder Marr's own hand and it had to be with the harbor authority by the twenty-first, and there were three days between the fourteenth of November and the eighteenth on which nothing at all happened, because the defense was discharged and the bottom was gone and the tank of nothing the yard was living on went on being nothing.

They began it on the Friday.

Nessa Pike cleared the north bench at seven in the morning, which she had not done since the second of October, and put the stove in the middle of it instead of at the end, and made Bevin Rook bring the crew's book and put it flat in front of her with the spine squared to the edge of the bench, and then made him go and stand at the far end of the shed by the stove-pipe with his arms folded, which he did, and which was the first sensible thing anybody in that yard had done with him since the thirtieth.

"You are not writing it," she said. "You are reading it back. That is the only job you have today and you will have it every time there is a page. Read it the way you read the air, and if you cannot say a sentence in one breath it is a sentence that has two things in it and it comes apart in a room."

"I can say a sentence in one breath."

"You can say *a mile and two hundred yards* in one breath. You could not say the twenty-four marks for the hand and the sixteen for the reading in one breath and you said both in the hearing room on the fourteenth and it took you four seconds and the officer wrote something down while you were saying it."

"I did not know he was going to write something down."

"That is the whole of what a reader is, Bevin," Nessa said. "You do not know who is going to write something down."

---

The purpose was six parts and Calder wrote them in the order a clerk would want them and not in the order he wanted them, and the difference between those two orders was the whole of his week.

The first part was who they were. A Free Keel crew of the harbor line, four hands and three and a half, holding a labor chain found proved and countersigned on the twenty-fourth of October and not disturbed since, in a shed on the fish dock with a bench, a book, a chest of drawings and a stack of iron on which one piece had a saw kerf in its crown shackle that nobody could account for and a third of the sale entered to another crew who had asked to be paid in February.

The second part was what they asked for. A one-season provisional working charter on harbor ground inside the line, running to the last day of March next, with the harbor's eighth and the harbor's share of any raising, and the crew's own share retained at the figure the crew had already agreed among themselves and not altered by the granting of anything.

The third part was what they did not ask for, and Calder wrote that part four times, and each version was shorter than the one before, and the last one was nine lines and had no sentence in it that could be read in a room as a complaint.

They did not ask for the notice of the thirty-first to be altered, narrowed, re-served or lifted. They did not ask for the four hundred yard bar off the outer marks to be lifted, and had no intention of going within it. They did not ask for a supervised look at the thing at the fifth seam, or for a plan, or for a countersign, or for a circuit officer. They did not ask for the custody room, or for either of the two items in it, or for a comparison of anything in it with anything outside it. They did not ask for anything outside the harbor line and they had no instrument that would let them have it. And they did not ask for the Second Bell license, which was a Crown qualification, and which no man in the harbor authority had the power to hand out, and which nobody in that shed was going to get by asking for it in the wrong room.

"That third part is the best writing in it," Nessa said, at four in the afternoon, "and it is the part a man in a cutter's coat will read twice and the part the reeve will read once and be glad of, and neither of them is reading it for the reason you wrote it."

"I wrote it for the reason a man reads it."

"Yes," Nessa said. "And the reason a man reads it and the reason you wrote it are going to come apart in a room, and the room will find the difference, and the room always finds the difference, and the only question is whether it finds it on the twenty-eighth or on the twenty-ninth of next month."

---

The fourth part was the ground.

It was the three points, and it was the three points without the ten yards, because the ten yards had been withdrawn on the fourteenth in front of a clerk, and what is in no record and on no wall is the difference stated as a measurement, the withdrawal itself being on a fresh sheet nailed under the Navy's paper where a man has to lift a corner to read it, and neither of those was going in a purpose either. So the ground went in as three places on one bearing where the bottom comes up, and nowhere else on two miles of a course the harbor authority itself sounded, with the first of them entered as what it was, which was a bedded seam of transparent material in the mole's own foundation running north under the rubble to about two boat lengths, of which the crew had used the south end as a position and had said so in its own book on the fourteenth, and with the tender's count of it being nine feet at the south end and ten and a half at the north end and the diver's own count of the same thing being seven and eleven and entered as given by Rook on his own statement.

And then the load-bearing sentence, which Calder wrote last and which is the reason a Free Keel yard in a fish market has anything at all in four months.

The crew does not know what the three places are, does not offer a figure for the interval between them, and will not be asked to say what any of them is for, and no person in this harbor has been asked.

"That has been in your book since the fourth," Bevin said from the far end of the shed.

"It has been in my book since the fourth and it is going in a filed purpose on Monday, and the difference between those two things is four months of a reeve's authority and a clerk's stamp."

"Read it back."

"It is in front of you."

"It is in front of me and you have not read it and that is the last time anybody will be in a position to say you did not read it." Bevin came over and put his right hand flat on the page, the way he did. "Sixth. That the crew works under this charter, if it is granted, with a stated reason entered before every bottom, a named tender who is not the diver, a cutoff the tender can pull without asking anybody, and a book that four men in this yard can read the same day it is written. And that the yard will not ask the harbor authority to lift the third clause of the notice of the thirty-first, and will work under it, and will say in this room and in every room after it that a clause which stops three named people from earning a living inside their own harbor line has been read in a Registry as a measure about glass and is not one."

"That is the sixth part."

"That is the sixth part and it is a page and a half and it is the best thing in this shed since the fourth of November, and I want to ask you something and you are going to say no."

"Ask."

"It is not in here." Bevin took his hand off the page. "You have asked for a charter. A charter is a permission to work, and to work you have to be in the water, and there is not one word in six pages about being in the water, and the reeve's own undertaking of the twenty-fifth of October says you are off the bottom till spring, and a charter does not say a word about a reeve's undertaking because a charter is not that office's instrument and it cannot reach it. So on Monday you are going to stand in a room and ask for paper and you are going to be handed paper, and you are going to be standing on this mole on the twenty-eighth of November with a charter in your coat and no bottom, and we will have spent a winter being a legal yard."

The stove ticked. Calder looked at the six pages on the bench for a while.

"You are right," he said.

"I know I am right, and I would rather be wrong and told so in a shed than right and have it come out in a room on the twenty-eighth with a naval officer in it."

"That is the best sentence anybody has said in this yard in a month, and I am writing it down and you are going to read it back." Calder took the pencil. "It goes at the top of the next page, in your words, dated today, and it goes in the book and not in the purpose, because the purpose is a paper about paper and a reeve cannot read a man's private arithmetic in a room, and I am not going to stand in front of a clerk on Monday and tell him what I have got wrong, because I have not got it wrong yet, I have got it incomplete, and a man with an incomplete purpose may be helped and a man with a wrong one may only be laughed at."

"That is the first thing you have said all week that I believe you have thought about in advance."

"I have thought about nothing else since Tuesday night," Calder said, and Bevin read it back twice, and Nessa countersigned, and the eighth of November's page went into the book with the sixth of December written on it in Calder's own left hand and no date against it yet.

---

His father was at the far bench with a gunwale in his hand on the Friday afternoon, and he had been there since eight and had not said anything to anybody, and on the Saturday morning he was not there.

Nobody said anything about it at first. Bevin Rook said the name of a ship and Tobias Wren said a thing about a fairlead and Nessa Pike wrote, and at eleven o'clock on the Saturday Nessa sent the boy down to the office at the end of the row to ask, and the boy came back and said that Alden Marr had sent word by the fish-dock boy that he was not coming in and would not be coming in for a few days, and that it was the cough, and that he was not to be disturbed and was not to be sent for.

"Two days," Bevin said. "He is sixty-one and he has not missed a morning in this yard since Michaelmas."

"He has missed since the second of October and I have been in this yard for all of it," Nessa said, "and I know what I am looking at and it is not a cold. It is a man who read two hundred and forty words on a slate on the Sunday night and worked at the near end of his own bench for two hours without speaking, and who has been warm for six days, and who has now gone back inside."

"He will come back in."

"He will come back in and he will be at the far bench and he will not be in the north end, and there will be a gap of four feet between the two of them for the rest of the winter." Nessa capped the pen. "Write it, and do not write a reason, and do not let anybody in this yard go and ask him for one, and if a man off a cutter's boat comes to this shed and asks where Alden Marr is, the answer is that he is at his bench and not well, and there is no more to it than that."

Calder wrote it. Nineteenth of November, Saturday: *Alden Marr not in the yard and has sent word it is the cough and is not to be sent for. Not chased. No reason given and none asked for.*

And then he did not go, which was the thing that cost, and he noticed the not going about four o'clock in the afternoon when the light went flat on the west side of the mole, and he stood at the bench with the sixth page in front of him and thought about a man of sixty-one in a cold shed four streets away with a cough that had been coming on a forty-minute cycle since before Calder was born, and about the eight words the man had said on the Sunday, and about a sentence he had not been asked for.

He did not go. Bevin Rook read the whole of the sixth page back at five o'clock and did not know any of it was about anything, and Nessa Pike took the pages off the bench and squared them and put them in an envelope with the notice of the twenty-eighth inside it, because a purpose is a paper and a paper wants an envelope, and Calder Marr signed across the flap and did not thank her.

Then there were twenty-one hours to go before the office opened on Monday morning, and he went home along the row with his hands in his pockets and stopped at his own gate, which was his father's gate and had been for eleven years, and stood in the road in the dark for a while without going in.

---

The purpose went in on the Monday the twenty-first, with the door shut and without a hearing, and Calder Marr gave it to the harbor authority's clerk in the flat voice of a man handing a paper across a counter.

Kest read the whole of it standing up, twice, in a barge with a stove in it, and did not sit down for either, and he did not interrupt once, and at the end he put it down on the soft edge of the table with both hands flat on it.

"You have written nine lines asking for nothing," he said.

"I have written nine lines asking for nothing and three pages asking for a paper, and both halves are true and I would rather you read them in that order than the other."

"I read them in the order they were written." He did not look up. "Mr Marr, this is the fourth purpose this office has had from you in seven weeks, and it is the first one that does not put a thing in the record that a room can use. The first one said *placed*. The second one put a drawing on this office's table. The third one was two hundred and forty words with a brother left out of them. And this one says, in nine lines, what it does not want, and one of the things in the list of what it does not want is the thing I have spent this month trying to get off the table."

"Then let it stay off the table."

"It will stay off the table," Kest said, "and on Monday it will be in a hearing record in this harbor and it will go to the circuit office and it will go to a cutter's boat by the middle of January, and every office that reads it is going to read nine lines of a yard that is not asking for anything and is going to ask itself what a crew writes nine lines of not asking for, and the answer it will arrive at is that the crew is not asking for the thing it wants because it wants the thing it wants more than the paper. And they will not be wrong."

He put the purpose into his own book and wrote the hour against it.

"I will read this into the record on Tuesday with the door shut, as I said I would, and I will read the whole of it and not a summary, and the record will show that a purpose was filed on the twenty-first and read on the twenty-second and that nothing in this room is minuted. And then on the twenty-eighth I will hold the sitting anybody objected to, and I would like you to have spent the twenty-second, tomorrow, working out what you are going to say to me behind a shut door at ten o'clock in the morning, because I am going to read your six pages into the record and then I am going to ask you one question about them and I am not going to be kind about it."

He looked up then.

"What question."

"I do not know," Kest said. "I have been in this office fifteen years and I have never read a purpose of six pages that asked for nothing in nine of them, and I do not know what to ask, and that is a new position for a reeve to be in and I do not intend to be in it for longer than one morning."
