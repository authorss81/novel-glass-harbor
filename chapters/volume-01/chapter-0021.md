# Chapter 21

*What May Be Measured*

Wednesday the 26th was the first morning of a month that had nothing in it, and Calder Marr found out what that sounded like by standing in his own yard and letting it sound.

The *Kittiwake* was on the hard above the slip with her bilge blocked in and no cradle under her. A bilge block is not a cradle, and every man who looked at that boat knew what she was doing up there, which was lying on a hard bottom in eight or nine feet of tide and taking the sun twice a day on the same planks until the garboard strake checked and there was oakum in her that Tobias would have to cut out and put back in March. There was no pump in the yard either. The pump was in pawn across the width of a reeve's counter with a sleeve packed in it and a cracked web in the housing, and it came back on the fourteenth of next month, and no man in the yard believed that a new housing could be got for it before then. The tin was empty. The bag was empty. The air pot was a third gone and had been a third gone on the fifteenth, and there was no margin in it whatever, which meant that a job needing air had to be paid for before the job started, and there was nothing in the yard with which to pay for it.

"Write it down," Nessa said. "All of it. In one line. Then look at the line."

He wrote it down. The one line ran off the page.

"Twenty-day defense to the fourteenth of next month," Nessa read over his shoulder. "Bond of twelve marks paid out of a tin that is empty. Pump, clamp stock, cordage, and the drawing chest held to a clerk's generous eighteen and all four of them working for somebody else. An eighth of any future raising, off the top, before wages. Four hundred yards off the outer marks. Shoal permit spent. Chandler's note eleven weeks into six and six marks lighter than it was a fortnight ago and not one week forgiven. Forty marks owed to Venn, first after food. Air a third gone with nothing in it. No boat of our own and no pump of our own."

"That is eleven things," Calder said.

"It is eleven things and every one of them was put there by a person who was doing their job." She put her finger on the fourth one. "The eighth. You did not bargain for the eighth. It came off the top of everything we ever raise from that ground for the rest of the year, and it will be in the room when we ask for a charter, and there is nothing you can do about it and no reason to write it down twice."

She went out to the slip and left him with the line, which was the arrangement and had been since the middle of September.

---

He went down to the reeve's office at half past eight with a slate under his arm and no list of grievances, which was new, and with his ears ringing through the half of the morning that the *Kittiwake*'s block of oakum was being hammered out on the hard above him.

The office was a barge with a stove in it and the gangway built for a shorter man. Kest was behind the table with the plan of a small room in front of him, and the young clerk with ink on his cuffs was copying something into a book the width of his forearm.

"I am not here to complain about anything," Calder said.

"Then you are the first man through that gangway in eleven years who has said that." Kest did not look up. "Sit down. What do you want."

"I want to know what I am allowed to do to a piece of ground I am not allowed to touch."

Kest put his pen down, which was the signal the whole harbor authority ran on.

"Say it as four lists," he said, "and I will answer all four, and I will write it if you want it in writing, and I will not be pleasant about the third one."

He wrote on the back of the plan with his own hand and turned it round. Four columns, and Calder read them standing up because the light in the barge was bad and he was not going to sit down for a man who had just offered to write.

The first column had four lines in it: *Look at it from land. Read what the harbor authority has written. Take a bearing with a compass from a station on harbor ground. Ask any person what they have seen and write down that they said it.*

The second column had one line: *Sound it, in a boat, in water inside the line, under a work booked on the board and paid for before it starts.*

The third column had one line, and the line was the officer's paper of the fourteenth, and Calder read it twice and put his finger on the first four words and stopped.

"Not the outer marks," he said. "The first line says not within four hundred yards of the outer marks. This one says the ground and the water over it, and you have written it wider than his paper."

"I have written it exactly as the paper says it and you have not read the whole of the paper." Kest turned the sheet a little so the lamp came across it. "Read the whole of it. I will wait."

Calder read the whole of it. It was the paper that had come in a boat's bow on the fourteenth with a man's coat on him and no pennant showing, and he knew most of it by heart, and the sentence he had not had in his head was the sixth, and the sixth said that the ground claimed, and the water over the ground claimed, was not to be approached, sounded, worked, or sounded from, by any means whatever, and that no instrument of the harbor authority on that ground was of effect.

"Not sounded from," Calder said.

"Not sounded from. That is not a phrase I wrote. That is a phrase the officer wrote, and I have had it on my counter for twelve days, and I have read it eleven times, and it is the reason I have not sent my own leads over that ground." Kest put both hands flat on the table. "You may look at his ground. You may read every book this office has written about his ground. You may take a bearing toward it from the mole head, the fish dock rail, or the coal wharf gable, and if you take a bearing I will not ask you what you took it to. You may not sound toward it. Not with a lead, not with a pole, not with a man in a boat with a string and a weight."

"So the third column is one line and it is the whole of the problem."

"The third column is one line because the officer wrote one line, and if I split it into six I would be writing a rule instead of a bar, and then in four months somebody would point at the six and say the office had softened it." He took the sheet back. "Write that down as I have said it, in the yard's own book, in your own hand, and it will bind you exactly as tightly as the paper binds me, and it will keep you out of a court where a naval officer is sitting in the corner with a notebook."

The fourth column had two lines. The first was *Take any object off harbor ground under a claim window, and the window closes when the courier takes it.* The second was *Do not take anything off the mole's toe without telling me first, because I have to write it in my book whether you do it or not.*

"That one is not the officer's," Calder said.

"That one is mine, and I have put it there because you found a piece of glass in my toe in the dark on the eleventh and told me about it the same night, and because in this harbor I would rather be told late than not at all." Kest squared the four columns. "That is the whole of the law as it bears on you this month, and it is more than you had, and I have given you no gift in it. Do you want the gift part, or do you want the book?"

"The book," Calder said. "Which book."

---

"The shoal book," Kest said. "That is what I was going to bring out if you had said anything else. It is not a secret and it is not a Registry matter and it does not go to Veyr. It is the harbor authority's own record of the ground it works, and the authority has sounded the inner and outer shoals every spring since before I came into this office, because a shoal that is not sounded is a shoal somebody drowns on."

He had the clerk bring it up from under the counter, and it came up in two parts because it had been bound in boards twice and both times in a hurry. The first part was a hand forty years old. The second was the current hand.

Calder turned to the spring entries and found what he had hoped for and was afraid of in the same page.

The office had a method and had kept to it. Once a year, in the first week of the month, a boat went out with a lead on a wire and a man at the oars and a man with a book, and ran the shoal on a stated course — the same course, the same spring, the same man for years, so that the figures could be compared with each other — and set down a figure at stated intervals of distance along the wire, with the hour against it and the man who took it. The figures ran in a single column, and every fourth line had a letter against it, and the letters were the seams and the marks the authority had put on the mole to say so.

It was better than Calder had expected and worse for his purpose. It was a survey and it was a survey of the *whole* of the shoal, not of one part of it, and it went out past the outer marks to where the officer's paper begins, because a spring sounding is done under the harbor charter and had been done every year without so much as a letter, and it had been done in the years the *Vigil* was in commission and had not been missed.

"What is your bearing?" Kest said.

"West-southwest, off the mole head, three bearings inside four degrees on the twelfth, taken by three men with the hour and the man against each."

"Four degrees is loose."

"Three and a half for Wren and four for Rook and it is the best the coast gives in a slant tide." Calder turned the page. "Then the shoal is sounded on a stated course, at stated intervals, with a stated man, once a year, and the same course every year, and every figure in it is a figure a man can check by going out and putting a lead over the same wire on the same line."

"Yes."

"And it goes past the outer marks."

"It goes past the outer marks because the mole's face is shoal water and there are people who need to know where it is and the officer's paper arrived on the fourteenth of last month." Kest said it without any weight at all. "Which brings me to the thing you have been circling since you sat down, so I will say it plainly and you can stop fishing for it."

He turned the book round himself and put his finger on a line near the foot of the page.

There was a gap.

It was not a blank space. It was a *dated* gap. The last figure before it stood at an hour on a stated day, and the figure after it stood at an hour four days later, and between the two, in the same neat column, in the clerk's hand of the time, there was one line that was not a figure.

*Sounding of the outer ground discontinued. Officer of the cutter on the pile, paper of the 14th. Instruments of this office over that ground stated of no effect.*

"There," Kest said. "That is why nobody has measured it. Not because it is deep and not because it is dangerous and not because the money is short, although all three of those are true. Because a man in a boat with a pennant on her came into this office and said in writing that my leads were of no effect, and I stopped running them, and then four days later I sent the boat out one more time because I am not a man who can stop hearing a number."

"You sent the boat out four days later."

"I sent the boat out four days later, and the boat ran the course, and the lead came up on one station with a bottom two fathoms shoaler than it should be, and the book has that figure in it, and the line after it says the sounding was discontinued on the officer's further request." He put his finger on the second line and left it there. "That is a sentence I wrote in my own hand in the front of my own office, and I have thought about it every day since, and I have never been able to make it say anything else."

"Two fathoms shoaler," Calder said. "Where on the course?"

"That is what you are going to have to do without." Kest took the book back and shut it. "I will let you read the book, because the book is the harbor authority's record of the harbor authority's own ground and there is no office in this harbor that can close it to a man who asks in a room. I will not let you copy it. Not one figure out of it goes into your book in anybody's hand except your own, and the figure you want is the one figure in eleven hundred that I have told you I will not hand you, and you may have it out of the book with your own eyes on a Thursday morning, and I will stand in this office while you do it and I will not be told when you have finished."

"Why Thursday."

"Because on Thursday the clerk is in and the stove is lit and I would rather it was done properly." Kest's mouth did not move. "And because you have a hearing in ten days and I have read a good many files this year, and a man who takes a figure out of a book and writes it in his own book on the day he needs it has a very short memory, and I would rather your memory were two days old and written down at the time."

"Two fathoms shoaler than it should be," Calder said, "is a hump."

"It is a figure in a book. What it is, is yours to argue and not mine to name."

---

He did not go out to the mole head, because Kest had told him on the eighteenth that he was not to be on the mole, and a man who has been told not to be on a piece of ground does not go and stand on it on the nineteenth day and enjoy himself.

So he took his bearings from the two places that were not the mole: the fish dock rail and the coal wharf gable, with a prismatic compass off the harbor's own locker that the clerk let him carry as far as the gate and no further, and he had Bevin Rook with him carrying the slate and saying every number back at him twice whether or not it matched what Calder thought he had read.

The fish dock rail was useless and he knew it before he had the compass out, because iron in a rail takes a compass somewhere else entirely, and the coal wharf gable stood ninety feet off a stack of pig iron and a coal tip.

"That is not a bearing," Bevin said. "That is a bearing with pig iron in it."

"That is a bearing with pig iron in it, and I would rather have it in writing that I took it anyway than not have it." Calder wrote it down with the pig iron in it. "Which is the third time this month that the honest number has been worse than the lie, and I am beginning to think that is a description of this entire undertaking."

Then the tide bell went at the top of the make, out in the road where it had always hung.

It went three strokes, and the strokes arrived, and under them, a third of a second late and much too even to be anything, the flat note came up out of the bottom of the harbor and said the three marks and the flick.

Calder's head began to turn.

"Nine-forty," Bevin Rook said. "Top of the make, southerly light air, water rising two inches on the mole face, and you have called it a bell twice now. I have written it down. You have not got to see it first."

"Thank you."

"I did not want to see it first because I did not want to have to argue with you about it afterwards." Bevin put the pencil behind his ear. "And because on the twelfth you said a man who listens very hard for a thing out there is going to hear his own, and I have been thinking about that for a fortnight, and I would rather be the one holding the pencil when you say it out loud."

Calder stood on the coal wharf with a prismatic compass in his left hand and a bell going on in both his ears and a boy of twenty-two holding a slate, and he had a moment of being entirely and completely tired, which was new, and which he understood to be what it felt like to be forty instead of thirty-two.

"Write it as a knock and not as a voice," he said. "Not because it is not a voice. Because I have not got an instrument on this coast that can tell me the difference, and a man who writes down what he would like to hear is a man who has stopped being a witness and become a man with a story."

Bevin wrote it. Under the hour, the state of the water, the bell's own strokes, and then a line that read: *Knock, delayed, in the bottom off the mole. No source. Not written as a voice.*

Then he read it back, and Calder did not change a word of it, and they walked up the road to the yard with the compass in a bag and the slate under the boy's arm and the tide making behind them.

At the bench Calder took the slate and wrote the plan out in full, because a plan is a thing you can be held to and an opinion is not. He read it aloud to the yard at four o'clock with the stove going and the door open, and Nessa wrote it in as he said it.

*Three stations on harbor ground, none of them the mole: the fish dock rail, the coal wharf gable, and the third to be agreed with the reeve and put in his own hand.*

*Three bearings, each taken by the man who took it, each with the hour and the state of the air, and no bearing spoken in this yard by a man who did not take it.*

*One figure out of the harbor authority's own shoal book, read with the office's clerk in the room, written out in the crew's own hand at the time, not copied, and not to be used to found any claim.*

*Nothing sounded toward the officer's ground. Nothing approached. Nothing sent to the fifth seam or the third seam or any seam of the mole's toe without the reeve's written permission first, and the tide to be named on the paper.*

*Nothing said out loud about what the ground is for until there is a man on this coast who can be asked.*

He read the last line twice himself before he let Nessa write it, and Bevin read the whole of it back from the book, and the number at the ladder was not in it because the ladder was a boat that was lying on the hard at the bottom of the slip with her bilge blocked in and no cradle under her.

Nessa countersigned. Calder signed left-handed. And out in the road, a quarter of a mile off the mole on the bearing of the pale run, the *Vigil*'s tender lay with her oars shipped and her floats run out, holding the near end of a line that went away into the west, and she had not moved in a fortnight, and nobody in Morrow Quay had seen a boat hold a station so patiently or so uselessly.
