# Chapter 22

*The Space That Was Never Emptied*

Thursday the 27th began with a queue at the hearing room door at a quarter to nine, which was not unusual, and with Ors Kest standing in his own doorway reading a sheet of paper he had written himself and did not look up for anybody.

"Four things," he said, when Calder got to the front of it. "You may not read this office's old wreck files in this room. You may read them in the barge, one at a time, in daylight, with the clerk present, and the clerk will note that you asked. You may not copy them. You may write out such figures as you need in your own hand, in your own book, at the table, and you may not take the file out of the room and you may not take a sheet out of it. And before you are permitted to ask, you must file with this office a written statement of the purpose for which you ask, and I will read it into the record in the hearing room, out loud, with the door shut."

"You have not asked what I want the file for."

"I know exactly what you want the file for and that is why I have written the conditions first, before you have told me, so that you cannot do the thing men do, which is to explain themselves afterwards." He turned the sheet over. "There is one further thing, and it is the whole of why this file is not the file you want. The harbor authority's file on the *Aster Gannet* is a harbor file. It is thirteen years old and it is a wreck entry and it records what came ashore and when and who was standing in the room. It is not a salvage report, because my father signed a salvage report and that is a different paper in a different book. It is not a seizure report, because that is the Navy's and it is in Veyr. It is not a custody log, because that is the Registry's and it is in their building. It is a harbor document, and there are four offices in this business and only one of them writes a thing like this, and if you can get your hands on it you must be able to say which one it is at every moment you are holding it."

"That was a speech."

"It was a warning with three sentences in it." Kest put the sheet down. "Purpose. In writing. And I will tell you now what will happen when you hand it in, so that it is not a surprise you can be angry at later. If you file a purpose about ground, I read it into the hearing record, and the record is the harbor authority's, and what is in it is what a reeve of this harbor has said out loud in a room with a clerk writing it down. That is a different animal from a man talking in his own yard. And after the reading, any man in Morrow Quay may come into this room and read what you said and take it apart in front of you. In eleven days I am going to hold a hearing or I am going to let the defense fall, and I do not yet know which, and what you file goes in the file either way."

"File it," Calder said. "That is what I came to do. I have brought it."

He had brought it, and he had brought it written out fair in his own hand and left-handed on the back of a slate, because a slate is what the yard wrote on and a slate can be produced in a room without an argument about who wrote it.

Kest read it standing up, in the voice he used for a hearing, twice, and then read it into the record with the clerk's pen going.

*Purpose of the request. To show that the ground described in the Free Keel yard's own bearing sheet, and the ground described in the harbor authority's own shoal book, lie on a single line running west-southwest, and that on that line the bottom comes up at stated intervals and nowhere else, and that no measurement of that ground has been made by this office since the officer of the cutter on the pile stated on the 14th of this month that the instruments of this office over that ground were of no effect. The Free Keel yard asserts that the ground is placed and not scattered. It has no measurement of that assertion. It states the purpose of asking for the file of thirteen years ago because that file may show whether any person has ever measured a carrying space on that vessel or has ever had cause to.*

Kest read the last line twice before he looked up.

"Placement," he said. "You have written the word *placed* on the record of this office eleven days before a hearing, and you have written *scattered* beside it, and you have said in the same sentence that you have no measurement of it."

"Yes."

"Either you believe it and you have no proof, or you have no belief and you want the record to say a thing. Which."

"Both," Calder said. "I believe the ground is placed. I cannot show it. And if somebody in this room says that a man who asserts what he cannot show is a fool, I will say yes, and I would rather have said it in here than at the hearing with a clerk writing it down for me."

Something went across Kest's face that was not a smile and was not softness either.

"Entered," he said, to the clerk. "Purpose filed, read into the record, no measurement claimed. Take it to the barge."

---

The *Aster Gannet*'s wreck entry was nine leaves of a harbor form and had been kept in a tin box in the barge's stern locker since the year it was written, and it came out with the smell of a box that had been shut a long time.

The clerk put it on the table, wrote the date and the hour and the words *one file, read in room, not copied*, and turned it round, and stood back, which was more than a boy with ink on his cuffs is paid for.

Calder read it. Kest stood at his own table with his hands behind him and let a man read a file in his presence without once looking at it, and Mara Venn stood by the stove in her Registry coat with her book closed under her arm.

The entry was four hands, which was normal, and it began the way they all begin: the vessel, the port of registry, the date she came in.

She had come in in pieces over four days.

That was the first thing that put a hand on Calder's arm, and he put it there himself without meaning to. She had not been brought in whole. She had come in over four days on a rising tide, in four parts, and the entry recorded the order: the bow section, the after body, the engine space and its plate, and then, on the fourth day, a quantity of small wreckage that came in on the ebb and was stopped by the mole's third seam. Somebody in the office had written that sentence in a hurry and it was the only sentence in nine leaves with any colour in it.

Then the survey's figures, entered as *as surveyed*, the entry said, which was the entry being scrupulous.

And then, on the fourth leaf, against the list of standing spaces, in the same hand, the line Calder had come for.

*Number four, port side aft. Grating in place. Not cleared. Contents not removed.*

He read it twice. He put his finger under it and did not say anything for a while, and Mara Venn came across the room and read it over his shoulder from about a foot away, which she was entitled to do and which she did, and he did not stop her.

"That is the phrase," she said. "Not cleared. Contents not removed. Do you hear what that is?"

"It is a compartment that was not emptied."

"It is a compartment whose emptying was recorded as somebody else's job." Her voice was flat and entirely without drama, which was how she delivered things she wanted a man to keep. "A harbor reeve does not write *not cleared* about his own ground. He writes *cleared* or he writes nothing. He writes *not cleared* when the work of clearing was done by another party and he is recording that it was not his, and he writes *contents not removed* when the contents went somewhere he did not follow. That is custody language, Mr Marr, in a harbor's hand, on a harbor's form, and it is the fourth document and it is the first one any of us has read that says an office was kept out of a room."

"And it does not tell us what was in there."

"It does not tell us what was in there, it does not tell us who took it out, and it does not tell us that there was anything in there at all. It tells us that nobody in this harbor ever opened that grating, and that somebody wrote down the fact that they did not open it, in advance, in a hand, and initialled it." She looked at Kest. "May I ask the reeve whether that is a practice of this office or a decision of one man on one day."

Kest had not moved.

"It is a decision of one man on one day," he said. "And I will tell you what I remember about it, and I will tell you as a man who was a clerk in this office in the year after, and not as anything to do with the hearing, and you may write none of it down. Your father did not go out to her. He was asked whether he wanted to go out to her, and he said that the harbor's business was the harbor's ground and that the vessel was under a company that had a cutter alongside her, and that a man who puts a boat alongside another man's wreck without being asked is a man who is told afterwards what he saw. That is what I remember. I was thirty-one and I had been in this office two years. I have thought about it every few years since, and I have never known whether he was right, and I have never known whether he was frightened, and both of those can be true of a man at the same time."

Calder looked up from the file.

"And the figure of the space," he said. "Eight foot four by six foot nine clear and eleven foot two under the grating. Did this office ever measure it as found?"

"No," Kest said. "The entry says *as surveyed*, and the survey was made with the grating down, and this office did not go out to her. There is no figure in that file of a space measured after the wreck came in, and there is none in any other file in this harbor, and I have looked, because you have not asked me to and I would like to know it too."

"You have looked."

"I looked on the twenty-second, and I would have said nothing, and I am telling you because a man who has read a file and has not said so is a man with a private file, and this office does not keep private files." He put one careful hand on the box. "That is the whole of the answer to the question you actually came in here with, and it is a bad answer, and it is the correct one. Nobody measured the space because the space was never emptied on any record in this harbor, and a compartment that is never emptied is a compartment that is never measured, and a vessel that is a cutter's business for four days on end is a vessel whose compartments are not a harbor's business at all. Your brother's compartment could have been any size on earth and this office would have carried on writing *as surveyed* until the tin rotted."

The stove ticked. The clerk's pen had stopped and Calder noticed that the boy had stopped writing some time ago and was standing with his hand on the box, waiting to know whether there was more.

"Write the figures in your own book," Kest said, "and write the line about the grating, and write the word *custody* nowhere, because I did not say it and if you write it you will be quoting a Registry woman's mouth from a room she was not in. And when you are done you will hand me the file and I will lock the tin, and there will be a note in the box that a file was read out on the twenty-seventh of this month by a Free Keel yard, and that note is the only thing anybody in this harbor will be able to check in a year."

---

Calder wrote the figures in the crew's book at the bench that afternoon with Bevin reading them back twice out loud, because that is what they did with numbers, and the boy's voice on the figures made them something that had been read out of a paper in a room and not something a man had made up in a shed.

Then he wrote the line about the grating, and then he wrote under it, in the column where a man puts the reason a thing is not yet a fact:

*Fourth document, harbor authority, own hand, read in the reeve's office, the twenty-seventh, file not copied, figures written fair by Marr, read back by Rook. What it shows: the standing space four was never cleared and its contents were not removed, and no office in this harbor has a measurement of it as found. What it does not show: what was in the space, who removed anything, or that anything was in the space at all. It explains a silence. It does not fill the silence.*

"It explains a silence," Nessa said, reading over his shoulder. "That is a good line and it is a hard one to earn, and I want to know whether you are going to be able to keep saying it out loud for the next four months, because a man who explains a silence today will be telling everybody in the fish market that the space was full by Friday."

"I will say it."

"Say it in those words and not in others. And sign under it." She squared the book. "And Calder."

"Aye."

"Ten days ago you would have filed a purpose that said you had found a line of wrecks. Today you filed one that said you have no measurement of anything you said." She capped the pen. "That is the whole of what I have been trying to get you to do since the sixteenth and I am not going to pretend it was my idea."

It was Tobias who found the thing at four o'clock, and he found it by being cross about a chisel.

The clamp stock was in pawn and the bench was bare and he had gone along the row of benches to the far end to get a drawer he did not need out of the way, and he came back with the rear bench's tool board in his hands and set it down hard enough to make the inkpot jump.

"Somebody has had my old offset chisel," he said. "The one I keep for the inside of a stem. It is not in this yard. It has not been in this yard since Michaelmas, and I know it is not, because I looked for it in September and it was gone then, and I did not say anything about it in September because I was not going to start a thing with a woman in a Registry coat over a chisel."

"Which girl of nineteen," Calder said.

Tobias put the tool board down.

"The Registry one," he said. "Your conservator. The first week she was here I watched her turn a plate in a chair with a flat steel under the edge of it to lift the fouling off a scar without touching the scar, and I said to myself, there is a woman who knows what an edge is worth, and she will be at that bench every time she comes into this yard for as long as there is a bench. And now I cannot find mine, and I have not said a word about it for a fortnight, and I am saying it now because you have just come back from a room where a man told you that nobody has ever measured a space, and I would like there to be one honest thing in this yard that is not a file."

He went out. Calder sat with the tool board in front of him and looked at the row of slots, and there was a gap where a chisel that had belonged to a man who had taught him to dress a seam ought to have been.

The gap had been in the board in September too, when Tobias had looked for it and said nothing. It had been in the board on the fourteenth, when the plate had been on that bench with a conservator working over it. It had been in the board on the twentieth, and on the twenty-fourth, and on the day before. A man who wanted to make something of it could make almost anything of it, and there were two people in this harbor he could make it out of, and one of them owed him forty marks and one of them was owed by an office in Veyr.

He picked up the pencil.

*27th. Tool board, rear bench. Offset chisel, inside of a stem, Wren's, missing since about Michaelmas, reported by Wren this day. I do not know what to do with this. I am not going to ask her about it, because a man who begins asking a witness where her steel is has stopped believing the rest of her, and because a chisel left in a working yard is a chisel somebody needed and did not think of at the time. Not chased.*

And then he turned the page and put the date at the top of a fresh one and wrote the purpose he had filed in the hearing room, in full, in the yard's own book, so that any man who came into this shed could read exactly what the Free Keel yard had told the harbor authority and exactly when, and could not later be told it had said something else.

Bevin read it back. Calder signed left-handed. Nessa countersigned.
