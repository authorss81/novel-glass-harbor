# Chapter 37

*What the Yard Cannot Pay*

The shackle came off on the Sunday afternoon at twenty past four, and Elias Voke paid fourteen marks in the road at half past five, in coin and in small silver, and read the line before he put it in Calder's hand.

Then he said, "Fourteen to the chandler."

"Four," Calder said. "Four to the air, two to rope and the day's food, two to the harbor's share, and four to the note. The two I set against that note on Saturday go on it tonight along with those, which is six on the note, and it is the first money on it since the twenty-fifth of last month. And there are two of the fourteen still in the bag and I have not found a thing to spend them on."

"Your note is eleven weeks into six and you have told me so twice since Monday and you have not had a week off it since September, and you have just put six marks on it in one night, which is not what I expected to see done, and I am a man who pays this authority's moorage every winter." Elias Voke did not make an argument out of it. "I noticed, that is all. A man who is seen is a man who can be asked to do a thing later, and I may want a thing later, and I would rather have told you now that I saw a yard with a broken hand and an empty tin than be the one who says it in a room on the fourteenth."

"You are on the list for the fourteenth," Calder said, "if you want to be."

"I have been nineteen winters on the authority's books," said Elias Voke, "and I have never once been on a list, and if I am going to be on one I would like it to be for the dues and not for the *Anna Ray's* mooring, and you may put that in your book in those words and I will read it when you have written it."

He went down the quay. Calder wrote it in the book, and Bevin Rook read it back, and the book said five marks in the bag, which was five again, the same five as on the Saturday before two of them went on the note with four of Voke's, and the first time since the second of this month that a mark had come into the yard which was not already promised to the air, the rope or the chandler, and five is not a pot.

---

The thing about Bevin Rook's hand was not the hand.

The hand was a fortnight along and the bonesetter had looked at it on the Wednesday of last week and said the same four words she had said on the thirtieth of October, which were *hold, and leave it*, and Alden Marr had re-bound it on the Wednesday night before last with the linen doubled at the heel of the palm so that it could not close, and would not do it again because he had done it twice and had said both times that a man who re-does a binding three times is a man who cannot admit the first one was good enough.

The hand was going to be a boat hand again in June. That was the sentence Alden had said in October and the sentence the bonesetter said now, and nobody in the yard argued with either of them, and nobody in the yard believed it either, and both of those things were true in the same week.

What was happening in the yard was not the hand. It was the fact that a boy of twenty-two who had carried a book, a board and a pencil for four weeks had become the load-bearing part of the yard's record, and had not been paid a mark of it, and nobody had said anything, and on the Sunday night, after Elias Voke had gone down the quay, Bevin Rook said it himself.

"You have not paid me," he said.

Nessa Pike put down the pencil.

"No," Calder said.

"Four weeks. The fourteenth of October to tonight. That is a book, and a board, and every entry read back twice, and the terms for the seventh written out and signed, and three sheets of three points, and a fifth-seam sheet, and a board copy of the notice of the thirty-first copied fair in my right hand in a day, and the market wall paper moved and re-nailed with the date and the hour on the back of every sheet, and the pay owed to me from the twenty-ninth is nothing at all because I have not earned any of it, because I cannot hold a line and I cannot hold a compass and I cannot take a tender's cleat." He put his right hand flat on the bench. "I want it said in the room on Monday. Not as a term. As a thing I am owed, with the hours in it, because the terms were sealed on the fourth and I did not argue for it and I do not think I was wrong, and the sealing of the terms is not the same as a boy not being owed anything."

"You were not wrong," Calder said. "And I will say it, and I will say it with the hours in it, and I would like to know first what you think it is worth."

"Four a week," Bevin Rook said. "That is the going rate for a boy on a town's books and it is what my mother is going to ask me about on Monday and it is what I have been telling her. Four weeks is sixteen marks. And I want you to tell me, in front of her, that the yard has it and cannot pay it, and not that the yard has not got it, because those are two different sentences and she will hear the difference and I am not going to be the reason she hears the wrong one."

It was the first time in four weeks that the boy had asked for money.

"I have five in the bag," Calder said, "and two of that is the air for the next tide, and two of it is Elias Voke's money and it is not spent, and one of it is what is left of a Saturday in the middle of October. The chandler has had six marks off that note since Saturday morning and will have the rest of it when there is any, and the note is still written up in his book as eleven weeks into six because I have not asked him to write it down again and neither has he, and the pump is in a clerk's hands until tomorrow, and the iron on the stack is worth twenty marks at a fair sale and a third of that is entered to Sella Brigg and a kerf nobody can account for takes it to scrap. So the yard has not got it. The yard has got a claim on it. And I would rather tell your mother that on Monday in a hearing room, as she asked me to, than tell her a number on a Sunday and take it back on the Thursday."

"Then tell her the second one," Bevin said, "and I will stand next to you when you do and I will not say anything, and I have thought about it and that is what I want."

"Why."

"Because a boy who says you have got it when you have not got it is a boy who will be asked to say it again in a room, and I have watched a man say *it lies in a row* three times in a week and I do not want to be that at twenty-three." He took the pencil behind his ear with his right hand, which was still the only way he could do it. "And because I have worked out what I would be if I was owed nothing, and I do not want to be owed nothing. In a yard like this a man with no claim on it is a man who can be sent away without a conversation. So I would like the claim, and I would like it in the book with the hours against it, and I would like the book to be read out on Monday by you and not by me, because if I read it out then it is a boy asking."

Nessa Pike wrote it in.

*13th, Sunday. Rook claims wages for the reading of the book and the board and the writing of the entries, from the 14th of October, at four marks the week, four weeks, sixteen marks, and the market wall paper, and the clerk's reason for the sealing of the terms is given and stands. Sixteen marks is claimed and is not paid. The claim is a claim and not a gift, and the yard's ability to pay it is nil, and the yard does not ask for the claim to be reduced, and the boy's own words are that he would rather have the claim than the money, and the twenty-four marks of wages for the hand are a separate claim and stand beside it.*

"That is the whole of what I have," Nessa said. "Read it back, and then we will do the other half of it, and the other half is mine."

---

The other half took an hour and it is the reason any of this is written down.

"You are not going back in the water before the tenth of December," Nessa said. "That is my measurement and not the bonesetter's, and I have written it in twice. On the tenth of December, if there is anything inside the line with a bottom in it, you go, and the tender is Marr, and the tender's word is *Enough* and you do not argue with it, and I will be on the fall because I am the only other person in this yard who can be.

"And I want to know what you are going to be doing for the four weeks between tonight and the tenth of December, because I put you on the board and the book when your hand went and I did it in front of three people and I said it was for your own good and that I would not be arguing about it in April. And I have been watching you for four weeks and I am going to say the thing I said in October, which is that a man who spends five weeks holding a pencil is a man whose head has gone soft and whose ears have gone soft, and that in June you will want to go down too fast, and that I am not going to be the woman who made that prediction and then profited by it for four weeks."

Bevin Rook did not say anything for a while.

"I have been going soft," he said. "I know I have. I read the board four times this week for nothing and I have caught myself composing the entry before you have said the number, and yesterday I said *nine and ten* at the ladder in my own head before anybody had called a number at all, and that is not reading, that is guessing, and I would not have caught it if I had not heard myself do it."

"Then here is the arrangement and it is not a kindness," Nessa said, "and I want it read back and I want it in the book in these words.

"From tonight the reading of this book is a trade and not a job. It is a trade because it is the only thing in this harbor that three men and a woman cannot do between them, because there are four of us and one of us can only write with one hand and the other three cannot hear each other, and that is a fact about ears and not about character. It is not a job because nobody is being paid for it, and I am not going to stand in this yard in four months and tell a man with a sling that the book is his and that he ought to expect money for it, because he will expect money for it and he will not get it, and a boy who has been promised a thing and not given it is a boy who has been done to, and this yard has been done to enough."

"Write it," Calder said.

"And the four marks a week goes on the claim, and does not come off the twenty marks, and is not the same four marks, and the boy will be owed twenty-four marks at the end of it, of which four a week is the part for the reading and twenty is the part for the hand." She capped the pen. "And on the tenth of December the reading stops and the wages start, and if the ten of December comes and there is no work with a bottom in it, then the reading stops anyway, and the boy comes into a yard with no reading and no work, and that is what the arrangement is, and I would rather it stopped than that it went on for a month with nobody thinking about it. Sixteen marks for the reading and twenty-four for the hand is forty marks this yard owes a boy of twenty-two, and there is not one mark of it in the tin, and the tin is empty, and the two of those facts are going in the book in the same sentence."

---

Sella Brigg came at eight and stood in the yard door with her hood down and did not come in, as she had not come in since the twenty-ninth of October.

"My crew stands no cover for this yard this winter," she said. "I said so on the twenty-ninth of October and I have not changed it and I am not here to change it."

"You are here about the boy," Nessa said.

"I am here about the boy, and about the strop, and I am going to say the strop first because it is on paper and the boy is not on paper." Sella put her slate on the bench, in her own hand, with the hour on it, which is what she did. "The *Low Lantern's* cover is not offered to this yard again this winter. That is my statement on my own slate and the crew knew it before you did. And the strop of the fifteenth of September holds a third of the sale of the iron, and the iron is on your stack with a kerf in it, and the iron is not sold, and I am not pressing you and I am not going to.

"And I am going to say a third thing and I have thought about it for two days and I would have said it in a market if I had not said it here. The last thing I said in this yard was on the twenty-ninth of October, and it was that a boy choosing nine with the hand already caught is worth more to this yard than the nine marks, and I asked for it to be written in the book before it became a story. It is in the book. It was read out. And a man from a cutter's boat read it in a hearing room on the eighth of this month, out of your own book, in front of a clerk, and wrote in his own book that a Free Keel yard's record contains a statement by a member of the *Low Lantern's* crew's employer, and he read it as a crew that buys loyalty with wages, and he read it wrong, and he read it wrong because of the two hours I spent on a slip in the first week of November telling you about a stove."

Nobody said anything.

"I am not withdrawing it," Sella Brigg said. "It is in the book and it is true and I would say it again. But I would like it noticed that a true thing in a working book is a thing a man with a notebook can take out and bend, and that the bending is not his fault and is not mine and is not the boy's, and that the only way a working book stops being bent is if the thing in it is also in somebody else's record with a seal on it. And I am not going to ask you for my third of the iron in a room with a naval officer in it. Ask me in February, when there is no officer in it, and I will take it."

Then she looked at the sling.

"Twenty-four marks," she said. "Six weeks at four. I have read the book and that is right and the boy is owed it and I am telling you that I have got eleven marks in the *Low Lantern's* box and I am not going to lend it to you, and I am telling you that I have got it so that you cannot ask me, because if I lent it to you then it would be in a book as a favor from a crew that has already told you it will not stand your rescue cover this winter, and a man in a cutter's coat would read that favor in a room and the boy would be a boy who was helped."

"I was not going to ask," Calder said.

"I know you were not. I said it out loud so that there is a line in your book with a date on it saying I was not going to ask, and if there is not one, then in three years somebody will say I offered, and I did not offer, and I will not be a woman who offered."

---

Tobias Wren came in last, at nine, and stood at the end of the bench with his hat in his hand, and he did not sit, and he said the thing he had come to say and it was about the boat.

"You will want to move the *Kittiwake* to the inner slip when the pump comes back tomorrow, because she is lying on a hard with no cradle and the bilge blocks are oakum and the oakum is going, and you have three weeks before the gales and no timber and I have got two feet of sound oak baulk out of the mole's inner face which I took on the third of this month with a chisel and which is on the third to the eleventh and about which the notice says the face is closed to all persons on any work, and I took it anyway, because a man with a hand axe at nine in the morning is a man doing timber, and I have written it in the book and I would like the reeve to have it.

"And it is not enough for a cradle and it will not be enough for a cradle, and the yard does not move her to the slip tomorrow. The yard will leave her on the hard where she is, because if the yard moves her to the slip then the yard has a boat, and if the yard has a boat then the yard puts a boy with a hand that cannot close in the front of a boat on the strength of a pump that comes out of a tin in a clerk's hands, and that is how a boy who chose nine ends up choosing something on a Monday that he did not choose on the twenty-ninth." He put his hat on the bench. "I am sixty-one and I have been in this yard since 1961 and I have watched a yard make itself poorer by being careful once, and I would rather be a yard on a hard in March than a yard that put him in the water to look as though it had not lost him."

Nobody in the shed had said the word *April* that day, and Nessa Pike had said it four times in four weeks, and Tobias Wren had just said the whole of it without saying it once, and Calder understood that the old man had heard her say it and had come down to the shed at nine o'clock on a Sunday to say the other half of it out loud so that there would be two people in the yard who had said it and not one.
