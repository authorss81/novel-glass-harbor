# Chapter 48

*The Tenth of December*

He was awake before the light because his arm was doing the burn, which it had been doing for two days, and he lay in the dark at his father's house in a room he had slept in for eleven years and tried to make a noise he had made in a shed on the thirteenth of October without deciding to.

Three marks and a flick. That was in there. He had the shape of it and the length of it and the place in the middle of it where a boy's breath went, and he had the fact of it, which was that it was a call and not a word, and that a call means *I am here and where are you*, and that he had answered it in a fog on a quay when he was eleven years old and he had not missed once in as long as he could remember.

And then the end of it, where it used to lift, was not there.

He lay in the dark and went at it for a quarter of an hour the way you go at a thing with a cold chisel, four inches at a time, and it was not there, and it was not that he could not remember it. It was that there was nothing to remember. There was a call in him and the call stopped, and where the call stopped there used to be a thing that a boy did with the end of his voice, and it was not a word, and it was not a thing anybody ever wrote down, and it was his.

He got up at half past five and put the kettle on and did not light the stove.

---

Nessa Pike was at the bench at seven with the book open and Bevin Rook's reading of the previous day's entry written out in the flat hand and the hour against it, and she was doing what she had been doing since the twenty-fifth of October, which was reading the whole of the day's work back before anybody was allowed to say a word about it.

"Read it," she said.

He read it. Eleven minutes forty inside a limit of forty. Air seventy minutes unspent. Named tender Pike, named cutoff, independent line, seated weight, reason entered in the harbor authority's own book at eight o'clock with the clerk present and not edited. The anchor described in nine lines by a conservator and under them two lines in the same hand saying that the holder states that he does not know where the object was at the time of the scene and that the conservator has not tested the statement and will not be asked to. And then the account, four paragraphs, in his own left hand, and it was as good as he had ever written anything.

*He was low on a deck with a case high on his right shoulder and his hand under the near end of it, and there were four men on the case and he was the smallest.*

*The man with the paper was behind and above him and to the left, and he turned his head and had the underside of the paper and a mouth and no letters, because a paper held the wrong way up is a shape.*

*Numbers were read and the words could not be put in order. A phrase with one word in it was read twice and the word was empty, and the case went past the man's knees between the two readings and the man did not look down.*

*He counted the men on the case and the count was four and he was one of them.*

*A tag lay on the line at his feet with a number stamped into it. He had the first two figures and lost the third.*

*A hand was put on the collar at the back of it and it was not rough and not gentle, and he was moved, and he went looking at the man with the paper and not at the case, and the man turned the paper over.*

*A part of the scene would not resolve. It was described as a doubled edge with a hard rim. The witness stopped pushing against it and did not go into it.*

*No name is entered and none has been asked for. The crew does not know who the witness is.*

And then the two lines that were the price, which he had written himself in the shed at two in the morning and which were also Mara's two lines in a different order.

*The yard does not state that the witness was any person it has known. The yard states that the anchor was a piece of the stern board of the vessel the scene was on, and that this is a fact about a ship and not about a family, and that the crew has not been able to establish a name and has not asked for one.*

Nessa Pike took the book off him and put it flat and squared it.

"Two things and then we are going out," she said. "The first is that entry is the best piece of writing you have done and you did it in four months by being told no by four offices and one woman, and I am not going to stand here and praise you for it, because the praise is for the four offices and the woman and not for you, and you know that, and I have watched you not know it for four months and I am not going to let you keep it.

"The second is the other half of the sentence you just read me and you did not hear yourself say it, so I am going to say it and you are going to hear it.

"You have put a boy on a deck in a public record and you have not put his name in it. Not a mark and not a name and not a sound. A clerk in Veyr will read that page in four years and he will know that a boy of twenty stood on that deck with a case on his shoulder, and he will not be able to find out who the boy was, and in nine years somebody will come to this yard and ask, and we will not be able to tell them." She put her hand on the page. "That is what a public route is. It is the only way that anybody will ever know a boy was there, and it is also the only reason nobody will ever know which boy. And I want you to hear both halves of that in the same breath, because I have watched you for four months get a good thing and only look at the half of it that was comfortable."

Calder Marr stood in a shed with the stove cold behind him.

"That is what a public route is," he said.

"That is what a public route is," Nessa said, "and you chose it, and I am not going to ask you whether you chose it gladly, because you did not, and I can see it on your face, and I have been in this yard five weeks and I can see it on your face and so can he." She nodded at Bevin Rook, who had the book under his arm and had not said anything for a quarter of an hour. "And I am going to say the last of it and then we are going to the mole.

"On the third of November you wrote four paragraphs in this yard saying that you had thought about going out at two in the morning with a punt and a lamp and a line on a rim, and that you had not gone, and you gave three reasons, and the second of them was that a figure you take alone in the dark in a boat makes every other figure in the record a figure somebody has to take on trust from you. That is still the reason. And yesterday you did the opposite of that and you did it in daylight in eight and a half feet with a woman on your shoulder and a boy with a book and a reeve's permit and a reason in a municipal ledger at eight o'clock in the morning, and you gave that up on purpose, and this morning you have found out what it costs, and I want it in the book in your words and not in mine, and then I want the book shut."

"It is not in my words yet."

"Then it is not going in," Nessa Pike said, "and we are going to be on the mole at the top of the flood in an hour and forty minutes, and the tenth of December is the day you put in a paper on the fourteenth of November and I put in a book on the thirteenth, and there is a boy going in the water for the first time in six weeks and he is owed forty marks, and if you write nothing in this book today then you have not written anything in it for two days and I will not have that on a morning when I have to be on a fall."

---

They went down at half past nine on the tenth of December, at the top of the flood, on the south end beyond the eleventh seam, and the permit was Kest's own and the reason was entered in his book at eight o'clock and the limit was thirty minutes and the tender was Calder Marr and the man on the fall was Nessa Pike and Tobias Wren had the boat.

Bevin Rook went over at ten past ten in about eight feet with a light under his arm where he could not lose it, and Calder Marr had the cleat in his left hand and read the depth and the air out loud every ten minutes in the flat voice, and Nessa Pike had the fall and the board and the knowledge of exactly how long a boy with a hand that cannot close can stay in eight feet of a moving bottom, which was not as long as a man with a whole hand and which she had written in a book on the thirteenth of November with a date against it.

He came up at nineteen minutes with a fouled ring off the plate's lowest plate and the ring's pin in his right hand and his left arm held across his chest against his body, which is the way he had held it since the twenty-ninth of October and which nobody had ever taught him.

"Seventeen on the call," he said at the ladder. "Nineteen by mine."

"Seventeen goes in the book."

"Seventeen is not what I would have said if you had not called it."

"No," Calder Marr said, "it is not, and I have not heard you say a number I did not call since the twenty-ninth of October and you have not made me say one either, and if that has been no use to you then you are owed twenty-four marks and not one of them has been paid and I have not got it."

The boy's face did something and he got back onto the thwart.

Then he said, "Nessa. My hand."

Nessa Pike looked at the fall and then at the board and then at the water.

"Your hand is in the bottom of your pocket on a cradle in eight feet," she said, "and you put it there at about fourteen minutes, and I watched you do it and I did not say anything because I wanted to see whether you would take it out, and you have had your hand in that pocket for five minutes and you are about to go down for the ring's mate, and the mate is four feet away and a man cannot do that on one hand without putting the other one on the iron."

"I did not say I was going down."

"You did not say you were not going down either, and that is the answer." She had not raised her voice. "Enough."

It went into the boat in about nine seconds and Bevin Rook came up the ladder with his right hand holding the lead of the ring's mate and his face the color of the basin, and he was angry, and Nessa Pike did not apologize and did not soften it and did not look away from him.

"Twelve minutes was my number," she said, when he was on the thwart. "I gave you twelve and I gave you twelve and you were on the fall for nineteen, and the difference of seven is the difference between a man who is fit and a man who is not, and I have been the only person in this yard who has ever been allowed to say a number like that out loud, and I said it, and you do not get to be angry with me and be right about the air at the same time."

"I am angry," Bevin Rook said. "I am not saying I am right about the air."

"That is the correct answer and I taught you it in October and you have used it against me twice since and I would like a third." She capped the board. "And on Monday you are going to hear me say a thing about money and I am not going to ask you to make it easier, and you may be angry at me about that as well, and you will have earned it."

---

They put the fragment into the custody room on the Tuesday afternoon, the thirteenth, carried down by Bevin Rook in a padded box with Nessa Pike packing it herself and a boy carrying it with one hand, and the courier from the circuit office took it at the counter of the Morrow circuit office in the west of the city and wrote on the back of the sheet a number in a small square hand, and it was the third number that had been written in that hand in that harbor in three months and none of the three digits were spoken aloud in a hearing room.

The entry read: one piece of transparent mineral, worked, in two portions keyed at a join, recovered on the eighth of December from a plate of iron in nine feet of water on harbor ground inside the harbor line, at a depth stated, and entered as *a fragment of undetermined origin, not compared by this office with any other item*, and the seal line under it was the same seal line that had been on the paper of the eleventh of November and on the order of the fourteenth of October that took the other two out of this yard, and the seal line read *Magister Ilya Serr, senior Registry examiner*, and there was nothing on that page and no other page in the building that said why a transparent mineral of undetermined origin recovered on harbor ground is presumed Crown material, and nobody in that building had ever written it down.

The claim was entered against it in the same hand and Calder Marr read it before it was signed, and it said that the claimant is the Free Keel yard of Morrow Quay as a municipal party holding a one-season provisional working charter of the twenty-eighth of November, and that the claim is entered for the purpose of a record and not for the purpose of possession, and that the claimant is not entered as a custodian of the item, is kept out of any handling of it by the second clause of a notice of the thirty-first of October, and does not ask for any handling of it and will not ask.

"That last line is the whole of the value of that claim," Kest said, reading it upside down in the hearing room on Monday the twentieth, "and I want the room to understand what it is. A claim that asks for nothing is not worth anything and is the only kind of claim that can be entered without a countersignature, and the yard has entered it so that there is a line in a Registry book with a name on it, and there is now a third line in that book with a name on it instead of a seal alone, and a man in Veyr who reads the file will read three items and one municipal claim and no reason for any of them, and that has been true about two of them since the fourteenth of October and it is now true about three."

"Can the yard withdraw the claim," the officer asked.

"It cannot," Kest said, "and that is the other half of the value of it. A party that has entered a claim cannot withdraw it without a request, and a request goes up, and a request is a document with a date on it, and the yard is not going to make one, and I would like the vessel to hear me say that I have advised against it and the vessel may enter that I advised against it."

---

Then Nessa Pike read out the money, in the shed, on the Tuesday, with the book open and the door shut, and she made Bevin Rook read it back and she made him read it twice, and it is in the yard's book in her hand and in his.

*Tenth of December. The reading of this book is a trade and it stops on this date. Wages begin at this date at four marks the week and are not paid. The claim of sixteen marks for the reading from the fourteenth of October and the claim of twenty-four marks for the hand from the twenty-ninth stand and are not reduced and the whole of them is forty marks, of which this yard has none, and the first four weeks fall in January and February and the yard has entered what it has and cannot pay it and does not ask that it be reduced.*

Bevin Rook read it back twice with his right hand on the page and his left arm across his chest, and then he said the thing he had been not saying since the twenty-ninth of October and had not said once, and it was not the thing anybody expected.

"Nessa, I have read that entry eleven times since the fourth of November and I have not once said *when*, and I have been waiting to be asked, and nobody has asked me and nobody was going to, and that has been the arrangement for six weeks and I have let it stand because the arrangement was easier on me than the question. So I am asking now. Four a week, and I have got a hand that closes in June and a boat after that if there is one, and I am twenty-two. What I want to know is whether the four is being paid to me or whether the claim is."

"The claim is being paid to you," Nessa Pike said. "There is no four a week. There is a claim of forty marks and a stoppage on a date and no money, and I have been calling it wages since Monday because wages is a word that makes a man feel he is owed something on a Friday, and it is not a word I should have used, and I am taking it out of the book on Wednesday and putting *claim* in its place, and I am telling you that I am taking it out because you asked me and not because anybody in this yard told me to."

"Take it out on Wednesday," Bevin Rook said, "and read it to me first, and put it in with your name on it and not with Calder's, because it is your sentence and I have been in this yard five weeks and I know the difference between a thing a man is told and a thing a woman decides."

He put his right hand flat on the page.

"Forty marks," he said, "and the first of March is ten weeks off, and the chandler's note is at eleven weeks into six in a book I have read in four different shops this month, and none of the shops writes a new figure, and the iron on the stack is worth twenty at a fair sale and a third of that is another crew's and they have asked for February. So I know the sum and I have known it since the fourteenth of November and I did not say it in a room and I am not going to say it in a room, and I am going to be a boat hand in June if this yard has a boat in June, and the twenty-four marks for the hand is the only one I have ever been angry about, and I am not going to be angry about it in a hearing room, because a boy who is angry about it in a hearing room is a boy who is owed nothing and I have worked out that I would rather be owed forty than be owed nothing and have to be told so by a man in a coat."
