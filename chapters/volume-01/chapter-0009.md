# Chapter 9

*The Three-Second Gap*

The copying room of the Morrow circuit office was a long cold box with a counter down the middle of it and four stools, and the clerk who worked in it had the stillness of a person paid by the page who has learned to be dull on purpose.

Mara Venn was at the counter in her Registry coat with her sleeves pushed up, and Calder was on the far side of it with his hands behind his back, in the position he had put his hands in on the dry dock.

"You may read them at the counter. You may not take the originals out of the room, and you may not have them copied, because a copy of an inquest record is made by this office at this office's expense, and the reason for the reason is that a man who carries a copy of a document around in a coat is a man who will be asked one day where he got it."

"It is not fair," she said before he answered. "It is enforceable, and it is written down, and you will be annoyed by both." She had four sheets on the counter with a clerk's stamp on the corner of each. "The ferry's public pressure log. The ferry's own survey, as filed in the inquest record. The Navy seizure report of the *Vigil*. And the harbor's certified copy of the salvage report your father signed."

That last one had been in Calder's hands before. It was a bad copy of a bad copy, and the plate note was on the second page, in a different hand from the rest of the report, which was a repairer's hand, and the note said that a plate had been cut from the ferry's own iron plating and had not come back to the pile, and that the foreman had reported it and had been told to go on with the work.

"The log first," Mara said. "It is the shortest and it is the one nobody has ever wanted."

The *Aster Gannet*'s pressure log was a strip of lined board with a column of marks down it in a clerk's hand, and it recorded the ferry's hull pressures at fixed intervals through the morning of the Gannet Cut: an entry every quarter, the depth, the pump, the state of the valves, the master's initials against the hour.

Mara put her finger on the third hour after the seizure.

"Read down," she said. "One. Two. Three. Four. Five. Six. Seven."

"There is a gap."

"There is a three-second gap." She turned the strip so the lamp was across it. "Not a page. Not a line. Three seconds. A hand making marks in a column did not stop, because there is no rule about a stop. He made a mark, and the pen came down again, and the mark at the end of the three seconds is heavier than the others, which is what happens to a nib when a hand comes back to it after being somewhere else."

"Which is not proof of anything."

"Which is not proof of anything, and is proof of less than a man would like, and is not a lie, because nobody wrote it. Somebody was not on the board for three seconds and then they were, and that is all that page can say." Mara took her finger away. "Your father signed a report about what the divers could reach. The Navy signed a report about what the cutter took. The Registry signed a log saying the evidence was uncontested. Three offices, three documents, none of which contradicts another on its face, and every one of them says something a person could have believed when they wrote it."

"And this."

He put his finger on the two heaviest marks on the board.

"This says somebody's hand was somewhere else for three seconds during a seizure in a loading space, and the page cannot tell me where they were, or whether it was a woman or a man, or whether they came back smelling of oil." He took his hand off. "I have been reading that gap since I was nineteen and I have got exactly that much out of it."

"Twelve pages of the survey, and the ninth is the one."

The ninth page was a surveyor's sheet of measured figures drawn to scale, and among them was number four, the aft standing space on the port side: eight feet four by six feet nine clear, and eleven feet two in the depth under the grating.

"Eight foot four by six foot nine," Calder said. "And the manifest says number four stood empty."

"The manifest says the hold stood empty. Number four is on the survey as a standing space, and the survey's whole purpose is to record what fitted where, and a man surveying a ferry records that space whether or not anybody ever puts anything in it." Mara turned the page over and put a sheet beside it.

The sheet beside it was a Crown yard specification, filed with a refit account for a different vessel and therefore public, and it was a plate pattern: a keying schedule at the long edges, a flange form, a plate thickness in sixteenths.

Mara put her finger on the row.

"That is the plating specification for a mnemonic case on a Crown cutter of the *Vigil* class. Three-eighths plate, keyed at the long edge, and a flange form exactly like the one on your shard." She looked at him. "I measured that shard's cut edges in my own hand on Wednesday evening, and the keyed profile of that rim is not a repair. It is the way that plate was cut at a yard. And the flange on your shard is the same form as the flange on the specification, and the same diameter, to a sixteenth."

"You cannot read a size off a flange."

"I cannot read a size off a case, because the case's own dimensions are in custody papers in Veyr that I have never seen and neither has the harbor authority." Her finger moved down the specification. "But I can read a plate's thickness off a cut edge, and the diameter of a flange ground for a rim, and there is a workshop rule on this coast two hundred years old that a sealed case is cut with a flange allowance of a hand's width inside the finished rim. So I can give you the plate and the flange, and from those two a bellwright could give a length and a breadth within a foot either way. I have given him both in my own hand in front of you, and he has written it down as a range and not as a number."

"A sealed case," Calder said. "Cut out of that hull, at that yard, to that specification, and about that size."

"Cut out of a hull of that pattern, to that specification, at some yard working that pattern. Any yard on the coast that had the pattern could have cut one." She straightened up. "Which is the sentence I have to write, and I am going to write it now, and I want you to read it before I seal it: the shard's cut edges are compatible with a sealed case made to a public Crown yard specification, and the compatibility is not an identification, and no person has yet placed a cutter of the *Vigil* class on the ground where the shard was found."

"Then put the ground in it."

"The ground is in it." She had the anchor-bearing sheet out already, and it was the yard's own drawing, with the mole's seam, the shoal, the *Alder Reach*, and the run of the pale line on the bottom. "Sounding bearings, the first evening, taken off the mole's third seam and off the shoal anchorage by three independent bearings, with the time and the man who took each one written against it, all three in his hand, and I have checked them and they agree inside four degrees. And Kest's permit carries the *Vigil*'s recorded mooring ground as a description, and the *Vigil*'s mooring is on the public record, and I have laid one of these on the other on a bench in a yard at seven o'clock in the morning and they lie the same way."

"And that is a fact."

"That is a fact. A bearing, three bearings, and a public mooring line, lying together on the same piece of the coast." Mara's hands were flat on the counter. "And I want to say the other half out loud, because you are the kind of man who will take a fact and run. A case of that plate and flange and a workshop allowance is between seven and eight feet in its longest face. The survey says number four is a clear space of eight foot four by six foot nine. Those two numbers have never been in the same room before this week, and a case of that size would have more than filled that space and would have had to be handled through it in a way that could not have left the grating down and the entry clear."

She stopped.

"That is not a proof that the compartment was not empty. It is a contradiction now available to anybody who holds all four documents, and three of the four in this room are public and the fourth I am about to leave in this office. Nobody has put a case in that compartment. Nobody has measured that compartment. What a contradiction needs is a measurement, at the site, with a steel, and the site is past the harbor line, and the harbor line is the only place I am permitted to send a man this week."

"So it waits."

"It waits because of a line on a plan that a man drew with a ruler, and that is the whole of what a law is." She squared the sheets. "I am entering the four documents in the circuit custody log as consulted. The log is mine and it stays in this office, and the harbor will be given a certificate of what the public figures are, which costs the harbor forty marks and me a line in a book that somebody in Veyr will read one day with an entirely straight face."

"Mara."

"Mr Marr."

"That is the first time you have sounded like a person."

"You have a low opinion of my vocabulary." She closed the log. "Now go and be in your yard, because you have a second document to write and I have not read it and I am not going to."

---

The second document was the hard one. He wrote it at the bench under the north shed with the tide going out and nobody watching him write it, which was the only condition under which he could write it at all.

He wrote it on the back of a fresh slate and headed it in his own hand: *Account of the evidence heard by Calder Marr, unverified, from himself, and not corroborated by any document, witness, instrument, or second person.*

Then he wrote what he had seen, in the order he had seen it, in the flat language of a man writing a labor log.

*He was low, sitting or crouched, on a coil, with a case on his right shoulder and his right hand under the near end of it. He was one of four men carrying it. He was, in his judgment, the smallest of the four, and he had been given the near end because he could not be seen from the rail.*

*He could see six feet of deck, a rail, a second rail a foot beyond it, and a man standing with a paper. He could not see the far side of the deck, the end of the space, or where the case had come from or gone to. The case passed the man and the paper and went out of sight past the second rail.*

*The man with the paper was reading aloud. The witness could hear that numbers were being read and a phrase repeated that included the words standing empty. The witness could not put the words in order and will not supply them.*

*The case passed while the man was turning the paper over. The man did not look up.*

*Shouted words came from behind and above. They were cut in the middle and the two halves did not belong together, and the witness cannot reconstruct them.*

*A tagged line ran along the outboard side of the deck at the level of the deck edge, with fittings at intervals. A hand in a thick glove came into the low frame and closed on one of the fittings. A hard ridge of skin was on the two middle fingers at the second joint. The arm went back and down out of frame and the fitting went with it and the line went slack. Nothing was shown to be cut and no person was shown to be present at the cut.*

*A part of the scene would not resolve, and it is described in the conservator's book as a doubled edge with a hard rim. The witness stopped pushing against it and did not go into it.*

He came to the end of the page and read it back.

Then he wrote three lines at the foot of it.

*This account is one man's. It is not an account of what happened on the ferry. It is an account of what one man saw while he was carrying something, in one position, in bad air, and he could not see past a rail.*

*No mark, number, or reading of the sealed case was visible to him, and the figures on the ninth page of the ferry survey were not visible to him and were not part of what he saw.*

*It agrees with the public manifest nowhere and contradicts it in one particular only, and the contradiction is that a man read a hold empty aloud while a case of the size a specification implies went past him. That is a contradiction about two things happening on one deck. It is not a contradiction about the deck itself, and only a measurement can be that.*

He signed it, and dated it, and wrote his name against the fact that he had been the diver and the only source.

Then he took Mara Venn's unverified note out of his coat — the one she had read out of her book at the slip, the one that said a crew reported a personal association stated as unverified — and he looked at it for a while.

And then he did not add to it.

He put it back in the inner pocket of his coat, and he wrote on the slate that the personal association had been confirmed by the diver and not disclosed, and he left it at that, and it was the only line in either document that was false, and he wrote it in a hand that did not change, and it was the only piece of his father's silence he had ever managed to sell anybody.

---

Alden Marr was planing a gunwale when Calder came into that end of the yard, and he did not stop, and the shaving came off in one long pale curl.

"I heard about the glass."

"I know you did. News at the market, from Rook's mouth, before eight." The blade turned. "You went in a bell and came out with a bell in your head. Your grandmother would have had something to say about that. She said it about everything, so you will not get a better answer out of the family than you are going to get out of me."

"It isn't funny."

"Was there something in it?"

Calder stood at the end of the bench and looked at his father's hands, which were oil-blackened in the creases, and at the curl of wood coming off the blade, and at the shutter over the little window of the office at the back, which was shut, and had been shut since the Gannet.

"There was a case on her deck," he said. "Sealed, and large, and four men carrying it along a loading space, and a man reading a list out loud about the hold standing empty, and he read it twice while the case went past him."

The blade stopped.

"Was there?" Alden said.

"Yes."

"Then you have told a Registry woman a thing that is going to be in a book." He set the drawknife down flat on the bench, which he had not done since the shoal. "And you have come and told me, and I am going to tell you what I have told you for thirteen years, and I am going to tell it to you in one sentence instead of two, because I have got a gunwale to finish and I have not got the hours I used to have. Ask her. Ask the woman with the book. Ask the office in the city. Ask anybody in the whole of the Serein Dominion who has ever heard of a sealed case in this harbor." He picked the knife up again. "I have never seen one. I signed a report about what divers could reach and I did not see a case, and there is no more in that report than there was in that deck, and I have been the man who signed it for thirteen years and I would like to have one more hour of work before I am asked about it again in a worse room than this."

"Did you see a plate leave that pile?"

"I saw a great many things leave that pile," Alden said, "and I was in the water, and there was a storm on, and the pile went down in the dark, and I have never been able to tell one plate from another in the ten minutes after the water came into the bell, and neither could you."

The cough came and went on for a long time. Calder stayed.

"There was a plate," Alden said, when it was over. "The repairer wrote it down and I signed where he told me to sign, and the harbor paid me, and the harbor is still here. That is all the plate is. It is a line on a page that a man put in because a man told him to, and if you ever stand in a room and say to a judge that the plate proves something, he will ask you what the plate proves, and you will not have an answer, and you will have spent your breath." He turned the gunwale over. "And the survey you are so keen on. Did you ever see the ninth page of it?"

"No."

"Then you have not read the ninth page of it, and I have, and I read it once, and there is a reason it has been sitting in the inquest record for thirteen years with nobody looking at it, and the reason is that looking at the ninth page of a ferry's survey does not tell you what was on the ferry."

"Tell me what it does tell you."

"Nothing." Alden's voice was perfectly level. "That is what I have been trying to tell you for thirteen years and you have not once asked me, and now you have asked me, and my answer is that the ninth page is a page of numbers and I signed a page of words, and there is a difference between them that has cost this family everything it had, and you have just put a bell in your head over a thing that came off the bottom of a mole, and I am not going to help you with it."

Calder stood there.

Then he said, "I have not asked you once in thirteen years."

"No."

"I am not going to ask you twice," Calder said. "I am going to take what you have just given me, which is nothing, and I am going to write nothing about tonight, and I am going to leave."

"You have written something already. You always have. That is how you are built, and I built it." Alden did not look up. "Go on. And Calder."

"Yes."

"Do not go into another one. Not for me, and not for a Registry woman, and not for a bell." The blade turned once more and the curl came off. "Your mother is buried at Ashtide. I would like at least one of us left in this family who can be got at in an ordinary way."

---

He got the message at the fish market, from Bevin, who had a paper in his hand and a face on him.

Bevin was not the boy who had come down the slip at half past three. He was a different shape of frightened, and he was out of breath from the fish market wall.

"Kest's boy is round the fish market with a notice up," Bevin said. "It's not a paper he's carrying, it's a board. And the notice is about us."

"What does it say?"

"It says that a Registry order is to be served at the Free Keel yard this morning before eleven, and that the harbor authority will hold a limited inspection at the yard at eleven for the purpose of a disputed claim, and that the Free Keel is not to make public use of the provenance record of the seized objects pending that inspection." He stopped and got his breath. "And there's something else. There's a boat come into the outer road this morning with a naval pennant on her and a cutter's rig and about four guns, and she's taken a berth at the naval pile, which nobody in Morrow Quay has ever seen a naval vessel do, and the whole quay is standing about looking at her, and Calder — Calder, her name's on her transom in fresh paint. *Vigil*."
