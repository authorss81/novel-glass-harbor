# Chapter 14

*Kest's Terms*

Kest sent for him on Tuesday morning, not to the barge but to the hearing room itself, a square room with a table, six chairs, and a slate wall where the twelve-day defense was chalked in the reeve's careful hand.

Calder sat with his bandaged arm on the table and listened.

"A claim window," Kest said, "is a short permission to work a named wreck and say what you found. Yours is spent, because the glass is gone and the window closed when the courier took the boxes. A work permit names a site and dates and a bond, and lets you put a diver in the water. Yours named the inner edge for two days, and both are used. A one-season charter" — he said the words plainly, as a distant thing — "is a wider seasonal permission to work beyond a single claim and keep a share through the season. It is granted in this room after a hearing, and it must be renewed, and you do not hold one and will not hold one this month."

He laid three papers in a row.

"Your twelve-day defense holds for twelve days and lapses on the thirteenth unless a sitting is held. The sitting must fix a bond and a term, or the defense falls and the naval claim stands alone on the ground. The bond I must ask is thirty marks. The term I can offer is twenty days further, with the harbor's share raised by an eighth against any future raising, to cover the risk of holding ground the Navy also claims."

Calder did the sum. Thirty marks. The yard had seven, and iron on a stack it could not sell under price, and a third of that iron already entered to Sella Brigg.

"We cannot meet thirty," he said. "We can meet twelve, and the iron when it sells."

"Seven in the bag on Monday night," Calder said, "and four of that gone to the chandler before I could put it into air. Twelve is five more than we have. The iron is a third promised to another crew, so a third of what it fetches is not mine to offer, and it may not fetch before the equinox. That is the whole of it."

"Then you are eighteen marks short and you are offering me a sale." Kest put the pen down. "I am not being hard with you. That is the arithmetic, and you may as well have it said out loud in a good month as find it out in a bad one."

"Then you will lose the defense." Kest did not raise his voice. "I am telling you the figure the office requires, not the figure I wish."

Calder looked at the slate wall. "What do you want for a lower bond?"

Kest was quiet a while. Then he said, "Withdraw the public duplicate from the market wall pending the sitting. Not destroy. Take it down and hold it in this office. The Navy's officer has asked it, and it would make my sitting easier."

That was the thing Calder could refuse. The duplicate was a record of objects, not of anything inside them, and Kest himself had said no office in Veyr had power over it. Calder had given his word to Nessa and Bevin that the position went on the wall the day they went out. To take it down would be to unmake the one promise that had held.

"No," Calder said. "The duplicate stays where any hand can read it. You wrote that it stays. I will not take it down to buy a bond."

Kest nodded, once, as if he had expected it and was glad and sorry together.

"Then the bond stands at thirty, and I will write that you refused the courtesy." He dipped the pen. "And now the other thing, which you will not like either. The harbor lifts the drifted mole anchor on Thursday's slack water, with a crane and a chain and a slip's crew, at the harbor's cost and on my mark. Your lead line comes off and mine stays. You are not to be on the mole while it is done. Not because I doubt you. Because the Navy's tender is watching the mole and I will not have a Marr on the wall while municipal tackle comes up."

That was the hostile thing, and it was plain hostility dressed as procedure: to be kept off the mole while his own mark came up, eight feet down off the third seam where he had found the glass, to be told two days before it happened and shown it afterwards from the fish dock.

Calder stood. "Is that the order?"

"It is." Kest came round the table and did something that could not be explained by hostility at all. He took from his coat a folded harbor order, already signed, for five marks' worth of pump packing and tallow, chargeable to the reeve's own repair account, and laid it beside Calder's bandaged arm.

"Your pump wept through the *Merrit* tide," he said. "The coal wharf master sent me the note. Take this to the chandler and draw the packing. I will answer for it at audit. Do not thank me in this room."

Calder looked at the order and at the man.

"You keep me off the mole and you give me tallow."

"I keep you off the mole because a tender with a glass is watching it, and I give you tallow because a yard with no pump is a yard that cannot answer a bond." Kest picked up the slate. "Twenty days, thirty marks, an eighth of the share. The duplicate stays up because you refused me, and I have written your refusal. Come to the sitting with what you can meet, and bring your people."

Calder took the order because the yard needed it and hated taking it, and that was the whole of Kest in one morning.
