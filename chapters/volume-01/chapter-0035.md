# Chapter 35

*What a Woman in a Coat Is For*

The market wall in Morrow Quay had six things on it by the ninth of November and the two of them at the top had both been nailed there inside three days, one over the other, and a man could stand in front of the wall and read the whole of it from the step without moving his feet.

At the top, under a fresh brass-headed nail with a wax seal crushed over the head of it, were four sheets of a naval salvage claim signed at the bottom of the fourth by the captain of the *Vigil*, and under the corner of the fourth sheet, slipped four inches down the wall so that a man had to lift it to see anything, were three sheets of three points in the yard's own hands with a difference of ten yards on the second of them and a foot and a half of the yard's own phrasing underneath. Under those, the notice of the thirty-first with the circuit's seal over its nail. Then the public record of two objects, four sheets in Mara Venn's fair copy with every name on them. Then two crew copies of the same thing, printed without her name. And at the bottom, under a rule, the fifth-seam sheet, which said a plate had been seen in eleven feet and had not been measured and had not been touched and was under a bar.

Bevin Rook had put the naval claim up on the Tuesday night with his right hand and a borrowed hammer and had written the date and the hour on the back of each of his own three sheets and had not folded them, and on the Wednesday morning he had written under the date the additional line that they had been moved by the yard's own request and not by any office, and had read it back to Calder before he nailed them.

---

The paper for Mara Venn came on the Friday the eleventh in an inside envelope with the circuit's stamp on it, and it came to the Free Keel yard because it was addressed to the yard and marked for the attention of the tide conservator of the Morrow circuit, and there was no other address in Morrow Quay to send it to, which everybody understood and nobody wrote down.

She read it standing up in the yard, in the cold, with her book shut under her arm, and she read it twice, and then she came in and sat down on the end of the bench and asked for the whole of it to be read out loud with the four of them in the room, because she wanted the yard to have it before she answered it.

It was a request, in the circuit's own type, and it was addressed to the harbor authority and to the conservator jointly, and it asked for two certifications.

The first was of the fourth document: that the description of the ground entered in the harbor authority's hearing record on the seventh and eighth of this month, together with the three sheets of three points affixed to the wall of the fish market in that harbor, being read together, constitute a true description of the material ground and of its position.

The second was of the yard's record: that the provenance duplicate of the fourteenth of last month, being a record of two objects recovered on harbor ground within the harbor line, and of the position of the ground from which each was raised, is a true record of its own objects and of their origin.

And at the foot of it, the seal line, read out in the room the way a seal line always is: *Sealed and entered at the Morrow circuit office by order of Magister Ilya Serr, senior Registry examiner.*

Nobody said anything for a while. The *Kittiwake* was above them on the hard with her bilge blocked in, and out in the road a gull was working something on the mole steps that was not food.

"Two certificates," Nessa said, "and the first one is the ground and the second one is the objects, and both of them are of things the yard did not find and cannot check."

"Both of them are of things a conservator *is*," Mara said. "That is the difficulty and it is why I have not answered it in the road. I do not see why this office is asking me. Ask a clerk. A clerk would countersign it in an afternoon and nobody would ever know whose hand was on it."

"You are the tide conservator of the circuit," Calder said.

"I am the tide conservator of the circuit," she said, "and that is the whole of what I am, and I have been in this harbor four months and I have three *not stated* entries in a book against this yard and a query on my certificate expense dated the twenty-first of last month, and I would like everybody in this shed to understand the arithmetic of that before anybody says a word about the seal line."

---

She put the request on the bench and squared it, and then she did the thing that Calder had not expected and that he spent the rest of the day thinking about, which was that she read out the whole of what the request would do if it were signed, in the order it would do it, and without any opinion in her voice at all.

"If I certify the first document, then this office holds a Registry document that describes the ground on which two objects were recovered. It is not a description of the objects. It is a description of a place, and a description of a place with a Registry seal on it is a provenance act, because a place has an origin and a position and both of those are what this office certifies for a living.

"And if this office holds a description of the ground, then this office is a party to the custody of what came off it. Not the harbor authority. Not the *Vigil*. This office, in the person of a tide conservator with a seal, asserting in a Registry hand where the ground is. And the moment that is in a file, the two seized items in the custody room in the west of this city are not held pending examination on a question of ground. They are held on a ground that has been certified, and a thing that is held on certified ground is held, and there is no longer any question for a court of claims to be interested in, and the *reason* the items were seized stays exactly where it is, which is nowhere anybody can read it."

She turned the sheet over.

"And the second certificate is worse and it is better, and I want to be fair about which is which. It is better because it would be a Registry witness standing next to the yard's figures, and a Registry witness is heavier than a yard's wall sheet in any room in Veyr that has a Registry officer in it, and every man in this shed has spent a month learning that a document that is only in four hands goes into one office and is argued over there. It is worse because the moment this office certifies that the duplicate is a true record of the objects and of the position of the ground each was raised from, then the duplicate is a Registry record, and the crew's labor log, and the reeve's countersignature on it, and the two permit dates, and the four degrees of agreement between three men with a compass, and every hour of air that Bevin Rook read out of a gauge into a book in a shed — all of it stops being a harbor document and becomes a schedule to a Registry certificate. And a schedule is not a witness. A schedule can be produced without the man who wrote it and read out in a court by somebody who was not there."

"So the certificate makes it heavier," Bevin Rook said, from the end of the bench, "and then it makes it weaker."

"It makes it heavier in one office and weaker in four," Mara said, "and the office it becomes heavier in is the one that wrote the request, and I am not going to say the word *capture* about it because a word like that is a story and stories are what this business is made of and I am the woman who is supposed to keep them out of the hand's work."

---

Nessa Pike put it on the bench, in the flat voice she used for a slate.

"Then do not sign it."

"Mr Marr wants it signed," Mara said.

The room turned round and Calder Marr was standing at the end of his own bench with his hat in his hands.

"I asked you for it at six this morning," he said, "in the road, and I asked you before I had thought about it, and I am not going to pretend I had thought about it. I want the ground certified. I have wanted it since the second of this month, and I want the three points to stop being three sheets of paper on a wall in a fish market and become a document in an office in Veyr, because in four months somebody is going to say the yard's figures are a yard's own arithmetic and I want a hand in a Registry coat on them before that happens."

"You do," Mara said. "And you asked me for it at six this morning and you have not thought about it, and now you have thought about it, and I am going to tell you the thing about the asking and then I am not going to let you take it back."

"I am taking it back."

"You are not." She had not raised her voice. "Mr Marr, I told you on the fourteenth of last month at four in the morning in an infirmary that I had three places in a book where you did not tell me something, and that at some point one of them is going to be the reason a man's evidence is set aside, and I told you which of my instruments it would be, and I did it because I have decided that I would rather be disliked than be a woman who keeps quiet until it is useful. Well. It is the eleventh of November. There is a request on this bench with a seal on it and I have answered it in my own head in eleven minutes and the answer is no, and if you withdraw the asking now then the request goes back to the circuit unanswered, and the circuit's answer to an unanswered request is to send it again in a fortnight with a second seal on it, and the second one will be worded so that a refusal is a delay, and the third one will not come to this yard at all, it will come to this office, and the Registry of this circuit will describe the ground without a conservator in it and without anybody who has ever been on it.

"And if you do not withdraw it, and I refuse, then this office has in its file a request for a certificate, and a refusal, and the request was made of me in the presence of the yard, and the yard wanted it. That is a worse thing for you and a better thing for the record, and I am not your advocate and I am not going to arrange for it to be the better thing for you."

They looked at each other across eleven feet of a shed bench with the stove cold behind them.

"I am not withdrawing it," Calder said.

"Then I will refuse it," Mara said, "and I will do it in writing, and I will give four reasons and not one word of feeling, and the reasons will be correct, and I am telling you now that they will be correct whether you want them to be or not, and that is the only thing I have ever offered you and it is the only thing I am going to keep offering."

---

She wrote it at the bench from noon until two, and she wrote it in the yard rather than in her own office because she said a thing written in a working yard had the date of the yard on it, and then she read it out four times, once to each of them, and Calder wrote it into the crew's book in his own left hand and Bevin Rook read it back twice.

*Refused, and the grounds of the refusal. One. That the description of the ground entered in the hearing record of the seventh and eighth comprises three positions, and that one of those three is not a position but a bedded seam of stated length in a rubble foundation, and that this office cannot certify that a seam is a position, and that no person has asked this office to do so and this office has not offered. Two. That a Registry certification of a place is a provenance act, and that this office is asked for a provenance act in a file in which this office is already a party by reason of its custody of two objects, and that a conservator who certifies a ground cannot afterwards be a witness to method in the same matter. Three. That the second document asked for is a record of two objects and of their origin, and that the object of undetermined origin is entered as of undetermined origin in the custody log of this office, and that this office cannot certify the origin of an object it has entered as undetermined, and that the request is not capable of being answered in the terms in which it is put. Four. That the notice of the thirty-first of last month, which this office issued, is served and correct, and that nothing in either of the documents asked for contradicts that notice, and that this office will not issue a document that assists in the reading of its own notice as a measure about ground, and will correct that reading if asked in writing by an office that has jurisdiction.*

"That is four reasons," Nessa said, "and three of them are about what you will not do, and only one of them is about what you cannot do."

"Yes," said Mara Venn. "And I have put the one I cannot do in first, because the one I cannot do is the one that does not go away when I am tired, and because a person should be known by the reason she cannot give and not by the reasons she will not."

She signed it as tide conservator of the Morrow circuit, with the hour, and she dated it the eleventh of this month, and she wrote at the foot of it, in the same hand, one further line, and read that out too.

*Lodged for countersign. While the query of the twenty-first of last month stands, this entry does not leave my hand without a circuit officer's countersign, and the date of this refusal is the date it is countersigned and not the date it is written.*

"Then it is the twenty-second of this month," Calder said.

"It is the twenty-second of this month," Mara said, "and I have not chosen that and cannot, and I said so in a hearing room on the seventh and I am saying it again at this bench because I would like the yard to have it in a second hand. Eight days after your defense lapses. Fifteen days after the hearing. Written on the eleventh, dated the twenty-second, and there is nothing in the world I can do about it and I have thought about nothing else since Monday."

"You have told me the date three times now," Calder said, "and every time you have told me it you have refused something else, and I would like it noticed that I have never once thanked you for either, and I would like it noticed by somebody else in this room as well."

"Noticed," said Nessa Pike.

---

The rest of the day went on the wall, because there was nothing else in the yard to go on.

Calder went down at four to look at it, standing in the cold with his hands behind his back, and Bevin Rook came out and stood four feet off him without speaking, because that was the arrangement they had fallen into on the second of the month and had not discussed since.

"You put the Navy's paper over ours," Calder said.

"I put it over ours and I slipped ours four inches down so a man has to lift a corner," Bevin said. "I have written the date and the hour on the back of all three and the moved-by line on the front of the first one, and I read it back before I nailed, and it is on the wall now, and it is not a copy of anything, it is the same three sheets that were on the wall on the second, and that stood in chalk on the hearing room's slate wall until a boy with a cloth took them off that board at eight on the Monday morning, and that the reeve has put back on that board himself in chalk in eleven minutes and signed the corner, and they will come off it again on Monday at eight because he has done it every Monday for six years."

"I know. I am not finding fault. I am standing here."

"You stood here on the second and on the fourth and you have stood here every day since the notice went up, and the last three times you have been counting things."

"I have been counting things," Calder said, "and you are the only person in this yard who has noticed, and I would like you to go on noticing and to keep the dates on the back of everything we own, including the ones we have not made yet."

At the top of the make, out in the road, the harbor's tide bell went three strokes, and Calder stood still with his hands behind his back and did not turn round, and nothing came up out of the bottom of the harbor under it that Bevin Rook wrote down, and the boy took the slate out of his coat and looked at it and put it back, and neither of them said anything about that either.

---

Bevin Rook asked the question at eight o'clock, at the bench, with the stove lit, and he asked it in the flat voice.

"Can I ask her something that is not about the case."

"You can ask her one thing," Nessa said, "and you will not get a second one out of her today because she has been in this shed since noon and she is owed a day like anybody else."

"Can I ask whether a woman in a Registry coat is ever going to be anything else," Bevin Rook said, "because there is a thing on that wall with a seal on it that says *presumed Crown material until a recognized witness or a municipal claimant is entered against it*, and I have read it every day since the thirty-first of October, and I have never once been able to make out what a recognized witness is, and I have got a right hand that can write and a left arm in a sling and I have read every document in this yard since the eighth of October, and I would like somebody to tell me whether reading them is worth anything, because a man in a cutter's coat read ten of them in a room on the Tuesday and wrote in his book that the ground was continuous and as it was worked, and nobody in this shed can stop him."

Mara Venn closed her book.

"Two things," she said, "and they are both for you and neither of them is kind.

"A recognized witness, in the language of that notice, is not a person. It is a document. It means a document entered in a Registry book against an object, by an office, on terms that the office then holds. A family does not enter a recognized witness. A harbor authority does not enter a recognized witness. An office enters a recognized witness, and when it has, the object is not Crown property and it is not yours either, it is settled, and settled means a Registry examiner has looked at the material and certified that the material may be handled by named persons for named purposes, and every one of those purposes is one that somebody in Veyr decided you need.

"And reading them is worth everything," she said, "and it is worth nothing on the same day. You have read every document in this yard since the eighth of October and you have done it with a hand that cannot close and you have written ten entries in a book that four men can reach, and there is a naval officer in this harbor who has read the same ten and has a boat behind him. In four months the difference between you and him will not be what you know. It will be that he can get a thing done with it and you cannot. That is the whole of what I have to say to a boy with a sling on his arm, and I am sorry about it, and I have said it in front of the reeve's clerk's bench rather than to you privately because I do not say useful things to people in private."

"I would rather have had it privately," Bevin said.

"I know you would," said Mara Venn. "That is why I did not."
