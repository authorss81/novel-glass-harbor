# Chapter 31

*What the Room Is For*

The hearing room was over the tide-gang store on the quay, and it had one window, and the window looked at the inside of the fish market roof, so that anybody who wanted to know who was inside it could stand in the market and look up at the glass and see the shapes of people moving.

On the morning of the seventh the room held eleven people and one stove, and the stove was lit at half past eight because Kest lit it at half past eight whether there was a sitting or not.

Kest sat behind the table with the clerk at his right hand. The clerk was the young man with ink on his cuffs, and he had a new ledger open and the desk slate beside it and the two custody numbers — two numbers, digits not spoken — copied out on a separate sheet because a hearing record is not the place for a Registry number.

The naval officer of the *Vigil* sat in the corner with the notebook shut on his knee, and had sat in that corner at the sitting of the twenty-fourth and had not opened the notebook once in two days.

Nessa Pike came in with her coat over her arm because she had come from the yard and had not stopped to change. Tobias Wren took the chair by the door, which he had not chosen and which was the only chair with its back to the wall. Bevin Rook came last, with his left arm across his chest in the sling, and put the crew's book on the table in front of him and did not open it, and put his right hand flat on the cover of it.

Mara Venn came in last of all, in her Registry coat, with her book shut under her arm, and sat down apart from the crew on a chair against the wall, and the clerk marked her in the attendance column as *Registry, present, not a party*, and she looked at the two words and did not ask him to change them.

Calder Marr stood in the middle of the floor with his hat in his hand because there was nowhere to put a hat in that room.

"Before we begin," Kest said, "three things about the shape of this morning, and then the yard's terms, in the order they were written, and then whatever anybody in this room wants to say, and I will keep the order or I will not and you will hear which.

"This is not the defense. The defense of the twenty-fourth of last month is not before me today and it will not be before me today. That term runs to the fourteenth and I have written no order against it and nobody has moved it. What is before me is the yard's written terms and the Crown's claim, which was lodged in this office on the fourteenth of last month and has never been entered, and which I am entering today whether anybody objects or not, because a lodgment that is never entered is not a claim and it is not a bar either, and I have had it in a drawer for twenty-four days and I have not liked it in the drawer.

"And the second thing. Nothing said in this room today is safe. Everything said in this room today is read out in a room in Veyr within the year, and I will not be the man who says so afterwards for the first time." He looked at the clerk. "Write that down, and read it back."

The clerk wrote it and read it back.

"Then read the terms," Kest said.

---

Bevin Rook read them. He read them out of the crew's book, standing up, in the flat voice he had been using for numbers since the twentieth of last month, and he read them slowly, and he did not skip anything and he did not put anything in of his own, and at the end of each one he said the number of it, because he had been asked to on the fourth and had asked whether he wanted the numbers and had been told he did not.

The clerk copied them into the hearing record as they were read. When Bevin came to the third one he stopped for a moment over the page and then went on.

"Three. That the anchor-bearing sheet be not used and not asked about, being a drawing and not a finding of where the *Vigil* lay, and that the yard withdraws its own filed suggestion that the sheet and the mooring line be laid together in a room."

"Stop there," Kest said.

Calder said, "That is the term as written on the fourth."

"That is the term as written on the fourth, and I am going to read it twice and then I am going to rule on it, and I am going to rule on it before the yard has finished reading, because I have read it nine times since it was written and I want it in the record in the order it was decided and not the order it was read." He turned to the clerk. "Strike the sheet from this sitting. Not from the file. From this sitting, and from any future sitting of this office, at the yard's own request and in the yard's own words, and let the record show that the yard asked for it and that the reeve granted it in eleven seconds. Is that right."

"Yes," Calder said.

"Say it to the room, Marr, because in four months a naval officer is going to read this record and ask whether the harbor authority suppressed a document, and I want the answer to be the yard's and not mine."

Calder turned round and faced the eleven people in the room, which was a thing he had not expected to do and did badly.

"Term three is mine and I wrote it on Friday night," he said. "On the twenty-seventh of last month I filed a purpose with this office and the purpose said the ground on our bearing sheet and the ground in the office's own shoal book lie on a single line, and I did that because I had three figures and three figures on a bearing and I wanted them to be a line. The sheet is the yard's own drawing. It is a drawing. The three men who took the bearings can speak them and I have asked the room not to ask about the sheet, and the reeve is granting that, and I am not going to argue for it in the next four days and take it back on the fifteenth."

"That is entered," the clerk said.

"Enter it as a thing the yard said with a date on it," Kest said, "and not as a thing the yard agreed to. And get the sheet out of the chest and put it in the clerk's drawer with a slip of paper on it saying *not used at the sitting of the seventh, at the yard's request, by the reeve's ruling*, and the slip signed by both of us, because a document that has been set aside by consent is a different animal from a document that has been lost."

Nobody in the room had expected that, and Calder understood about four minutes later, on the mole, why it mattered.

---

Kest ruled through the morning. He did it in the order of the terms and he read each ruling out before he made it, which is a thing that four offices in this business do and one of them does properly.

The first term went the yard's way in nine words. The labor of the Free Keel yard upon the two dates of the permit of the twelfth of last month was found proved, as it had already been found and countersigned, and was not to be disturbed by anything said after it. The clerk stamped the countersigned labor log where it lay on the table, and the stamp was the same stamp that had been on it since the twelfth, and Calder looked at the ink on it and had to look at the wall for a moment.

The second term went the yard's way because it cost the yard nothing to ask. The four hundred yard bar off the outer marks stood; the yard had not asked for it to be lifted and did not intend to go within it; and the reeve recorded that the yard had said so in a room, on the seventh, in front of a naval officer with a notebook.

The third term went the yard's way by the yard's own hand.

The fourth term went part of the way and Kest made his reasons out loud, which is where a reeve earns or loses a morning.

"I will receive the three points as a record of the crew's own figures and of one figure out of a book this office wrote, with the tolerance of the method stated by the men who took it. I will not ask the yard to say what any of them is, and I have ruled that no line may be drawn between them and I am not lifting that today.

"And I am going to say the half of it that is mine, and then I am going to sit down again. The second of those three points is a bearing taken by three men from a station on this office's ground, on a sheet this office signed, and the bearing is good work and I will not have it touched. The first of them is not a bearing. It is something Rook found with his hand on a bottom at nine feet and it is bedded in my mole's own foundation, and I refused on the twenty-eighth to enter the words pale material and bedded, and I stand by that, and it is going to matter more in this room today than anything the yard has brought with it." He squared the paper. "The third of them is my office's own figure and the yard asked to use it and I let it, in daylight, with the clerk in the room, and one figure out of thirty-six, and if the Crown's officer wants to say that is a figure taken from a book this harbor authority stopped sounding, he may say it, and I will enter it, and the yard will not be asked to argue with him about it."

The naval officer opened the notebook for the first time in the morning and wrote about eleven words and shut it again.

The fifth term went the yard's way with a ruling attached to it, and the ruling was the thing Calder had actually wanted. The thing at the fifth seam, seen and not measured on the third at the top of the tide, was received as seen and not measured. The yard was not to produce a figure for it. And the reeve recorded, on his own motion and not on the yard's, that the thing at the fifth seam was entered in the harbor authority's own book on the third in the presence of three witnesses whose names were on the entry, and that the entry was made by the officer of this office and was not withdrawn by him.

"Take that last line down," Calder said. "That is yours and I did not ask for it."

"It is mine and you did not ask for it," Kest said, "and I will tell the room why I wanted it in, and then I will sit down. I wrote an instrument on the thirty-first for that ground and carried it in my coat for three days and showed it to nobody, and on the third I went down those steps and looked at my own foundation stone after serving a notice that closed it, and entered what I saw with three witnesses against it. I would like that in the record in my own words and not in yours, because in about two years a person is going to read that entry and decide whether the harbor authority of this harbor can be trusted to write down what it has seen, and I would like the answer to be that it wrote it down anyway."

Nobody said anything to that, and the clerk wrote it down, and the stove ticked four times.

The sixth term went the yard's way on a date. The notice of the thirty-first was received as served and as correct. The yard said, in one sentence, that it had said all it meant to say about that notice in the conservator's second note, which was with a circuit officer and bore the date of the twenty-second of this month, and the yard asked the room to note that date and not to wait for it. Kest entered the date, and the hour, and the fact that the note was not in the room and had not been read into the record, and then he did the thing that was in nobody's terms at all.

"Mr Venn," he said. "Is the note in existence."

"It is," Mara said.

"Will it be read into a record before the fourteenth."

"No."

"Will it be countersigned at all."

"It will be countersigned or it will not come back, and I cannot tell you which, and neither can you, and I have written down the date it will bear if it does, which is the twenty-second, and that is a date the office has put on it and not one I have chosen." She did not open her book. "Mr Kest, I will say one thing in this room because it is going to be said in it in a worse room otherwise. The second note does not say the notice is wrong. The second note says what the first note did and did not say, and that a join travels along a joined line, and that a line of glass lying in a row on a bottom is not thereby a joined line. If the notice of the thirty-first has been read as a measure about glass, then the notice has been read as something my office did not write, and I have asked my office to correct that in its own hand while it still can. The office has not done so. That is all I have."

"Thank you," Kest said, and wrote *Registry officer present, statement made, not contested by this office*, and Calder understood that she had just made herself the only person in the room who had said the word *quarantine*'s opposite out loud in front of the man who could carry it to Veyr, and that she had done it in nine sentences and had not once asked the yard for anything.

Then Kest took up the seventh term and read it twice, and put it down.

"The seventh term asks this room to hear the Free Keel yard say that a clause which stops three named people from earning a living inside their own harbor line has been read in Veyr as a measure about glass and is not one." He looked at Calder. "I am going to hear it, because I am not going to be the reeve who refused a hearing about a paper. Say it in your own words and not out of the book."

"Clause three of the notice of the thirty-first," Calder said, "stops me, and Pike, and Rook, from going into the water inside this harbor on the authority's work without a written plan countersigned by a circuit officer. That is three people out of a yard of four. It is not a measure about glass. It is a measure about three men who handled a piece of glass once, on a bench, on the fourteenth of last month, and put it in a box and gave it to a courier, and the notice does not say that and does not need to, because a person who has handled it is a person who has handled it forever in the eyes of an office that has written it down. And the yard is not asking for the clause lifted. The yard is asking to be heard saying what it is, out loud, in a room, with a date on it, so that when somebody in Veyr reads the file in a year there are two documents about this notice instead of one, and one of them is a man saying it plainly and one of them is a notice that says it in four clauses and a seal."

"Entered," the clerk said.

"Entered, and I am going to add the reeve's own words to it, and the yard does not get to choose them." Kest looked at the naval officer and then at Calder. "I have the only countersign in this harbor that would not help, and I do not have the one that would. I have read the fourth clause of that notice four times and I have not found one word in it that is wrong, and I have read the fourth paragraph of the conservator's note of the fourteenth of last month, which I was shown in this room on the twenty-seventh, and I have not found one word in that wrong either. I have not got a paper that says this notice is wrong, and I have not got a paper that says it is right either, because a reeve's own seal on a four-clause notice is not a finding that the notice is right, it is a finding that a man served it correctly. And I would like that in the record, and I would like somebody in this room to argue with it, and I do not expect to be lucky."

"Entered," the clerk said, and Kest wrote *heard, not decided*, and the word *not decided* was the most useful thing the reeve had put on any slate in three months.

---

The eighth term was the bottom, and Kest took it up at four minutes past one in the afternoon with the room gone quiet in a way the room had not been quiet all the morning.

"Term eight asks that the bottom be restored to the holder of the crew's book for the term, in writing, with a stated reason entered before he goes in, a tender named who is not the diver, and a cutoff, and asks for no date and no place." He read the last four words again. "I told a man on the mole on the twenty-eighth of last month that the answer to this would be yes, and I said it out loud with three of you standing on it, and I have had four days to decide whether I was being a coward or a courtesy, and I was neither. I was being a man telling another man what a room would do, which is a thing I do not like and cannot stop doing.

"Restored. In writing, to the holder of the crew's book, for the term, with a stated reason entered before he goes in, a named tender who is not the diver, and a cutoff the tender can pull. No date. No place. And the harbor authority's own undertaking of the twenty-fifth of last month, by which Calder Marr is off the bottom, is lifted by this instrument and by nothing else and from this moment."

Calder said, "Thank you," and Kest said, "Do not thank me yet, Marr, because I have read the eighth term nine times and there is a word in it that is going to take your bottom off you in the same afternoon, and I would rather say the word out loud in this room than have you find it on a piece of paper in four days."

He put two fingers on the notice on the table.

"That is *the term*. And the term of this defense of the twenty-fourth of last month runs to the fourteenth of this month. You have your bottom back for seven days from today and not one day after the fourteenth, and if the term is not extended on the fourteenth then on the fifteenth the undertaking of the twenty-fifth stands again in my hand without either of us having done anything. I am telling you that because you would not have known it and would have gone to the mole."

"That is fair," Calder said, and it came out of him before he had decided to say it, and he heard himself say it, and did not take it back.

Kest wrote the restoration and the limit of it into the record, and the clerk read it back, and it was in the book, and it was signed by the reeve, and it was worth exactly nothing at all, and everyone in the room knew that, and the naval officer in the corner wrote two lines and did not look up.

The ninth term was the authority's own instrument of the third, and Kest took it as a piece of paper rather than as a request.

"Received," he said. "Six clauses, dated the third, at the top of the tide on the eighth, being a spring tide, held open at this office's pleasure and without a new purpose and without a new bond. It is conditional and the condition is a plan countersigned by a circuit officer of the circuit office, and that office has not asked about a plan, and I have not asked it for one, and I am going to tell the room exactly why I have not asked, and it is the last thing I say today.

"I have the countersign to put in front of that office that would move it. A reeve can write *requested* on a form. A reeve's request is a pressure, and a pressure is a thing an office is entitled to refuse without giving a reason, and if I ask, that office will refuse, and the refusal will be on the record, and the record will then say that the thing at the fifth seam may not be looked at because a reeve wanted to look at it." He put his hand flat on the notice. "So I am not asking. That is what this office needs and it is in my own voice, and Mr Marr can go and ask it himself, in his own voice, on his own paper, without this office anywhere in it. And when he is refused — and he will be, and I would be ashamed of the office if it were not — the refusal will be a circuit office refusing a plan and not a harbor authority being refused anything at all."

Nessa Pike said, from the back, "That is the whole of it, then, and you have said it in front of us, and the term asked you to."

"It is the whole of it," Kest said, "and I have said it, and I would like it noticed that the yard asked me to say a thing in my own voice and the thing I said was a refusal, and that is not a thing a man does for a yard he likes."

The tenth term was the last one on the page, and Calder read it himself, because it was his and he had said he would.

"Ten. That if this defense falls on the fourteenth, the Free Keel yard will say in this room on the fourteenth what it has, and how it came, and what it does not know, and it will not go to another forum with the same figures, and it will not put another crew's numbers into this book to make this one look better."

"Answered," Calder said. "I am answering it now, in this room, so that there is no condition on it. If the defense falls on the fourteenth I will stand in this room on the fourteenth and say what we have and how it came and what we do not know, and I will say it in the words on that page and not in better words. And if the defense does not fall on the fourteenth then these figures have been read out in a room with a clerk in it, and they are in that room's record, and they go no further from here — not to the circuit office, not to the Court of Wreck Claims, not to the cutter's boat, and not to the market wall, and the three sheets on that wall are not figures and a man may take a ruler out of his pocket and do his own sum.

"And the last part of it, and it is Bevin's part and he wrote it with me and he will read it back to me in a minute." He turned round. "No other crew's numbers go into this book. Not the tide gang's. Not Sella Brigg's slate. Not the *Grey Widow*'s. If a number of ours is better because a number of somebody else's is in the room, then we do not use it, and if a number of ours only holds up beside a number of theirs then it does not hold up. That is the whole of it and I would like it read back."

Bevin Rook read the whole of the tenth term back, word for word, and his right hand was steady on the book and his left arm was across his chest, and when he had finished he said, "That is what is written and that is what you said, and they are the same, and the reeve can check them."

"Checked," the clerk said.

---

The room emptied at half past three and then it did not, because the *Vigil's* boat came alongside the quay at four with a man in a cutter's coat on her foredeck holding a flat case against his chest, and the officer in the corner stood up, and the clerk closed the ledger and opened it again, and Kest said one word to the room that made Calder's stomach go cold.

"Enter it," Kest said, and then stopped, and put his hand flat on the ledger, and did the second half of it in front of fourteen people, which he had not had to do in fifteen years.

"I am not going to read four sheets of a naval claim at four o'clock on a Monday with a tired clerk and no stove in the barge and half the market standing in the road outside. This office entered a lodgment on the fourteenth of last month and has had it in a drawer for twenty-four days, and I have not liked it in the drawer, and the reason I have not read it is that I did not want to read it in a hurry.

"This sitting stands adjourned until ten o'clock tomorrow morning, and the claim is entered then, in this room, in full, in order, with the seal line and the signature line read out, and the yard is at liberty to object to it before it is entered and the objection will go on the same page. Mr Officer — name yourself for the record, and say whether the vessel objects to a Tuesday morning."

"Vessel's officer, by the authority of the commanding officer, and the vessel does not object to a Tuesday, and does not need to be told what time the reeve's clerk prefers his tea."

"Then be here at ten," Kest said, "and bring the paper you have in that case and leave it in your own hands, and Mr Clerk will not touch it until the captain's hand is on the table."

The naval officer said, "The case is a Registry case and it does not travel in a municipal room without a courier's hand on it."

"Then the courier may stand in the doorway," Kest said, "and may not come further, and this office will note in its own record that the claim of the imperial cutter *Vigil* was tendered on the seventh of this month at four o'clock in the afternoon and entered on the eighth at ten, and that it was lodged on the fourteenth of last month and not entered for twenty-four days, because the reeve did not like it in a drawer. Both of those dates go in. Neither of them is a courtesy to anybody."

He shut the ledger.

"Tomorrow at ten," he said. "And Mr Officer — the yard may name its objection at ten and not this evening, and the reason is that I want the claim in front of the yard in its own words before the yard answers it, and if a man objects to three words in a document he has not been read, then in two years somebody will read the objection and say the reeve had not read the claim either. The Free Keel yard objects tomorrow or not at all."

Calder Marr stood in the middle of the floor of a hearing room on a Monday evening with his hat in his hand and understood that he had eighteen hours to find out which three words, and that the reeve had just given the room a rule about objecting to a document you have not been read, and had done it in front of a naval officer on purpose.
