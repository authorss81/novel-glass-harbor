# Chapter 71

*A Ground and Not a Refusal*

The argument about how to answer the circuit office began on Thursday the second of March at about eight in the morning with a slate on the bench, and it was not finished until twenty past ten, and two and a half hours of it was one woman not being asked anything, and that is the first honest work the yard did that day.

Nessa Pike wrote the three ways on the slate and then rubbed two of them out, which is the only way she has ever had of choosing.

"You cannot refuse it," she said. "A refusal is dated at a counter and a dated refusal is an answer, and an answer is a thing the other office gets to read first and answer second, and it has been standing unanswered since the twenty-seventh of January and every week it stands it gets a little cheaper for them and a little more expensive for us. Mara, what is it costing a week."

"Fourteen days in the shape of the file," Mara Venn said. She had been in the shed since half past seven with her cup and she had not drunk it. "Not in marks. In the file. The office is not waiting for a decision, it is waiting for a date, and every date it has is a fact about a party in Morrow Quay that has not answered a request in a fortnight, and by the third seal that fact will be the whole of what a man in Veyr knows about this yard."

"And the third way."

"Say a ground," Calder Marr said. "Not a reason. A reason is what you write at eight in the morning in the reeve's book so that a man can go in the water. A ground is what you say to an office to make it do the next thing, and the next thing has to be one we have read before we say it."

"That is right," Mara Venn said, "and I am not going to tell you what the ground is, and Calder, look at me for one second. You are not going to ask me."

"I was going to ask you what it costs."

"You were going to ask me what it costs and then you were going to ask me whether it is a good one, and the second one is the one I am priced for and I have not got an office behind it any more, and a woman with no office behind her who is asked whether a ground is good is a woman being made useful, and I have said no to that in this shed in front of four people once and I am not going to have to say it twice in five months."

Nessa Pike rubbed the last of the three words off the slate and left the blank under them, which is the yard's way of saying a thing has been decided.

Then they wrote the ground, and it took until about half past ten, and it went in as four paragraphs in Calder Marr's own left hand, and the reason it is in his hand and not hers is that he is the party and she is a trade.

---

**The first paragraph** was the easy one and it said what the sheet is. That the public sheet of the fourteenth of October, in three parts and four hands, is a record of two objects and of measurements and of custody, and is not a witness, and is not to be read as the content of anything, and that the limits are written inside the rules and not in a note at the foot of them, and that the party has put the sheet on a wall in a market where any man may read it at no charge to him.

**The second** was the count, and it was the paragraph Bevin Rook asked for by name.

"Ten on the twenty-third of November, when the count was published," the boy said. "Eleven from the eighteenth of January, when a man called Amos Pryde asked for one at his own asking and was given it. The office's paper is dated the thirtieth of December and says ten, and ten was the correct count on the thirtieth of December, and the party does not say that the office is in error. The party says that both figures are true of their own dates and that the party has entered both with the dates and has struck out neither, and that a party which has told the truth twice in public has not corrected anybody and has not been corrected."

"That is the whole of the ground in one paragraph and it is worth having," Mara Venn said. "Go on."

**The third** was the ground itself and it was the shortest of the four.

"That the party cannot deliver what is not in the party's hand," the boy read out, "and that of the copies of the said sheet now in circulation in this harbor, two are upon the party's own market wall, five are rolled in oilcloth in the party's own bench chest, three are upon the walls of crews who are not parties to the said instrument and whom the party has not asked and whom the party has no power to ask, and one is with a master of no crew of this association at a stall upon this dock, who asked for it on the eighteenth of January, at his own asking, to be kept by him and not returned, and that the fact is entered in the party's book in his own right hand with the date upon it, and the party does not dispute that the office may ask that man as it may ask anybody, and the party has no standing to ask him and has not asked and will not."

"And that is four copies the office cannot get from us," Nessa Pike said, "and seven it can, because the chest and the wall are ours and there are seven in the two places."

"Yes," said Calder Marr.

"Then stop there, because that ground says the office can have seven and we are handing over seven, and I am not going to have a man write a ground that is a refusal dressed as a difficulty when we have seven copies sitting in a chest four feet from where he is writing it."

---

**The fourth paragraph** was the one that was not in anybody's head at eight o'clock in the morning, and it was found by the boy, at about eleven, with a pencil and a bad wrist, and he read it out before he had decided to.

"The party's book contains an entry of the twenty-sixth of January which is of the day of another crew and not of the party, being the work of Noll Tarner of the *Sea Wren* upon his own claim and his own account, entered in the party's book on the twenty-eighth of January at his own asking and without his asking for anything else, and copied to the party's market wall at the same time. The said *Sea Wren* is not a party to the said instrument. She has not been asked by the party and the party has no power to ask her, and the said instrument does not name her. And therefore the party cannot deliver that entry either, because a party that delivers another crew's day to an office which has not asked that crew is not keeping a record, it is handing the day over, and the yard has spent five months finding out the difference between those two things and it is not going to unlearn it in one afternoon for a three-mark fee."

Nobody in that shed said anything for about half a minute, and the stove ticked, and down on the dock a capstan went round somewhere.

"Read that back," Nessa Pike said.

"I wrote it, I am not reading it back—"

"Read it back," she said, "because in about a year somebody is going to want to know whether that paragraph was in the ground when the ground went up the coast or whether it was put in afterwards because it suited somebody. Read it back."

He read it back. Calder Marr read it back after him. That is three, and the rule is that four men read the same day it is written, and the yard had not found a fourth man that morning and had not gone and got one, and Alden Marr was at the far bench and was not asked for anything and was not going to be used to make a number come out right, and Calder Marr did not go over to him.

"That is a true thing," Mara Venn said, "and it is the only one of the four paragraphs that the office cannot get round, and I am not going to say any more about it than that, and I am going back to the beginning because you are all going to do the thing you always do and treat the best paragraph as though it were free."

---

**It cost three marks and two days.** She had priced it on the twenty-seventh of January in three figures and the yard had entered them in the order she gave them, and nobody in that shed was going to ask her for them again, and Calder Marr said them out loud himself like a man reading a receipt.

"Three marks to have anything entered at a counter. A day's walk each way, for two of us, so two days. And a man standing in a queue, which is the third mark of the six and the form is free. Three and two, and we are not paying the other three and I am not going to explain in a book why not."

"You are going to explain it in a book because I will not let a man walk a day for nothing and call it nothing," Nessa Pike said. "Two men out of a crew of three and a book is not free. It is the most expensive two days this yard has had in five months, because on the two days you are not on this dock there is no crew for a man who comes up the dock, and there is a fleet in this basin and one of them will want something in the next fortnight."

"Then it goes in as two days and not as a figure," Calder Marr said, "and it goes in with the day it stops us working, and I will not have it entered as money, because if I enter it as money I will have to enter what a day is worth and I do not know, and a man who does not know does not put a number in a book."

"Enter it as a day," Nessa Pike said, "and enter under it that the party states he does not know what a day is worth, and put my name against the objection so that in four years a clerk in Veyr can see that a shipwright in this yard said the number was owed and the holder would not write it. That is the whole of what I have been asking for since November and it has taken me until now to know that I wanted it."

It went in that way. The boy wrote it and read it back and read it back again, and there is a line in the book of the second of March with two names on it and no figure, and it is the only page of that winter where a figure was wanted by two people and written by neither.

---

They opened the bench chest at about two in the afternoon because a number in a paragraph is worth nothing until a man has put his hands on the paper, and there had not been a hand on any of the seven since the eighteenth of January.

The chest is against the side of the bench with the shoal iron stacked against it, twenty marks' worth of unsold shackles and a crown with a saw kerf in it, and the whole stack is the bond of the charter at a clerk's thirteen, so that nothing in that chest can be sold without a clerk's hand on a page, and the five rolls in oilcloth lay on top of the iron with a piece of the yard's sailcloth tied over them.

Nessa Pike untied the sailcloth herself and counted them out on the boards, and they came out flat and cold and a little stiff at the folds, and there was tar in the weave of the first one where it had lain against the iron all winter.

"Five," she said. "Two on the wall. Seven."

"Eleven," the boy said.

"Eleven is not ours," Nessa Pike said, and put her thumb on the first roll. "Seven of them are. And I want the seventh one counted twice and I want it in the book that it was counted twice, and I want the date on it, because the day this ground goes up the coast is the last day anybody in this yard can say for certain where these seven are, and after Monday they are a number in a paragraph in a ledger in the west of this city, and a number in a ledger in the west of this city is a thing that does not come back on a cart."

The seven were counted twice and the date went on it, and then they went back into the chest in oilcloth under the sailcloth, and the chest was shut, and the twenty marks of iron were against it again, and the crown with the saw kerf in it was where it had been since October.

The fee went on the counter on Monday the sixth with the ground in Calder Marr's coat and the twenty-ninth day of January in it in the other pocket, and the market on the Sunday before had been the busiest of the winter, and the *Sea Wren* had been alongside the fish dock at six in the morning with her pump in her and a job on her board and Noll Tarner on the gunwale talking to a man about a shackle, and she was the boat in the fourth paragraph, and her day was in the second, and neither man in that shed had said a word about why it mattered that a ground naming her was going up the coast on the Monday, and the boy wrote the ground out again on Sunday night in his own hand and did not read it back to anybody because there was nobody to read it back to.

He wrote it out because it was going to be a copy, and a copy of a ground is a thing a man can hold, and the yard had learned five months ago what a man can do with a sheet of paper once he is holding it and has nothing else to do with it.

Calder Marr and Nessa Pike went up the coast on the Monday morning at twenty-five minutes past five with the original in one coat and the copy in another, and it is two days there and back together and they will be in this harbor again on the Tuesday night with a receipt and a paper and a figure written in pencil on the back of the receipt, and the reason it is the two of them and not the boy is that the boy is the only one of the three who cannot be replaced, and a fish dock with nobody on it for two days is a fish dock a man comes to and finds shut.
