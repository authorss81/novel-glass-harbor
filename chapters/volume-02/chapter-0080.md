# Chapter 80

*The Last Day of the Charter*

The herring skipper was at the head of the fish dock at half past seven on Friday the thirty-first of March with a fouled ring of his own and a lead line and twenty marks in his hand, and he had walked up from the second lay on the last day of the month because he had known for a fortnight what day it was.

"Six hours of flood after eleven," he said. "Four foot six at the head, and it is the second seam, and it is mine and it is my own account and I have read your wall and I know what the notice does not close. Twenty marks and I have it here and I do not want it argued about."

"No," Calder Marr said.

The man stopped with the coin in his hand and did not put it away and did not offer it again either, and he waited, because men from this dock have learned to wait about four seconds before they say the second thing, and Calder Marr had it ready.

"I am not going to argue you out of it and I am not going to tell you the weather. I am going to tell you the reason and you are going to like it less than the weather. Our instrument runs to the end of today and does not go into April. I have not got a second season, I have not got a paper for the first of April, and I have no office to ask one of, and I am not going to put a boy of twenty-two in the water on the last day of a charter to finish a job I have started, and I would not have done it in March of any year and I am not going to start on Friday the thirty-first."

"You have got a charter until midnight."

"I have got a charter until midnight and a pump and a boy with a wrist and four weeks of a wage I have not paid," Calder Marr said, "and the honest answer to your question, which is what do I do, is that I do not know. That is the sentence. I have been in this harbor five months saying a smaller thing than what I want and I am not going to hand you a bigger one on the last day."

The man put the twenty marks back in his pocket, which took him longer than it should have.

"Who do I ask," he said. "Not for a fish dock. For the general run of it. Who in this harbor takes a fouled ring."

"Nobody that I know of, and I know of three, and one of the three is in a lighter at the pile with his pump in it and the other two are not crews any more. The harbor authority's own tide gang is off again on the first of April, and the authority's money went on four sets of moorings and a season of warp in January and there is nothing in it, and the reeve will tell you so himself if you ask him. And I would take it if it were mine and I would not take the child's money to do it, and I am telling you both of those because you walked up here before the market on a Friday and I owe you a whole answer."

The man went down the hill without a word and the coin went with him, and the boy entered the whole of it under Friday the thirty-first with the hour on it — the twenty marks, the six hours of flood, the second seam, the refusal, and the cause, and the cause in the first column, because a man on this dock can check it by asking the man who came up the hill.

---

Kest came down the fish dock at about ten in the morning on foot, with the clerk a good deal behind him carrying the ledger, and it was the first time in four months that the reeve of Morrow Quay had been inside the yard's shed, and there is one chair in this yard and it is a bench, and a man of forty-four sat on a boat's chest with his hands on his knees.

"Two things and then I will let you get on with it," he said, "and neither of them is in a box, and the clerk has brought the book up the hill to do them in and not for any other reason."

"Go on."

"The first is a statement in my own hand and it is dated today and it goes on the wall of this shed and into this office's ledger and into a file I do not control, and it says that the one-season provisional working charter granted on Monday the twenty-eighth of November runs to the end of today, the thirty-first of March, and does not extend into April; that this office cannot renew it and holds nothing that renews it; that whatever comes out of Veyr upon the subject of a second season is a date this office neither controls nor has been told of; and that on the day this instrument ends the holder is a man with a book, a wall and a pump, and no paper." He put his own hand flat on his own knee. "I have written it eleven times in five days and made it shorter eleven times, and there is a version in a drawer upstairs that says *I regret*, and I have burnt it, because a reeve who regrets a thing that has not happened is a man asking somebody else to feel it for him."

He read it out and the clerk entered it and the boy read it back, and Calder Marr read it back after the boy, and Alden Marr was at the far bench and was not asked for anything and did not read it and Calder Marr did not go over to him.

"The second thing is not for the wall," Kest said, "and it is the reason I came up the hill instead of sending a man with a boat's chest, and it is one sentence. The ledger is the record and the wall is a convenience, and your market wall carries eight things tonight with a naval paper nailed over one of them and a four-inch gap, and the count of the copies is eleven, and two offices hold a different figure in their own type, one of them for a fortnight and the other since December. I have entered all of it: the vessel's slate, your two dates, your eighth thing on the wall, and the paper of the sixteenth, where the circuit office corrected a count off one of mine in a day. Nothing has been struck out. I have never had a file with my own hand in it in that many places, and the man who sent those figures up to that office is me, and it cost this office something to send them, and I sent them anyway, and I will not sit in a room in Veyr and be the only fool in it."

"Why now," Nessa Pike said. "You could have sent it in January."

"Because in January I did not know what your record was going to be used for," Kest said, "and now I have watched a boat go into a lighter over it, a man ask a conservator in a market about an object of October, and a request that wants a book delivered into another city, and I have stopped being able to tell which of those is the ordinary use of a record and which is the other thing, and a reeve who cannot tell has to write the difference down before somebody else does it for him."

Then he got up off the boat's chest and looked at the stove, which had not been lit, and said, "That is all of it. I have a barge at the mole at eleven and three men short from the first of April and a north berth that has been out of service since the first of December and is still out, and the fleet will want the third lay and the third lay fills. Marr — I have spent this office's authority four times since the twelfth of January and every one of the four cost somebody who was not your yard a berth, a rope, or a hearing. That is finished. I have nothing left to spend on you and nothing coming, and if a second season comes it will come out of a city and this office will read it with everybody else. You can stop watching me for a favor. I have not got one."

---

At one in the afternoon the chandler came down the hill past the market with the page under his arm and a paper twist in his coat, and nobody had sent for him and he had sent no word, and he stood in the doorway of the shed and said, without being asked, that nineteen marks of iron had gone onto that page on Wednesday the eighth and that the page was at twenty-seven, and asked what the yard's count of the weeks was.

"Thirteen. It has been thirteen since the eighteenth of November and it is a figure in my head and there is no book in this coast that it is in."

"Twenty-two," said the chandler. "And it has been twenty-two since I said it to you in January, and the last figure I ever wrote in that page says eleven, and neither of us has moved his number in twelve weeks and the difference between us is nine weeks of bread. I am not going to take either one down."

"So I am going to say the thing I have wanted to say since November and I am going to say it once. If you asked me for a fortnight I would write one on that page in my own hand and ask you for nothing, and in April there would be a man in this harbor who could say the Free Keel yard came to its creditor and asked. And you have not come. And every week you do not come is a week that nobody in this town can say that about you, and that is worth more to you than the fortnight, and I am not going to explain it twice."

"I am refusing it, and going to keep refusing it, and I would like to say the reason out loud, here, at the head of this dock, and not in a road, because it is not good enough for a road. I do not know what the first of April is. A yard that goes to its creditor with no date in its hand is a yard that has admitted it has nothing, and I have five marks and a pump and no instrument, and I am not going to be told that in my own handwriting before a man in a city has finished reading a file. So the note stays at twenty-seven and I pay it in the ordinary way out of an ordinary week, and the first ordinary week is the week after whatever happens, and if there is not one then it is a long road, and you get my road and not my fortnight."

The chandler said nothing else about it, put the page back inside his coat, took the paper twist out of the other pocket and set it on the bench, would not take a shilling for it, and went back up the hill. It was a quarter of pound of candles and his shop had a rule about the last day of March.

---

The money was read out at four in the afternoon on Friday the thirty-first of March and it was the last column of the winter and there were five people in that shed and the boy read it twice and then read the position, which is the thing he does.

"Gross in the last two weeks of March, fourteen, on the twenty-first, from Harl Dace of the *Grey Widow*, for half a day on a shackle of his own at the first seam on his own account, and the pump hired and not lent. The eighth two, the share two, air two, and a hand two. The yard's is six."

"The bag was eleven on the first of March at seven in the morning and it is five this afternoon, and here is every mark of it in one column because you should be able to check me. Air two on the third, the counter's fee three on the sixth for the ground, the pump's iron five on the eighth, a fortnight's bread, oil and candles four on the fourteenth off this bag and onto the note, six in from the twenty-first, and a fortnight's on the twenty-eighth at three, off this bag and onto the note. Five.

"And the note. It was at one mark on the first of March and it is at twenty-seven this afternoon, nineteen of that being the iron on the eighth. And the three counts of the weeks upon it are all exactly what they were on the fourth of January, and two of those three numbers were said out loud in this shed an hour ago, and the third is in no book in this coast at all.

"And the tin has two crowns in it that went in on the first of February and have not been spent, and I am putting that in so that a man in a year does not think they were missed."

"Read the owing back," Nessa Pike said, and the boy read it, and it is the part of the book he reads slowest.

"Forty marks to Bevin Rook, sixteen for the reading of the book from the fourteenth of October and twenty-four for the hand from the twenty-ninth, standing whole, not reduced, not asked to be reduced, and not paid. Forty marks to Mara Venn, first after food, unpaid since the fifteenth of October, not paid, not reduced, and not asked for by either of us in a month in which a form cost this harbor a pump. Eight marks and a day of a boat to the harbor's bellwright, entered under a rule on Monday the twenty-seventh of February and not paid, and it will be eight again in a year, and the point of it is that it is the same every time. and eight shillings and tenpence of a true third of twenty marks in the name of Sella Brigg, entered on the sixth of February beside the six marks that were paid, undiminished and unpaid, because the yard would rather owe a woman eight shillings and tenpence than have a round number in a book that a clerk could not check.

"And the wage. Four marks a week from the first of February. Four weeks paid, the fourth of them a week late and out of the boy's own arithmetic. Four weeks of March have fallen due and none of them is paid, and I am entering them in a column with no adjectives in it because that is the rule, and I have not asked and he has not told me a date."

Sixteen marks of a wage in a column, in a hand that was crushed in October and again in January, in a shed on the fish dock, on the last day of a charter, with nobody in the room arguing.

---

There is not much else that happened on Friday the thirty-first of March, and what there was went in.

The *Kittiwake* is on the hard with her bilge blocked in and no cradle under her and has been since the twenty-eighth of November, and the harbor's carpenters are free now that the fleet is in and have not been to her. The ninth seam's mooring is in service and it is a mooring and not a berth, the north berth is out of service and is the only wall in this harbor that takes a north-easter, and on the last three it took eleven boats' ground tackle off men who could not pay for it, fifty fathoms of winter warp lie on the north wall where ninety were to lie, and forty feet of the harbor authority's own marline is in the sea. Three men of the tide gang went off on the twenty-fourth of February and are off again on the first of April.

A man of the third lay with nine winters of his own went out in a northeast on the twenty-eighth of February and has not come back, and there is not one line about him in the book of this yard except the line that says there is not one line about him. The working boat *Sea Wren* is at the pile under the cutter *Vigil* with her gear and her pump in a lighter and her master's own logbook in a naval file, and a copy of a leaf of this yard's own book is in her master's coat. Nobody has heard from the circuit office since the fourth request of Saturday the eighteenth, and that request stands unanswered in that office and awaits the same, and the yard has answered nothing and refused nothing: the counter fee for a second ground is three marks, the bag was two on the eighteenth, and the ground is not sent. That is a delay, and the delay is free, and the only reason it is free is that nobody's boat is in a lighter over it.

The bell is on the harbor's ground in eight and a half feet off the south end, and its tongue is half worn through, and the going out costs eight marks and a day of a boat, and those eight marks are in the column above and are not paid.

At about half past four the harbor's tide bell went in the road, three strokes, and the water off the south end gave it back a hand's width of a second later, and Calder Marr's attention went out of the room for about a second and then came back into it, and nobody at that bench said anything about it, and the boy did not put a pencil on the page, and the slate with the heading and the six paragraphs and the one false line upon it was on the shelf where it has been since the fourteenth of October, and it was not moved, and the line is still false, and two words would make it true, and a true slate with a boy's name in it is a document in a yard any man may read.

The book was shut at about five and the pencil went on top of it, and the eighth thing on the market wall was thirteen days old, and there is a copy of it in a hut at the pile and a copy of it in a book in a city in the west that says the party holds seven copies it has been asked for and has not given up, and the ninth thing on that wall would be whatever a man in that city decided to write about them, and he could have that for nothing.

And the last day of a one-season provisional working charter in Morrow Quay was Friday the thirty-first of March, and it ended at midnight the way a date ends, and the first of April is a Saturday, and a second season is a date that comes back out of a city by somebody else's hand or does not come back at all, and the yard had five marks in a bag, twenty-seven on a note, and two crowns in a tin, and a book with a boy's hand in it that nobody outside this shed could read.
