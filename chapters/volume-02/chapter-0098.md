# Chapter 98

*The Second Column of a Bell*

He went up the hill at half past twelve on Monday the first of May with the writ in a sealed jacket under his arm, and he was not going up the hill to ask a man whether his name was on a piece of paper because that is a thing a boy does, and a boy with a hand that has been going since the fourth of April cannot hold a page either, and Calder Marr had watched that hand go for twenty-eight days and had said nothing to anybody about it, and on Monday the first of May he said it out loud in a room in front of a man from a circuit office, and it was the fourth name on the writ and the first sentence he had ever spoken in that room that was about himself.

Tam Slee was in the net loft on the Monday with a coil of his own warp in his hands and two men in there with him, and Calder Marr put the jacket on the bench and took the writ out of it and read him the whole of it, all of it, including the clause, and Slee listened with his head on one side and did not interrupt him once, which took about four minutes.

"That is a leash," Slee said, "and it has got my name on the fourth line and not the third, and the third one is a boy, and whoever wrote that sheet knows something."

"The fourth name is not the one with the most experience in the water. Read that again and it says nothing of the kind."

"I am content to have it," Slee said. "Write that I am content to have it, and put the hour, and I will not have the yard's word for it. I am content to have it. That is all." He put the coil down. "And I will tell you why in ten words, because you will not believe me otherwise, and you have earned the ten words. It is not a chain and it is not money. It is that a man in a boat is the only kind of man anybody hires, and for nine years I have been hired because somebody wanted a man in a boat, and this is the first time anybody has written down that I am one, and if I ever want to stop being one there will be a page that says I was, and that is worth a night's wage to a man of forty-one."

Three men read that back — the boy, the yard, and Dace, who had come up the hill at one o'clock and had not been asked — and the fourth read-back was the yard's own, and the yard read his own words after three other men and then put the pencil on the book, and it is the first time in the whole of this yard's book that the holder has read back after his own crew.

---

Corvin Sarn came down the hill at about two and the writ was on the bench and the jacket was on the bench and the eight marks were in his coat and he wanted to see the paper, and he read the first three lines of it and put it down.

"That is a better piece of paper than anything I have ever been handed," he said, "and it has cost that mooring, and I heard the reeve say so in a room this morning because somebody told me in a market on Saturday, and the man who told me said the reeve said it out loud, and the reeve did, and I am not going to say one word about the mooring because I have no trade in it.

"Now. You want me to say what is on the front of that slate, and you want it in a book with two columns, and I have read one of them in my own hands in a room and I know what your two columns are for, so here is the whole of it and I am going to keep it in this order and if I get a thing wrong you will put it in the second column and not in the first."

He put a finger on the board.

"**Checkable by any man who can get off a mole in this harbor.** The water is eight and a half feet at eight and a quarter past six on the twenty-eighth of April, off the mole's own root, and there is a pole with a mark cut in it at the same height from the seventeenth of February, and it is a fortnight since anybody looked at that pole and it is the one thing in this harbor that has been left alone on purpose. The fouling is there and it is about the same as it was, and about the same is not a figure and I will not give you one. The mass of it is what it was, and you can see the marks in it where the flood has been at it. The ground line is where it was. The tongue is not on the frame.

"Not checkable, and I am going to say why as I go. **What the water has been doing to that thing for three months and a half**, and that is not a figure a man can take off a pole, and I have been in this harbor since the twentieth of October and I have watched every bellwright in this coast get that wrong in a shed, which is that they write down what the water has done as though it were a date. And **the scour on the seaward face is different from what it was on the seventeenth of February, and I cannot tell you whether it is different better or different worse, and I am not going to guess in a shed with four men in it.** In February there was a pale scour about a hand's width on the seaward face from a foot above the water to the fouling, and there is a scour about a hand's width on the seaward face now and the water off the south end is different this year, and there is no instrument in this harbor that will tell me why, and I am not going to stand in a doorway and tell you the reason the ground is safe, because the whole of the craft is the going out and the going out is tomorrow and the ground is not a thing anybody can certify in a shed.

"And then there is what is actually wrong with it, and it is one line and it is a bad line and I have been carrying it about since Saturday.

"**The tongue is not half worn through. The tongue is two parts of a chain in a lead plug.** Somebody has put a piece of chain in a lead plug and hung it in the frame of a working bell so that it strikes, and it has been striking on that for a very long time, and the frame is cut in two places where the chain has been swinging against it, and the cut goes right through the casting on one side of it. And I am not going to make a repair out of that and I am not going to make a finding out of that and I am not going to put my name under it, and I have said it out loud in a shed and if it goes in a book it goes in the second column with a reason and the reason is that no man in this yard has ever been in a bell and none of them is going to be, and one man was, on Tuesday the twenty-seventh of February, and he is not going to explain it to me because nobody has asked him and nobody is going to.

"That is the whole of me and it is eleven sentences and it took me four years to be able to say the middle one and I have said it in a shed and not in a room and I will not say it again until somebody asks me in a room, and if nobody does then that is a craft and not a trade, and that is what my father said."

**Calder Marr did not ask him.** He wanted to, in a way that was physical, and he came within about four inches of it, and the boy was on the bench with the pencil and had not written anything, and a shipwright who has been in that yard for four months said from the door, in about eleven words, that the holder had asked four people nothing this month and had been told so once, and Calder Marr said that was the second time in five months and it was the truth both times.

"That is in the second column and it goes in with a reason," Nessa Pike said, "and the reason is that the party has not asked and will not, and that the party came within four inches of asking on Monday the first of May and did not, and that a man of fifty with a blacksmith's forearms has now said in a shed that he has an eleven-sentence answer to a question nobody in this yard has put to him, and that whoever wants it can go and put it."

"It is in the first column as well," said the boy, who had not been asked. "I am not going to write down that a man did not say a thing. I am going to write down that a man said a thing and the yard did not ask him what it was, and the reason is that the yard has not asked, and both of those are checkable by asking anybody in this room in four years."

---

The second column was read back at about four o'clock on the Monday afternoon, on the mole's steps and then in the shed, in the yard's own book, in Calder Marr's own left hand, with the writ lying on the bench where anybody in the room could put a hand on it, and there were four men and one of them was the holder of the writ and the yard had asked for the fifth and could not get him.

*First column, checkable by any man who can get off a mole in this harbor: the water, eight and a half feet by the pole at eight and a quarter past six on the twenty-eighth and again on the first, the pole being the one with the mark cut in it on the seventeenth of February and not looked at since; the fouling, being there and about the same, which is not a figure; the mass of it, and the marks the flood has left in it; the ground line; the absence of a tongue on the frame; the name of the harbor's own bellwright, being Corvin Sarn, and that he is not of this party and is not of the Free Keel and has not been asked a question by this party; and that the going out of the second of May is bought, and that the bellwright has read a writ with a clause on it and put it down and said it was a better piece of paper than anything he has ever been handed.*

*Second column, not checkable from a mole: what the water has been doing to that thing for three months and a half, and that this is not a figure any man can take off a pole; that the scour on the seaward face is not what it was in February and that the man who knows is a man of fifty and cannot say whether it is different better or different worse; that the frame is cut through the casting on one side where something has been swinging against it for a very long time, and that the party is not going to describe what that something is, because one man saw it and one man has not and one man has been asked nothing and will not ask; that the yard has not asked and came within four inches of asking on Monday the first of May and did not; and that the whole of the craft is the going out, and that the going out is a person, in a boat, in the weather, and that the price of a person in a boat in the weather is eight marks and a day of that boat, and that this yard has not had that eight marks out of its own work in six weeks and got it on Saturday the twenty-ninth of April out of a market in four hands that were not the yard's and not the bellwright's, and that the bellwright knows the whole of that and has not been thanked for it and will not be.*

Nessa Pike read the first column back and Calder Marr read the second, and the boy read both again and the yard read both a third time, and the fourth man was Harl Dace, who had not been asked and had come anyway, and after he had read it he said one thing that went in the first column because he was in the boat.

"**The going out is tomorrow at the top of the flood and the tide is at half past one and the wind is in the south-west and going into the south, and it is a flat water in this harbor, and in this harbor that means the two of you will be back on these steps by four and I will be in my wheelhouse with a line out of my quarter, and if either of you tells me one word about this at a market before June I will put my hand over your mouth in front of forty men.** That is a man's own water going into a book and the reason is that a man in a boat can be asked about the water, and every one of you would rather have this in a column than in a conversation."

And then the thing that made the afternoon worth the walk down the hill, and it was not said by a man in a trade at all.

Sarn had a lead plug in the pocket of his coat about the size of a thumb and a half, and he put it on the bench and nobody touched it for a minute.

"That came out of it on Tuesday the twenty-seventh of February," he said. "It is the plug the chain was let in with, or most of it. It has a piece of chain in it and it has a hole in it and the hole is not a keyhole, it is a half-inch drilled and it is a clean hole and it has been made by a man with a breast drill and a steady arm and he did it to a piece of lead about eleven inches under a bell at eight and a half feet in a stone mole in a working harbor. I have had it in my coat since February. It has a number in it — a little bit of a number, a two and a bit of another one, and there is a fourth one that is not a two and that is how I know it is a number and not a price. **I am not going to tell you what I think the number is and I am not going to let anybody in this shed write it on a slate.** I have carried it four months because I am a man who keeps tools, and I am telling you now because I have said the other thing out loud and a man who has said the other thing out loud does not get to keep the first thing."

Nobody in that shed said a word for about eleven seconds.

"Take it out of this room," Calder Marr said. "Take it to your own bench and put it in your own tin and do not tell me where and do not tell me what the number is, and I am not going to ask you."

"I am not taking it out of the room," Sarn said, "and I know what I am doing and I would like it entered that I did it on purpose. It stays on this bench until every man in this shed has described it in his own words, and then it goes in the chest, and it is in the second column and not the first, because no man in this yard has seen it but him and none of them is going to, and a yard that keeps a thing in a drawer it has not got a figure for is a yard that has lost it twice."

They described it. The boy wrote it and it took four men four descriptions and four reasons and the four descriptions did not agree and the boy put all four in and read all four back, and the entry ends with the reason, in the yard's own hand, and it is the shortest line in that book.

*"That a thing in this yard's book that this yard has not got is worth more to it than the same thing in a drawer, and that the party did not find it and cannot describe it and will not be asked, and that the bellwright is keeping it and the bellwright is not of this party."*

It went into the chest at about half past four and the chest has a lock and the lock is the yard's and the boy wrote on the lid in his own right hand that there is nothing in it that anybody in this harbor can check, and that is true, and it is the second thing in that chest that says so.

---

At about six the boy read the position back and the holder of the writ was in the room and did not read it and let him do it, and it was the first time since the fourteenth of October.

"Seven marks in a bag. The note is at twenty-three, and it came down six on Thursday the twenty-ninth in the creditor's own hand out of a market, and it came down a further four on Friday the thirtieth, and both of those were paid by men who were not this yard, and the four of Friday went straight onto the page and never came into the bag because the creditor was standing in the market with a cart. Nothing in a tin. Four lines and sixteen marks in the wage column and the fifth of March still in it and the oldest. Forty marks of claim standing whole. Forty to a conservator with no office, standing, and she is not of that writ and the writ cannot reach her. Eight shillings and tenpence to Sella Brigg standing and undiminished. Twenty marks of unsold iron on the yard's own ground with a saw kerf in a crown shackle. A compressor gland to buy, a hand lead's line to make, and a delivery hose that does not exist in this harbor. And a writ on this bench that runs to the first of March next with a reporting clause on it and four names on it and one of them is a man of forty-one who said he was content to have it and did not have to be asked twice."

"A hand lead's line by Thursday," Nessa Pike said, "and I have the flax, and you will do it yourself because there is nobody else in this yard who can do it and I am not going to pretend otherwise in a book."

And the glass had been falling for twenty-eight days and had not made up into a rain, and it came round into the south-west about two o'clock in the afternoon, and the tide was at half past one on Monday the first of May and the going out was at the top of the flood on Tuesday the second, and the man who had the bell was fifty and had said twelve sentences into a book in a shed and had not answered one question, and the notice of the thirty-first of October stands whole and is not lifted, and the ninth seam's mooring is out of service from the day the writ was granted, and a man of sixty-one was at the far bench at six o'clock with his back to the two of them and was not asked for anything, and Calder Marr did not go over to him.
