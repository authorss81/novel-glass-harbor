# Chapter 60

*Under the Town*

The reason went into the harbor authority's book at eight o'clock on Thursday the nineteenth of January, at the reeve's table, in Calder Marr's own left hand, unedited, with the clerk present, and it is the two hundred and sixty-nine words out of the crew's book and not one word more, and the clerk read it back and Kest read it back and stopped him at the third paragraph.

"Stop there. The bell is hung, the boat is hired, the pump is the harbor's, and the notice of the thirty-first is on the wall and is not mine and is not being argued this morning." The reeve put his own hand flat on the page. "And I am going to write one line under it and the line is that this office has named the ground and the date and the top of the flood, and that the reason names a hand lead and a slate and does not name a man going in, and that this office does not require it to name one, and that if a man does go in, the reason will be re-entered before he does.

"And I am going to write a second line under that one, in my own hand, and it is short. The first clause of the notice of the thirty-first closes the ground I named on Tuesday, to all work and all approach by all persons, including this office. I named it and I knew what I was doing, and the party has not asked for the notice to be lifted, and this office has not lifted it and cannot. So a man in Veyr in four years is going to find two entries in one book in which this harbor authority put itself inside a closure created by a paper it did not write, and I would rather he found them from me in a ledger than from a clerk's slip in a net loft."

"That is not in your own words in the book of the twenty-eighth of November," Calder Marr said.

"No," Kest said. "It is in a better form than the one of the twenty-eighth, and I have written it in a better form on purpose, and I would like the room to notice that a man who is not swimming can write a paper about swimming very much more clearly than the swimmer could."

They read it back a fourth time and the clerk stamped nothing, because nothing about it is stampable, and the harbor's clerk put the hour down in the authority's own book because the reeve told him to and did not tell him why.

---

The top of the flood on the nineteenth of January was at a quarter to ten, and the harbor's crane and the harbor's barge came out at eight because the ground is the harbor's ground and the harbor pays their hire out of the authority's own money, and the harbor's clerk came out in the boat with them because the reeve had sent for him, and the harbor's tide gang stood on the mole at the third and fourth seams in two places in the way they had stood on the sixth of December and on the twelfth of January, which was the last time they had stood on that ground at all, and the reeve's man stood with them, and the naval tender was a cable's length off the mole with her floats in and out, and nobody from the *Vigil* came ashore.

Tobias Wren had the boat. Bevin Rook had the book and the slate and the lead line and his left arm across his chest in a sling, and his right hand on the lead, and the wrist on the other one was seven days old with a bonesetter's mark on it, and a bonesetter's sentence in a book on the fish dock said *a boat hand in June*, and it was not a promise and it was not a lie and it was not his sentence to say out loud, and he had his hand in the bottom of his coat because it was in the bottom of his coat.

Nessa Pike had the board and the cleat and no reason to be told anything, because there was no diver.

The slop went to a sheet of glare the way it goes in this harbor, and the mole wall stopped being a wall at the south end and became a slope, and the slope went into about nine feet of water and kept going, and two boat lengths off the south end the core was on its ground line in eight and a half feet with its fouled waist and its tongue half worn through, and the bell was there because it is always there, and nobody had done anything to it since the eleventh except the weather.

They worked the figure first and the figure was where it had been since the twenty-eighth of October, because it is a bedded seam of about forty feet in the mole's own foundation and the south end of it is the position and the position is where the boat lies.

"South end, nine," Bevin Rook said, reading off the slate. "That is the tender's count of the twenty-eighth of October, off the mole's toe at the third seam, in a boat on the outer side, and it is the figure in the hearing record and it is in a hand that is not mine, and my own count of the same water is seven. I am not going to improve either of them because we have had a better week."

They took the second figure forty feet north along the bearing, off the same line, at the north end of the bedded material, and it was ten and a half, which is the tender's count of the same seam at its north end. The boy said, without being asked, that his own count of it in October was eleven, and that a diver may say his own number out loud and it only goes in if a man on a mole can check it, and a man on a mole can check ten and a half and cannot check eleven, and that was the rule then and it is the rule now. The third figure, another forty feet north, in the edge of the water under the mole's root, was seven and three-eighths, and the soundings were ordinary, and the whole of the work for the first hour was a lead line and a slate and a boy with a good ear for a bottom that was coming up.

And then Bevin Rook put the lead over where the seam came out from under the mole's root and read a figure and did not write it, and read it again, and Calder Marr took the slate and looked at what the boy had written, which was one figure, and the figure was five and a quarter.

"Again," Calder said.

The boy sounded it four times. Five and a quarter, five and a quarter, five and a half, five and a quarter.

"Landward," Nessa Pike said.

"Landward," Bevin Rook said, in the flat voice, and he had the lead in his right hand and the line in his right hand and his whole body swung a foot to the north without moving his feet, which is what a man does when a bottom comes up under him and the water is over it and the figure says the shore is in front of the lead. "The bottom is coming up toward the mole and it is coming up at about a foot in thirty, and if you go on with this bearing for another sixty feet you are in two fathoms of water over a stone quay, and there is no chart in this harbor with that on it, and there is no chart in this harbor with a half of it on it, and the reason is not that the chart is old."

The tide bell went at four minutes past ten, three strokes, in the road.

Under the third stroke, out of the water off the south end, a hand's width of a second later, the answer came up, and it was low, and it was even, and it was the note, three marks and a flick, and it stopped when the bell stopped, and there was no call in it.

"Ten," Bevin Rook said, without being asked, and his right hand was on the page. "Ten logged since the twenty-sixth of October, and the span is from the twenty-sixth of October, and the water is at the top of the flood with a light air off the north, and the harbor's three went at four minutes past ten, and the answer is out of the core a hand's width of a second under the third, and the second is the same second as the eleventh and the sixth of December and the twenty-sixth of December, and there is no source and it is not written as a voice, and the answer was the note and there was no call in it, and that is the second time running and I am entering the second time running because it is a fact and not a question, and nobody in this boat has asked me a question about it and I have not got one."

Nobody asked him a question about it.

"Read it back," Nessa Pike said.

He read it back, twice, and then Calder Marr read it back after him, and that is three times, and the rule is that four men read the same day it is written, and the fourth was on the mole with the tide gang and had a slate of his own and wrote the hour on it and the depth and the answer and did not ask what any of it was, and the harbor's clerk wrote both of them in the authority's own book with the hour on it, and the reeve's man stood and looked at the water and did not say one word for four minutes, and afterward said that it was the longest four minutes he had stood on a mole in fifteen years, and that he had not understood one word of it and would not have wanted to.

---

They stopped the day's work at twenty past eleven, and it was Calder Marr who stopped it, and he stopped it at a figure that is not the figure the yard went out for.

"Twenty past eleven," he said. "We have four hundred feet of it on the slate and the run does not stop at the mole. That is all. I am calling the day."

"You have a tide until half past twelve and a lead and a slate and a boy with a good ear," Nessa Pike said, "and you are calling it at twenty past eleven."

"Yes."

"Why."

"Because a route that no paper has ever been written for cannot be asked about in a hearing room," Calder Marr said, "and a man who cannot ask is a man with no instrument, and I have been standing in this boat for two hours finding out that everything we have done since the eighth of October has been done under something with a name on it — a claim window, a work permit, a charter, a reason in a book, a notice with four clauses and a seal on the nail — and every one of those things was written by somebody, and a thing that is written can be read by a man in an office in four years, and this is not written. It is under a town. And the office that would have to rule on it has no chart of it, because the town was built on it, and because nobody in this harbor has ever wanted one, and there is no form in this harbor or in Veyr for a man to put a run under a town into.

"So we are going to go back up the steps at twenty past eleven with four hundred feet of figures in a slate, and the figures are good, and the figures are ours, and they are in the crew's book and in the authority's book and on the wall tonight, and the office in the west of this city has three items in it under three numbers and the reason for the seizure of the fourteenth of October is on no paper I have ever been handed, and this is one more thing I cannot put anywhere. And I am not going to be the man who took a bellwright's day and a hired line and a borrowed pump and put a boy on a mole at a figure nobody has checked, because that is exactly the thing I wrote three reasons against in this yard on the third of November, at two in the morning, in a punt with a lamp, and I have not got a better reason today."

Bevin Rook wrote it down in the book while Calder Marr was saying it, which he was not supposed to do, and then he read it back, twice, and then he read it back a third time, and then he said the thing that Calder Marr had not thought of and could not have thought of in twenty minutes.

"Entered, the nineteenth of January, and I want it in the same entry and in your hand as well as mine: *that the day's work was stopped by the holder at twenty past eleven, of his own motion, and that the holder states that the ground of the stopping is that the run does not stop at the mole and that there is no instrument in this harbor or in Veyr to which a run under a town can be put, and that the work will not be done again until somebody is found who can be asked.*

"And I am the man who wrote that and I am a boy of twenty-two with a hand that will not close, and I have been in this yard five months, and I would like it in the record that the man who called the day is the man who does not get to be the man who goes on. That is what a reason is for. That is the whole reason for a reason."

---

The core was hung and sounded and the delay was read off it and it is now a figure in two books and on a wall, and it is a hand's width of a second, and it belongs to the yard as well as to the harbor, and a bell hung on that ground and a bell struck in the road are standing in the same ground, and that is the only account anybody has of why it is late.

The day was paid as a day. The charter was used and the eighth and the share came off anything raised and nothing was raised and nothing was going to be raised and the day is entered in the authority's own book as a day of the holder's time on this office's ground at the rate of eighteen marks for a crew of three and a boat, out of this office's own account, and the authority's bills in that quarter go out on the first of the month, and the sum will be in the yard's hands on the first of February and not before, and Bevin Rook read that line back twice and did not say a word about it and wrote the date against it, and the bag was four marks on the eighteenth and it is four marks tonight and the tin has been empty since the eighteenth of November.

And the first of March is a Wednesday, and it is in six weeks less a day from that boat, and there are forty herring boats coming in, and the north berth is out of service and is the one that takes a northeast blow, and the ninth seam's mooring has been bought back with this office's own money and is not yet worked, and the coast office in Veyr has still not had an answer, and the only answer it has asked for is a statement of the capacity of a harbor to moor a fleet, which a herring skipper has offered to write for nothing and which the harbor authority has not written, and the notice of the thirty-first of October is on a market wall with a circuit's seal over the nail and is not this office's to lift, and the reeve who may suspend a one-season provisional working charter after an inspection, for cause, by himself, with a key and a book, said out loud on the twenty-sixth of December that he has no cause and will not, and has said nothing since, and has not been asked.

And a one-season provisional working charter is not a permission to work whenever the holder feels like it, and the thing the yard found on the nineteenth of January at twenty past eleven in the morning is a thing that cannot be entered in any instrument this office has, and there is no form for a man to put a run under a town into, and there is no forum that will rule on one, and the leash that was on this yard in November was a permit and a defense and a date, and the leash that is on it tonight is longer and it has a name on it, and what comes out of it is a date, and the date is the first of March.

Calder Marr went up the mole steps at half past twelve in the slop with a slate in his hand, and the boy came up behind him with the book, and the boy put the book in front of him on the top step without being asked, and Calder Marr read the whole of the nineteenth out loud in his own left hand, from the eight o'clock reason to the twenty past eleven, and Bevin Rook read it back after him, and the third man read it back after the boy, and the clerk's own clerk wrote the hour in the authority's book because the reeve told him to and did not tell him why, and down in the road the tide was making on the mole face and the bell was answering the tide bell a hand's width of a second late, and no man in Morrow Quay and no man in Veyr could say what was in the ground under the town, and the harbor authority had no chart of it, and the town was built on it.
