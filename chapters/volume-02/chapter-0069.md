# Chapter 69

*Forty Feet of Warp*

Edric Pringle came down the north wall at seven on Tuesday the twenty-eighth of February with the *Anna Beth*'s ring gone and about forty feet of her warp gone with it, and she was fifty-four and had a mouth on her that three men in a market had tried and one man in a harbor authority had not tried, and she had been difficult about nine marks on the twenty-sixth of January in a shed and had paid them on the Friday.

She put her hand flat on the wall at the head of it, about where the chalk was, and said:

"Here. And here is the thing I am going to say and I would like it said first by you and not by me, because I have been up the hill since five and I have had time to think about whose fault it is and I have decided that I do not care whose fault it is, only that you say it out loud before anybody else does."

"It is our shackle," Calder Marr said.

"It is your shackle in a stone post that never had a shackle in it, in a wall we worked yesterday, and it has moved the run of the water at that head, and I have got a ring in the sea and forty feet of warp and a boat that is not insured in the way a man in this harbor would want and the mutual account will give me a crate of fish because the mutual account is what it is. Fourteen marks and the crate. That is the whole of what I have and I am not going to argue it down and I am not going to be generous about it either, because I have a fleet coming in tomorrow and I have two boats short of warps now instead of one."

"Twenty-six yesterday for ninety fathoms," Nessa Pike said.

"Fourteen today for a ring," Pringle said. "I have read the figure on your wall and I have got a hand with a bad pencil and I did the sum on the way down. You will get the same on a good day and less on a bad one and that is the trade, and I have been in this trade forty years and I would like the four words said out loud in a book that I did it, not that the wind did."

"Enter it," Nessa Pike said. "In his words, in his own hand, the figure, the crate, the cause, and that the party is not disputing the cause. And Calder, say the fourth thing out loud and do not let me say it for you, because it is yours."

"I put a shackle through a stone post that was never meant to have a shackle in it, because this office had no better iron, and the wall's run is changed at the head, and the ring went with the new run," Calder Marr said. "And the office's iron and the office's fault and the office's wall, and I asked for better iron on Thursday and was told in the reeve's own barge that there was none and that this office was not going to buy iron in the last week of February with a fleet coming in, and I accepted that and I worked on it anyway, and both of those are mine."

"Then we are two men who did the same thing," Pringle said, "and I have been up the hill since five and I would like a crate of fish for the fleet that goes into the first of March at eight o'clock and not at some other time, and I would like a man with a book to write the hour on it, because in eleven weeks somebody is going to tell the fleet that the fish came at noon."

---

The funding was done before anybody touched a boat, and it took twenty minutes, and it is in the yard's book in the boy's own right hand with the hour on it, and it is the shortest money entry of the winter because there was very little in it.

Fourteen gross from Edric Pringle, on his own ring and his own warp and his own account, at the north wall, landward of the third seam, with no diver and no permit, which is not a favor and not a breach, because the notice of the thirty-first closes the mole's toe and inner face from the third seam to the eleventh and does not reach a herring skipper's own ring on the north wall, and no notice has ever closed water.

The harbor's eighth, two. The harbor's share, two. Air, two. Rope, a mark. A hand, two marks, the second day of Wend Crale, engaged on the twenty-seventh and paid on the twenty-eighth out of the day he worked.

"Enter the crate of fish as a thing and not as a figure," Nessa Pike said, "because it is not money and it is going into three men's mouths for a fortnight and it is the reason the fortnight's eating is four this week and not five, and I want that in the book in the same line as the figure it is the reason for, so that in a month when somebody asks why the yard ate less nobody has to guess."

Crale had a broken finger on the twenty-seventh and had not said so. He said so at eight in the morning on the twenty-eighth, when Calder Marr asked him, in about as many words, and then would not go on the water, and his second day was paid at two marks and entered as paid, and Nessa Pike entered the fact that a hired hand told the yard at eight o'clock in the morning that he was not fit and was not asked to be fit and did not have to be, and that the yard took the two marks off him for a day he did not do and gave him the crate of fish's half share in it.

"You are not paying me for a day I did not do," Crale said.

"I am paying a man for two days he was hired for," Nessa Pike said, "and you told us at eight, and I am not going to have an argument about it in a boat, and if you do not take the two marks I will write down that a hired hand of this yard worked a day and was paid and it will be a lie and there is one liar in this book already and I know his name and it is not you."

He took the two marks.

---

They found the ring at the fourth call.

The lead went over at the head of the wall on the first sounding and the boy called four foot six, and again on the second and the third, and on the fourth the lead came up and did not come up clean, and the line came tight, and Bevin Rook read it as four foot four and came up heavy and put it in the second column because a man on a stone cannot check what a lead is holding.

It was under a coping stone off the wall's head, a slab of the wall's own stone about the length of a man's arm, that had come off in the earlier blow and had gone down and had taken the ring with it, and the ring was under the slab and the slab was fouled and the slab was not going anywhere on a hand lead and a warp-lead in a half tide.

"Two hours," Nessa Pike said. "Maybe three. And that is with the boat holding a warp-lead and the wall holding a chain, and if the slab moves with the tide we lose it and we lose the ring and we lose the afternoon, and I have got four hours of tide and three of them are any good."

"Call it again," Calder Marr said, "and call the state of the water and call the tide in your own words and do not stop calling it because I have said the same thing twice."

They got the boat warp under the slab at about ten and heaved it on a chain from the wall head with the boat holding the warp-lead and Calder Marr on the chain and a man from the *Anna Beth* on the other end of it, and the slab came at about a quarter to eleven, sideways, and went under the boat's quarter and away, and the ring came with it in the eddy at the wall's foot with about nine feet of warp still bent through the shackle.

The boy called it: "Nineteen hundred and fifty-one. Tide is half past the ebb, the water at the wall's head is two foot four and falling and the float line says the same and I have checked it, and the ring is at the wall's foot in the eddy and it is visible and it is fouled with a mussel and a half on it and the warp is still in the eye."

"Visible is in the second column," Calder Marr said.

"Visible is in the second column and the depth is in the first and the mussel is in the second and I have a man on the wall with a boat hook and he says he can get a bight on it and I have not got a bight on it and I have not got a hand, and I am entering that I said so out loud in front of four people, and Calder, that is not the book and that is a fact."

It took another twenty minutes and the ring came up with a boat hook and a bight of marline and came out of the water with a mussel on it and forty-one feet of warp bent through a shackle that was not the yard's and not the authority's and was Pringle's own, and the warp's eye was there and the splice was good and could have been cut and mended and was cut instead because there is no time on the first of March.

The whole of it was over at two in the afternoon. The boy made four men read it back and the fifth was Edric Pringle, who read it standing on the wall in her own oilskin and said that the first column was right and the second column was honest and that in forty years she had never had a crew hand her a slate.

---

The money went in on the Tuesday night and the position was read out and read back twice, and the book has it in a column.

"Gross, fourteen, from Edric Pringle, of the *Anna Beth*, for one day's work on his own ring and his own warp and his own account, at the north wall, landward of the third seam, with no diver and no permit, and nothing raised.

"The eighth, two. The share, two. Air, two, and the pot is a third gone and the pot is now a third gone and about a quarter of a mark worse than it was in January, which I have worked out and I am entering because a man will ask. Rope, a mark. The hand, two marks, the second day of Wend Crale, and he did not work it and he was paid it and I have entered that he was not fit and told us at eight in the morning and was not asked to be.

"That is nine out of fourteen and the yard's is five, and the bag had thirteen on Monday the twenty-seventh and this morning the fortnight's bread, oil and candles went onto the note at the sign of the Drowned Bell and came out of this bag, three, and the note stands at one, and thirteen less three is ten, and ten and five is fifteen, and there is a crate of fish in the corner of the shed that is the mutual account's and not the yard's and a third of it is this yard's share of it, and the third is going into three men and a half and is the reason the fortnight's eating is four this week instead of five, and I am not putting a figure on the crate because a crate is not a figure."

"Read the last of it back."

He read the last of it back, and then he read the position, which was four marks short of what it had been on the first of February, and the four marks were his own, and the fourth week of a wage at four a week fell due on the twenty-first and had not been paid and was entered as unpaid on the twenty-first in his own right hand, and he read that too, and then he said the thing that had been sitting in that shed since the fourteenth of January.

"The four is coming out of the fifteen on Wednesday morning and I have not asked for it and I am not going to, and Calder is not going to tell me that it is coming, and there is no reason on any paper in this harbor for me to know that except that I have done the sum and the sum is a sum I am allowed to do."

"It is a sum you are allowed to do," Nessa Pike said, "and it is the only wage in this harbor that anybody could work out for themselves, and I would like it noticed that a man who can do his own arithmetic is a man nobody has to lie to about money, and that has cost this yard forty marks and a hand and it is the cheapest thing this yard has ever bought."

The boy shut the book.

Then, from the corner, the boy who could not read said the other thing, and it was the last thing said in that shed on the twenty-eighth of February, and it was not about the ring.

"There is a man called Neave Tarrant in the third lay with nine winters of his own," Piet Marrow said, "and he is out in this tonight and he is not back till Wednesday, and I am not saying it because anything, I am saying it because there are forty boats coming in and one berth and somebody counted forty in the market this morning and somebody else counted forty-one, and I counted them because I cannot read and I can count."

Nobody in that shed said anything, and Calder Marr stood at the wall of the shed with his hand on it for a moment, and then took the lamp down and went up to close the market wall, because there were seven things on it and the eighth was due to be a figure that somebody might chalk on a slate in the morning, and the slate wall in the hearing room was wiped every Monday at eight and there was no sitting called and none likely, and the first of March was a Wednesday and it was tomorrow, and there were forty herring boats coming into a harbor with one berth short, a north berth out of service, fifty fathoms of winter warp on the north wall where ninety belonged, forty feet of marline in the sea, nineteen winters of dues disputed and unpaid, and a coast office in Veyr that had answered a letter with the word *capacity* and had still not had the answer it asked for.
