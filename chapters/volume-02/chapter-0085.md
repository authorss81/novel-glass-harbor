# Chapter 85

*The One Question*

He went on the Sunday because it was the only day of that week on which a fisher at the top of Coombe Row was certain to be in, and he went alone because the question was not the crew's, and Nessa Pike said so at the top of the hill in about forty words.

"You are going on your own and you are going to ask a thing that is in your head and not in the book, and if she says no then there is nothing to write, and if she says nothing then there is less."

"That is the whole of why I am going on my own."

"The other whole of why you are going on your own is that you have asked four people nothing this month and a reeve and a clerk twice," she said, "and I would not have said it and I have said it, so put nothing in the book about it and remember it."

---

The house was the second from the corner and the door was low and there was a smell of woodsmoke and tea coming under it, and an old woman opened it before he had finished knocking, which meant she had heard his boots.

"You are the diver," she said. "The one on the fish dock with a hand that does not close and a book that does."

"Sera Mott, I am Calder Marr, and I am not here about a bell."

"You are not, either, and I have known that since the twenty-first of December, and you have come twice since and asked me nothing at all, and today you have come at three in the afternoon on a Sunday with a thing you have not said out loud yet, and I would like you to say it in the room and not in the road."

He said it in the room.

"Has there ever been a man who went out in a boat to keep one of them and came back and told you what it was like?"

She did not answer for long enough that he thought about the kettle. She was sixty-eight and had been a fisher and had the hands of one, and she put the kettle on the ring and sat down across the room from him in a chair that had been in a boat and had been repaired once, badly, with a piece of a different boat.

"No," she said, at last. "And that is the whole of my answer and I am not going to give you any of it twice."

"I did not expect—"

"You expected a story," she said, "and there is not one, and that is not me keeping something back. There has not been one for sixty years and I did not learn to be quiet about it, I learned it, and the learning is the only thing I have got that is worth anything and it is not worth a great deal."

She poured the water and did not drink hers, and he noticed that and thought of another woman two hundred yards down the hill with a cup she has not drunk out of in three months, and he did not say it.

"Now you are going to tell me what you have come with," she said, "because a man does not come to my door on a Sunday in April for a question he could have asked in December, and there is something on you and it has been on you since Tuesday."

So he told her. Not the glass — the glass is not his to tell and it is under a claim window that is not hers. The coast, that a master of the *Grey Widow* had put down in a yard's book on Thursday morning, three marks and a bar and a way in out of weather, and that the harbor authority had no soundings for five miles of that coast, and that the yard was going out on Thursday with a hand lead and a slate to find out how much of it was true.

"And the marks on it are struck with a seal off a boat," he said, "and the man who had the seal put on it has not been able to find out for three months what his boat is allowed to do with it."

"And now you have a trade on a Sunday and it fits," she said, "and you have come here to ask me about men, and you have a coast in your other hand, and you would like me to put them together."

"Yes," Calder Marr said. "I would like you to put them together."

"No," Sera Mott said.

She put her cup down on the table beside the chair and looked at the window for a while, and the light came through it at an angle that made the whole room the color of weak tea, and when she spoke again she spoke carefully, which was not a thing she had been doing.

"I have said that thing before to men with books and I have said no to all of them and I believed every one of my noes and I am not tired of them. An archivist from Veyr came in the autumn of the year before last and was extremely good about it and got nothing, and a man from the coast office in the winter before that was almost clever about it and got nothing, and I have not said it out loud to anybody since. And in December I told a man at a mole head that I would say no one more time in about two years, and it is April, and I am holding to that, and you may put the date on it and not the name."

"Then I will not write the name."

"You will not write the name because I am asking you not to, and I have never asked a man for anything in sixty years and I am not starting because I have had a bad winter, so you may put your own reason against it in your own book and let a man in a city wonder." She turned back to him. "But you are going to write that I was asked and I said no, because the day somebody in a city finds a page with nothing on it about an old woman at the top of Coombe Row is the day somebody in a city decides she was never asked. I have been not asked before and I did not like it."

"I will write that you were asked and you refused, and the name will not be on the page, and you did not have to be asked twice and nobody will ask you again this month."

"Not this month is not this year," she said.

"Not this year," Calder Marr said, and understood while he was saying it that he had just promised a thing on behalf of a crew he did not control, and that the promise was worth exactly what the boy in the shed was worth and would be paid in the same column, and that this was a trade he had learned in five months from people who were worse at it than he was.

Then she said the thing that made the afternoon worth the walk, and she said it to the window.

"You want to put my half and your coast in the same book. You will not do it, and here is why you will not do it, and it is not because I am protecting myself, it is because the two are not the same kind of thing and a book that has them in it is a book that a man in four years can read either way. Yours is a road of water with three stones in it and a bar of gravel under it, and nobody has ever taken it off anybody, and there is nothing wrong in it. Mine is about men, and men either went out in a boat or they did not, and there is no bar under that and there is no soundings sheet for it and there never will be one, and if you write the two down together then in four years a man will say the road was kept by men, and he will say it because it sounds like a thing a coast ought to be, and it will not be in your book and it will not be in mine and it will be in his, and it will be the first false thing anybody has ever said about either of us."

"A line may not be drawn between them," Calder Marr said, "and I have got a reeve's ruling about three other things that says the same in different words, and I am not going to draw one here, and I will not have a page that can be read either way. You are a person in a house on Coombe Row who was asked a question and refused it, and the coast is a coast, and one of them is going in this book and the other one is going in nobody's."

"That is the first sensible thing anybody has said to me in four years," Sera Mott said, "and you are a diver, and I would think less of you if you had got it out of me."

"I am going to ask you one practical thing and you may say no to it as easily."

"Ask."

"You were on that water before there was a chart office in this harbor," Calder Marr said, "and you have been up and down it since you could stand. Is there anybody you know of who has been out on that coast inside ten years — not you, and not a man who has only heard of it from you."

She looked at him for a while with the cup in both hands.

"I know of two," she said. "One of them is fifty-eight and he is in the south lay and he will tell you that coast is a tale and that you are wasting a morning, and he will say it in a market, in front of people, because that is how he has kept his name for forty years. The other one is thirty-one and he is a second-lay man with a boat with a bad wheel and he will not tell you anything at all without you sounding it first, and if you sound it first and it is true he will say so out loud in a market in front of anybody, and he does not care who is standing there."

"Then that is two answers and I have not paid you for either of them."

"You did not pay me for the first one and I gave you the second one for nothing, and that is the trade," she said, "and it is the oldest one on this coast and it is the one that everybody in your line of work has been refusing for five months. Now go away, and shut the door, because the glass has been falling since Friday and there is a woman of my age in this house with one stove."

---

He went down the hill at half past four and the yard was shut and the pump stood on its cart with the hose coiled, and the tenth week of the wage fell due on the Tuesday and would be entered in a column with no adjectives in it, and the bag was ten and the note was twenty-seven and the tin had two crowns in it, and the glass had been falling for six days and had not made up into a rain.

The boy had the book out and the pencil, and Calder Marr told him what had happened in about nine sentences, and gave him the rule: not the name, and the words she had used, and her own reason, and the hour.

The boy wrote it and did not ask for the name, and Nessa Pike read it back, and Calder Marr read it after her, and Harl Dace read it last because he was in the shed and had asked on Saturday to be allowed to read the book on any day the yard wrote in it, which was a thing nobody had thought to refuse.

"That is the first line in this book," Dace said, "with a hole in it that somebody asked for."

"It is the first line in this book with a hole in it that is not a hole," Calder Marr said, "and if you cannot tell the difference between those two things after thirty-one years at sea then I do not know what a book is for."

Nobody read it a fourth time, because there were only four men in the shed and one of them was the man who had been refused, and a page about a refusal is not a page that four men have to be answerable for, and the pencil went on top of the book, and outside the fly went along the water with the tide, and the water in the basin was the color of the inside of a bottle and the third lay was full and the mole-head light was not on.
