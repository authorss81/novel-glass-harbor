# Chapter 83

*A Line of Marks and a Bottom*

Harl Dace set the coast down on a fish-dock table at ten in the morning on Thursday the sixth of April with his thumb on the slate, and it took him until about eleven because he would not describe a thing in the order a man would think of it.

"You want it in the order you would go," he said. "I will give it to you in the order I use it, and if you write it down in my order then in a year a man can sail it and not have to unlearn it."

Three marks. The first was a cairn of ballast stones on a low point about five miles north of this harbor's headland, a point the men of that water call the Old Custom House because there was a house on it once and nobody can agree what kind, and the cairn stands eleven feet above the rock and it is a cairn of stones out of a ship and not one man's work, and it dries at half-tide and is black on the top three courses.

The second was timber. Nine fathoms of a ship's stem lying on a sandspit with its head up, the point of it, and the men who work that water call the spit the Nine Foot and the timber the Accountant, and the sand under it is white and hard and there is nine fathoms of water over it at the middle of a spring tide and two and a half on the bar behind it.

The third was a rock. It dries at low water springs, three days out of a month, and it is the size of a good-sized boat lying on her side, and the men who work that water call it the Kettle, and Calder Marr wrote that down and Dace said he would spell it the way it sounds, which is kettle, and that a man who wrote *cauldron* in a book would be a man nobody could use.

"And the bottom," Dace said. "That is the whole of it, and the marks are nothing without it. Inside the headland and north there is a bar that comes up to two and a half fathoms in the middle of a spring, and it is not sand. It is the old run of a gravel beach that was a beach when the water was lower than it is, and it goes across the mouth of a road that the water can stand on, and a man can cross it at any state of tide if he knows where, and a man who does not know where can be on it in a flat calm in a westerly and be aground for six hours with the tide over his mast."

"A way in and out of weather," Calder Marr said, "and in your own words, because you said this was the part nobody had ever asked you for."

"The Rooks," Dace said. "There is a creek mouth a mile and a half south of the bar, and it is the only piece of shelter on five miles of that coast that a boat can get into in a northerly, and I have been into it about four hundred times in thirty-one years. You come down on it from the south, outside the point, in the lee of the Old Custom House, on the flood and not on the ebb, because on the ebb the wind gets round the corner of that point and into the creek and you will have it on your beam before you have the anchor down. In a southerly you can go in anywhere along that coast, and in a westerly you cannot go in at all between the headland and the Kettle, and everybody who works that water knows it and nobody has ever put it in a book, because there is no office in this coast that has anything to do with it and there never has been."

"Why this coast," Nessa Pike said.

"Because the line of the three marks runs in and out of weather." Dace took his thumb off the slate. "You take a boat out of this harbor in a north-easter with the fleet, and you come round the headland and you have three choices and two of them are bad. You can stand out to the south-west into the stream and take it, and that is forty miles of open water with nothing on it. You can stand off and go slow, and that is the whole night and a crew's worth of coal. Or you come down inside the headland and you take the bar, and the bar is inside this harbor's water and it is under this harbor's own chart, and if the water is not up over it you can be in the Rooks before the man on the headland has his coat off. And I have done that in a force eight with a reef in and I have never once had a boat of mine lost on that coast, and I have lost two of other men's on a coast further south where there is a proper chart and a light."

"Two men drowned on ground somebody else called his own," Nessa Pike said.

"That is not the same thing and you know it is not," Dace said. "Harl Dace, on the second of March, at a fish dock, in front of witnesses, and put it in your book because it is true and because in four years somebody is going to ask me what I have got on this coast and the answer is going to be nine marks and a bad spring and a boy's broken finger, and I would rather there were a coast in the book as well."

---

The harbor authority's own soundings were the second thing, and Dace had brought the paper with him and would not leave it on the table, and put it down for about a minute and took it away again.

"That is this office's sheet of the inner shoals. It runs three miles out from the mole and it comes back in at the headland and it stops there, because there is nothing inshore of the headland for five miles north that this office has ever sounded and nothing up there to moor on and nobody to moor." He put the sheet in his coat. "So when you go and sound the bar you are not going to find anybody out. You are going to find a blank. And a blank is not a wrong figure and a blank cannot be argued with, and that is the difference between a coast nobody has charted and a coast somebody has charted badly, and I have wanted somebody to know which one mine is since I was seventeen years old."

"Then it cannot be checked against this office," Calder Marr said.

"No," Dace said. "It can only be checked against me, and against the water, and there is nobody else on that coast to check me against except a man who has been in the same road, and I have been trying for thirty-one years to find out whether any of them are still at it."

"Put that in the second column," the boy said.

"It is going in the second column," Dace said, "because a man on a mole in Morrow Quay cannot check a single thing I have said, and I am asking for a reason against me and you are going to have to write one. Write: *the party holds the account of a master of the *Grey Widow*, of no crew of this association, given on his own account, and not paid, and not engaged, and no part of it is checkable by any person in this harbor.* And put the name of the boat in it, because if I am wrong I would like there to be a name on it that a man can ask me about in a market."

The reason went in under that in Nessa Pike's own hand and the boy read it back and Dace read it after him and Calder Marr read it after Dace, and it is three, and the yard had not gone and got a fourth, and this time it did not need one, because the thing being written down was a man's own account of himself and the only readers it needed were the man and the writer.

---

He unwrapped the sailcloth at about half past eleven and put what was inside on the fish-dock table between two crates of fish that a woman moved without being asked, and it was a sheet of worked glass about the size of a folded chart, four inches across the hand-hole, with an iron collar round its edge and a loop at the head of it, and it was cloudy with age in the middle and clear at the rim, and it was warm, which a thing out of a sailcloth in the sun on a fish dock should not have been.

"That came out of a shop at Ashtide in the autumn," Dace said, "and I paid for it, and I want you to understand that before anybody says anything about it. A bellwright's shop, on the quay, with three men in it and a furnace going all winter. I went in with a thing I wanted to know and he said he could put it in glass if I could find a man who was willing to stand in his front room and say what he saw out of a window, and he said the word *willingly* the way a man says a word when he means it, and I thought that was a decent sort of shop."

"Who was the man."

"Corrie Ash," Dace said. "He is a boat hand off the *Anna Beth* and he was laid up in the winter with his chest and he had three weeks of nothing to do and he was minded to talk about it. He put his hand on the panel and he looked out of the shop's front window at the water and he said what he saw, and I stood in that shop and listened to a man describe a coast I have sailed since I was seventeen, and the description was mine, and I have been carrying it around ever since, and I would have told you it was a clever thing to do and I would not have come up this hill for it."

"Carried it in your boat," Nessa Pike said.

"Carried it in my boat, and got it out of the weather, and put it in the dry locker in the winter, and had it in the wheelhouse in March, and it is a sheet of glass about the size of two hands and it has a seal on it." He turned it over. "That is the part I cannot do anything with. That seal is not the shop's. It is not a maker's mark and it is not a bellwright's little scratch for himself. It is in an office's hand and it has got an office's name on it and it says what a thing is and who holds it and what a man may do with it, and I have had it since the autumn and I have been three times to the shop and twice to a house I was told was an office and I could not get anybody to tell me what my own boat is allowed to do with a thing my own boat is supposed to be holding."

He put his finger on the face of it, near the corner, where a struck mark about the size of a thumbnail sat in a ring.

"It names a custodian," Dace said, "and the custodian is the *Grey Widow*, and I have had three months to think about that and I have not been able to make it mean anything at all, and I have wanted it off my hands for a month and I have not wanted to give it away either, and that is the whole of why I am standing on your fish dock."

The boy had the slate against his knee and the pencil out before anybody had decided whether the entry went in.

"Stop," Nessa Pike said. "Do not write that word down yet."

"I was not going to write a name down," Dace said. "I was going to write what a man said."

"You were going to write a thing that is either the most useful line in this yard's book in five months or the one that costs this yard the only thing it has got, and I do not know yet which it is, and you do not either, and you have a bad hand and a good heart and that is exactly the combination I have to watch." She put her own hand flat on the slate. "And I am not going to have that object in this shed. Not because of the notice of the thirty-first, which is about recovered mineral and this is made glass and I have not got the law on it and I am not going to find out in a shed. Because the moment a thing stays in a shed it is in this yard's keeping, and this yard has not got a paper of any kind, and I will not hold a thing I have no right to hold on a day when I cannot answer for holding it."

"Put that in the book too," Calder Marr said, "in your own words, with the date, and put in that I did not argue with it, and put in that the object stays where it is, in the master's keeping and not in ours, until somebody with a power to say otherwise has looked at it."

"And who is that," Dace said.

"That is the question for Friday," Calder Marr said, "and the answer is a man in a coat who has a book and no power over glass, and I am going to go and ask him in the morning, and I am telling you on this dock now that I am going to ask, so that if he says no you and a dozen men will know that I asked."

The market went on behind them. The eighth thing on the market wall had been standing since the eighteenth of March with a naval paper nailed over one of the sheets beside it, and the harbor's tide bell went at ten past twelve in the road and the water off the south end gave it back a hand's width of a second later, and nobody in that street put a pencil on a page, because the only book in Morrow Quay that had ever made a line out of that bell was shut on a bench two hundred feet up the hill and belonged to a boy who was not there, and because there was no boat in this harbor that day with a slate on it, and the count in the yard's book stands where it stood on the thirty-first of March, because nothing has been added to it.

There was no money in that shed that day and there was nothing to sell, and the pump stood on its cart in the shed with the hose coiled and nothing to do until a man with money wanted it, and the bag was twelve, and the note was twenty-seven, and the tin had two crowns in it, and a man's mouth was the only thing this yard had put on the table in nine days that no office had asked for and no office could take away.
