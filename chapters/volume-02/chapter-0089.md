# Chapter 89

*The Ninth Thing on the Wall*

The ninth thing went up on the market wall at about ten on Saturday the fifteenth of April with a nail through the top corner, in Calder Marr's own hand, and it took him until about eleven because the sheet has the two columns on it and the second column has a reason against every line of it, and a man who has learned that in five months does not leave out a reason to save a quarter of an hour.

It was one sheet, and it was a foot and a half of writing, and under it, in the same hand, four lines that were not part of anything that came before it.

*That the party makes no claim to the water described above, to the marks, to the bar or to any use of them, and has asked for no instrument in respect of them, and holds none.*

*That the object described above is in the keeping of Harl Dace, master, of the *Grey Widow*, of no crew of this association, and has not at any time been in the keeping of the party, and the party gave a reason for declining to hold it and that reason is on a page in a shed and is not on this wall because it is about this yard and not about the sea.*

*That this sheet is not a seal, is not a claim, is not a survey, binds nobody, consents for nobody, and may be copied by any man who can read, and that the party will not ask any person for anything.*

*That the count of the copies of the public sheet of the fourteenth of October is eleven, and is eleven still, and nothing on this wall changes that, and no holder of any of them has been asked by this yard for any of them at any time and will not be.*

Amos Pryde read it from about two feet back with his thumb, the way he reads everything, and he had a mug in his other hand and had been standing there since it went up.

"That is my stall you have named in six months of this and I still have not got used to it," he said, "and it is the first thing on that wall that says out loud that a man is not to be asked for anything, and I have four men in this market who would each have paid a mark to see that line, and I am not going to tell you which four and I would think less of you if you asked me."

---

The man of fifty-eight from the south lay came at half past eleven and did exactly what an old woman in Coombe Row had told Calder Marr in advance he would do, which was to stand in front of a wall in a market in front of people and say that the whole of it was a tale.

"I have heard about this since Thursday," he said, and his name was Oswin Trask and he had a boat that was not in the south lay this month and had not been in the south lay for two seasons, and he said it without any particular heat, the way a man says a thing he has said before. "I have worked that coast since I was sixteen. There is a bar. It is a mile and a quarter off the point and it does not come up to two and a half on anything a man would take a boat over, and there is no cairn on the Old Custom House that I ever saw, and the Accountant has been a wreck of nothing for forty years and every man north of the Rooks knows it, and if you have men coming down your dock with a lead to sound a tale then somebody in this market is selling them the tale and it is not a free thing."

"Enter that in the first column," the boy said, and entered it, with the hour, and with the man's name because he had given it out loud in a market without being asked to.

"Will you enter the last part of it as well," Trask said. "That somebody is selling them the tale. Because I have been in this market forty years and I know what a page costs to print, and I have not got a page and I have got a boat that is not in a lay, and I would like it on that wall that a man of fifty-eight with no paper said a thing was a tale and was not proved wrong either, because you have not proved me wrong and I have not proved you."

"Enter that too," Nessa Pike said, and it went in.

And Calder Marr did not argue with him, and said why, in the street, with about thirty people in it, which was that a man who puts a man's objection on a wall in the man's own words has bought something that no argument could buy, and that Trask had been in the market forty years and had said the thing aloud and had not been asked anything and had been entered, and that in four years the only thing that would matter about this wall was whether both names were on it.

"You will get the worse of it," Trask said. "There is a man in a city who is looking for a reason and I have been in this market forty years and I can tell you what he does with a thing like this. He does not read your figures."

"He has never read one of my figures in his life," Calder Marr said, "and I would not want him to start."

---

The second man came at about four in the afternoon and he was thirty-one and he was of the second lay and he had a boat with a bad wheel in the basin behind him, and he stood in front of the ninth thing and read the whole of it, both columns, and then read the first column again, and then he went and got his own slate — he had a slate, every man of that water has a slate — and he compared two figures on it against the wall, in the street, with people looking, and then he said the thing that he had said he would say.

"Eleven feet at half-tide," he said, "on the top course and the black on it, and I have stood on that rock in October when it was drying at the same state and I would have said eleven. And two and three-quarters at twenty past two on the flood off a spring, and I have a figure of my own for that bar that I have never put in a book because there is no book, and mine is two and three-quarters, and I put mine in my slate in November and I have carried it about ever since and I never showed a soul."

"Your name is Coll Ferrow," the boy said, from the wall, because he had it in the yard's book by then, having given it out loud in a market without being asked to, which is the way a man on this coast gives a name and is why this yard can use it.

"Ferrow, of the *Kestrel's Sister*, second lay, and I have a bad wheel and I have not been out of this harbor in nine days because of it, and I would like that on your wall next to the figures, because a man with a bad wheel and a slate is not much of a survey and I would not have anybody think more of the two numbers than they are."

Then the man from the *Sea Wren* read it, which nobody had asked him to and which took him a long time, because he stood in front of it with his hands behind his back and went from the top of the first column to the bottom of the second and then started again.

"Eight things on this wall when I came down in March and nine now," Noll Tarner said. "I read the first eight. This one is the first one you have put up that says what it is not for, and I have a copy of a leaf of your book in my coat that you made me a free copy of, and I am going to say the thing that neither of us has said since the twelfth of March and I am going to say it to you and not to the wall."

"Go on."

"I read your six copies at the top and the four you cannot reach and the four that have said no to you in a street," Tarner said, "and I have been trying for a month to work out what a record of a dock is for, and I have not got it, and I have got a better answer than I had. It is not for the people who keep the ground. It is for the people who come after you. Every man on that wall has said something in his own words and every one of you has let him. That is the whole of it and it is worth nothing at all to any of us this month."

---

Mara Venn had a letter and she asked for it to be read out at the foot of the wall, and Calder Marr read it out, and she stood beside him while he did it, which she had not done once in five months.

"It came up the coast on Wednesday the twelfth," she said. "It is from a shop on the quay at Ashtide and it is not the shop that worked the glass — I have not given anybody that name and it is not going on a wall. It is a different shop, and it has heard that there is a claim seal on a piece of glass in Morrow Quay and it has offered me four marks in writing for an opinion on the seal, and it wants a document, because it has a boy of twenty-six who works there and does not want to be asked questions in a market about a thing his own trade made the frame for."

"And the answer," Nessa Pike said.

"There is no document and there is not going to be one," Mara Venn said, "and I said so in a letter on Wednesday in about ninety words, and I sent it on my own account and I paid nothing for the carriage and I would not let the yard pay for it, and the answer is on that letter and you may hear it, and then I am going to say the rest of it in my own words and I would like the boy to write it down.

"A trade can be asked a question and can answer it. A trade cannot be asked for a paper. A paper is an act of an office, and I have not held an office since the fourth of January, and if that shop has four marks for a piece of paper then it does not want my opinion, it wants a thing it can put on a wall of its own and be a shop with an opinion. They wrote back on Friday and said that an opinion given in a market is not a thing they can pay for, and that is correct, and it is the first time in five months that anybody has declined to pay me for something I actually did, and I am putting the date on it and the figure on it and the fact that I asked for nothing."

"Four marks," the boy said, half aloud, and the pencil did not move.

"Not out of your column," Mara Venn said, without looking at him. "Mine, and it goes in mine, and the forty marks in that book are not one mark smaller because of it and are not one mark larger either. Enter the four, the date, and that I was refused payment, and put under it that the refusal is a figure about my trade and not about this yard, because in about two years somebody is going to read that line and think this yard has cost a conservator something, and this yard has cost me everything already and it is written in a different hand on a different page."

The boy entered it and read it back and she read it after him, and it was a Saturday market with about forty people in it and a piece of worked glass in a sailcloth under a counter at the back of a shop, and nobody had asked for it and nobody would.

Then Calder Marr put a hand on the wall itself, flat, on the ninth thing, the way he had put a hand on the eighth in March, and stopped, and took it off again.

"How many copies," he said.

"That is the ninth question anybody has asked me this month," the boy said, "and there is no money in this yard to print a sheet of paper, and a wall with one sheet on it is a thing that comes down in a year, and I have been looking at the four in the chest and they are the sheet of the fourteenth of October and not this one, and I could write this one out twice in an evening and read it back, and two is not eleven and nobody in a city can tell the difference between eleven and two on a wall."

"Then it is two, and the second one goes to a man who has not been asked, because the whole of this wall is a list of people who have not been asked," Calder Marr said, "and the second one is Mr Pryde, and he will want to pay for it, and he may."

And the ninth thing stood up on the market wall through the rest of the day and into the evening, and the market shut under it at dusk, and the bag was three and the note was thirty and the tin was not touched and the eleventh week of a wage at four marks a week fell due on the Tuesday and was not yet due, and the third lay was full, and the ninth seam's mooring was in service and the north berth was out of service and had been since the first of December, and fifty fathoms of winter warp lay on the north wall where ninety were to lie, and the mole-head light was not on, and the glass had been falling for twelve days and had not made up into a rain.
