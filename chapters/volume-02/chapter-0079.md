# Chapter 79

*A Trade Is on a Shelf*

She was in the shed when Calder Marr came down on Wednesday the twenty-second of March, at about half past eight, with her cup and her book under her arm, and she had been there since seven, and Bevin Rook was on the bench with the book shut in front of him and his hand flat on it, and neither of them had said anything for an hour and a half.

"You told me on Tuesday night," she said, before he had got the door shut. "Not Piet. You."

"Calder told me at nine last night and I was up the hill at ten this morning before the market because I did not want to be told it in a street."

"I have been asked about an office that I have not held since the fourth of January, and by a man with a slate, in a market, on the Saturday, and I have had four days to decide whether to tell this yard or not, and I have decided, and I want it said at the beginning that I decided and that it was not Piet Marrow that made me." She put the cup down and did not drink from it, which was a thing she had been doing for sixteen weeks. "So that is the shape of it and I am going to say it once and plainly, and then I am going to refuse you something, and I would like the refusal to come second so that the first half is not mistaken for a favour."

---

"The man was not naval and he was not from the court and he was not from the office that has my file, and he had a slate and a bad coat and he was about thirty and he asked four people the same four questions, which means he had them written down and he was working through a market."

"Go on."

"He asked whether the woman who wrote the fair copy of the sheet on your wall had lost her office in January and in what month. He asked what she had examined in October, in her own hand and in her own office. He asked where that thing is now. And he asked whether she still came to the fish dock on a Tuesday." She turned her cup a quarter turn on the bench. "The first question is on your wall, because your wall says in its own limits that the fair copy is a conservator's and every name that touched either object is on it, and there are six of us, and I am one of them, and a man who can read can get me in about nine seconds. I told him January. I told him the fourth of January because it is on the wall and there is no use being coy about a fact that is nailed up in a market."

"And the other three."

"I said no to all three and I said it in four words each and he wrote down that I said no, in his own hand, in front of me, the way your boy writes things down." She looked at the boy. "He wrote down that I said no. That is what I want this yard to understand about it. He did not ask me a question I had to refuse. He asked me questions and entered my refusals, and he is going to be able to say in a room somewhere that a conservator of this coast has declined three questions about an object of October, and that is a different and a better thing for him than a refusal, and there is nothing I can do about it and I have not got anybody left to complain to about it."

Nessa Pike had come in by then and was standing at the back with her arms folded.

"Four days," she said.

"Four days is the whole of what I have to say against myself and I have been saying it to myself since Saturday, and I did not tell you on Saturday because I did not want to be useful about it, and I did not tell you on Sunday for the same reason, and on Monday I decided I was going to be frightened about it privately like an ordinary person and I did not manage it." She picked the cup up and put it down again. "I have not been frightened about anything since the fourth of January and I had forgotten what it costs to keep something to yourself that somebody else wants. That is the honest report and there is no figure in it and I am sorry."

---

"Calder is going to ask you for a figure now," Nessa Pike said, "and I am telling him so in front of you, because that is what he does and he has been getting better at it and I would like the whole room to have the count."

"Ask it," Calder Marr said. "I am asking you for a figure and not for a decision and I have asked you for nothing since the twenty-seventh of January, and this is me asking for a figure."

"What figure."

"What it would cost, and what it would take, and what would have to be true, for a person with standing to say in writing that the sheet on our wall is a true copy of the sheet the circuit office holds, and that its figures are the figures it says they are. Marks, weeks, and a name. I have not written a name on this page and I am not going to."

"No," said Mara Venn.

"That is four words. I have come a fair way to ask it and I would like the rest."

"The rest is that there is nobody." She said it flatly and she did not raise her voice and she did not put her hands anywhere. "There is no person on this coast who can say that, because saying it is an act of an office and I have not held an office since the fourth of January, and the only other person who could say it in Veyr is one I have not spoken to in four months and would not take a paper from on the ground I have heard about. And that is the price of the fourth of January and nobody has ever sent me the bill and here it is: the one thing the circuit office asked for on the thirtieth of December cannot be done by anybody, and the reason it cannot be done is that the person who could have done it was working in that office on the day she signed it and now she is a woman in a shed with a cup she does not drink out of. And in about two years there will be a file in Veyr that says a record was asked to be single and could not be made single, and it will be true, and the reason will be on nobody's page."

"Then say the part that is yours to say," Nessa Pike said. "You are a trade. You said it yourself in this shed in February. Say what a trade can and cannot do and do not do it as a complaint."

"A trade can take a sheet of paper and a fair hand and say what the paper is and when it was made and by what press, and a trade can say whether forty sheets came off one forme or two, and a trade can price that at six marks and three weeks and can be expensive about it. What a trade cannot do is stand behind it when a man in a room asks by what authority, and on the fourth of January I stopped being able to answer that question and everything else I know is still true and none of it can be used." She turned to the boy. "You will not write down the first half of that as though it were an offer, because I have been in four months of this yard making every person in it useful and I will not have my trade put on a shelf and then described as stock. Write down that I said the second half, in my words, with the date, and that I said the first half so that it would be clear I had not said the first half. And put my name to it, because I am asking to be on it. I am not being named this time. I am asking."

"It goes in," Nessa Pike said, "in her own words, and it goes in that she asked to be on it, and it goes under the date and not over it, and Calder, read the rule back to me before you touch the book."

The boy read the rule back and Mara Venn looked at the page while he did it, and she read it back herself afterward, which is the fifth, and nobody told her to.

---

And then there was a silence of about a minute and a half, and the fly came out of the stove and went back, and Calder Marr put his hand flat on the bench on the other side of the book and did not touch it.

He did not ask her. It is the fourth time in five months that he has come within four inches of it and has not, and the first time in five months that the four inches have been visible to two other people in a room.

"Enter this," he said, instead, and his voice was not steady and he did not pretend it was. "Entered the twenty-second of March, in the holder's own hand, in mine and not in hers: *that the holder has not asked the said conservator anything else, and that he is not going to, and that there is a thing he has wanted to ask her since the fourteenth of October, and that he has come to within four inches of it four times now, and that on this occasion she saw him do it and neither of them said a word, and that in about two years there is going to be a man in a room who says that a woman with no standing knew what was in that anchor, and that he will be right, and that there is no page anywhere in this harbor that says she was never asked, and that there is now one, and that it is this one.*"

"Read it back," Nessa Pike said.

He read it back. The boy read it after him. Mara Venn read it after the boy and said one sentence and did not say it to Calder Marr.

"Thank you for writing down the part where it would have been easy to ask me and you did not, and for writing down the part where I knew, and neither of those is the same as forgiveness and I am not going to call it that and neither are you."

Then Calder Marr asked her the last question, which was a figure and not a decision, and which was the only one he was entitled to ask her.

"What does a day of yours cost, and what do you live on."

"Six marks a day and I live on a stall-holder's hull that I look at for nothing because I have nothing to charge, and this week I looked at two and neither of them needed me and I told them so and they paid me the same, which was nothing." She shut her book under her arm. "And the forty marks stand and I have not asked for them since the fifteenth of October and I am not going to ask for them in a month in which a form has cost this harbor a pump, and if you try to pay me a mark of it out of a bag with eight marks in it I will walk it back down that dock myself and put it on the wet stones, and you know that I would, and you know that you would let me, and that is the arrangement and it is not a friendship and it is not meant to be one."

"I was not going to," Calder Marr said.

"I know you were not. That is the whole of what has changed in five months and it is a very small thing and it is the only thing in this shed I have ever been able to use."

She went down the dock at about eleven. The boy wrote the entry and read it back twice, and Nessa Pike read it a third time and then went out and stood in the road for a while with her arms folded, and the book lay on the bench with the entry in it in two hands, and the eighth thing on the market wall was four days old and said *a record of which there is one is a thing that can be taken away*, and there were nine days to the last day of the charter, and the bag had eight marks in it, and the note stood at twenty-four, and nobody in Morrow Quay could make a record single, and the reason why was on a page in a shed.
