# Chapter 64

*The Second Seal*

The boy off the mole came up the fish dock at four in the afternoon on the same Friday the twenty-seventh of January with a leather wallet and a bad cold, and he put the wallet on the bench and said the office had sent for a signature, which was the sentence he had been told to say at the door so that nobody would shut the door in his face, and then he took the paper out and put it on top of the wallet and stood there while it was read, because he had been told to stand there.

Mara Venn was in the shed. She had been in the shed since two o'clock with a cup of water she had not drunk, doing the only work available to a woman with no standing, which is to be in a room where something is being decided and say nothing until she is asked.

Nobody in that shed had asked her anything in eleven weeks and she had stopped expecting to be.

"That is the second request," she said, before Calder Marr had turned it over. "I know the shape of it from the window envelope. Do not read it out loud in front of a man who is standing there. Read it out loud in front of him anyway if you want, because a document read in a room is a document in the room."

---

It was four paragraphs and it was the paper of the thirtieth of December with a second seal on the second sheet, and the second seal was not a refusal and it was not a demand, and that was the whole of it.

The first request had asked the party to deliver the public sheet and all copies of it into the custody room of the Morrow circuit office, so that the record of a contested material might be single.

The second seal said, in nineteen words on a separate line in the office's own type: *That no ground having been received, the said request stands unanswered in this office, and this office awaits the same.*

"That is all of it," Mara Venn said. "Nineteen words. Read the type."

"It is the same type as the first."

"It is the same type, the same size, and the same composition, and it was set at Veyr or in this circuit's copying room, and it was set before you were born, and the office did not write those nineteen words this week. They have had them for years. There is a form in that building and it is used when a party has not answered inside the term, and it goes on a file and the file sits in a drawer, and nobody in that building has to be angry for it to arrive on your bench on a Friday afternoon in the middle of a wet week." She put her cup down. "That is what a delay is. It is not a decision. It is a document that costs the office nothing and costs the other party the count of the days."

"Then say the count," Bevin Rook said.

"The first request is dated the thirtieth of December," Mara Venn said. "The fourteen days run from the date of the paper and not from the day you got it. You were given the fee receipt of the fifth of January and the receipt itself told you that six of the fourteen had gone and that the fourteenth day was the thirteenth of this month, and you wrote that down and you read it back, and you have not done anything about it since, and neither has the office, and both of you have been waiting for each other for a fortnight in the particular way that two men wait for each other when each of them believes the other will move first."

"So it stood unanswered on the thirteenth."

"It stood unanswered on the fifteenth day, which is the thirteenth, and the office counts days, and today is the twenty-ninth day, which is the twenty-seventh, and that nineteen-word line is the office saying so to itself in writing." She looked at Calder Marr. "You have known that date since the fifth. Say so. He has known that date since the fifth and he has been letting it run, and I would like it in the room that the letting go was a decision and not a forgetting, because a forgetting is something a man apologizes for and a decision is something a man stands behind."

"It was a decision," Calder Marr said, "and the reason for it is that I cannot pay to answer it, and the reason I cannot pay to answer it is that a day of walking each way is three men not on a wall, and the reason three men not on a wall matters in February is that there is a wind coming and a mooring on the ninth seam that has been bought back and not worked, and if I am on a road to the circuit office on the sixteenth of February then that mooring is not worked in February."

"Then that is the reason and it is a good one and it is not the office's reason," Mara Venn said, "and I want the two of them kept apart in front of me, because in about two years a man in Veyr is going to read this file and he is going to see a party that let a term run in January, and if there is nothing in the file about a mooring, he is going to write a sentence about this harbor that will be in somebody else's hands for a century."

"Then what do we do," Nessa Pike said.

"We do the thing that costs the least and admits the most," Mara Venn said, "and I am going to tell you what it is, and then I am going to tell you what I am not going to do, and you are not going to argue with either."

---

"You enter the arrival," she said. "Not the answer. The arrival. The day, the hour, the name of the man who brought it, and the nineteen words as they are written, in his hand, in your book, and the date on the sheet you have had for three weeks is read back, and the fact that the fourteen ran from the thirtieth of December and not from the day you had it. That is free. It costs a man with a bad cold a walk up a dock and it costs you a column. It is the cheapest thing an office sells and it is the only thing in this exchange that is a fact.

"And then you do not answer, and you do not refuse, and you do not write a ground, and you do not send a man to the copying room to pay three marks to have a refusal dated, because a dated refusal is an answer and an answer puts the office's next move in a form you have chosen instead of one it has chosen for itself."

"Which leaves the file open," Calder Marr said.

"The file stays open and the file is dated, and an open file with a date on it is a thing I can be asked about in a room, and a closed file with a ground in it is a thing I cannot be asked about because there is nothing in it to ask about." She picked her cup up and did not drink from it. "I am going to say the other thing now and I would like it said out loud, all of it, and then I would like this subject closed for the month.

"I am named in the copy. I have read the copy, because the copy is addressed to the party and the party may show it to whoever it likes, and I am named in the third paragraph as a person who has already declined a related request. I did. In November. On a form, in my own hand, on four grounds, and it is in that office's file, and the effect of my name in that paragraph is that your yard cannot come to me about this and cannot pay me to come to it, and cannot use my certificate of method to answer it, and would be refused twice over if it tried.

"And I have no standing to refuse anything. There is no office in this coast left in which I can refuse anything at all. I can price a thing. That is all that is left of me as far as that office is concerned, and it is why the fourth of January happened to me the way it did, and if you ask me today whether you should answer this request, I will not answer you, not because I think the answer is wrong but because there is nothing behind me to refuse it with. I am a trade. A trade does not say no. A trade is expensive or cheap and it is on a shelf." She put the cup down again without having drunk from it. "I have been expensive in this room for eleven weeks and I have not been asked to do anything. Do not make me useful. I have watched four months of this yard make every person in it useful and I have read the book, and the book is full of it."

Nobody in that shed said anything for a while.

"Enter it," Nessa Pike said at last, "in the boy's own right hand, and read it back twice, and put the hour on it, and put the name of the man with the cold on it because he stood there while it was read and that is the only part of him that matters and he will be gone in a minute."

Then Calder Marr asked the one question he had in him, and he asked it as a figure and not as a decision, which is the difference he has been learning for a month and which he was going to be judged on in March.

"What does it cost to answer it," he said, "and what does it cost not to, in days, in marks, and in standing, and I want the three in that order and I want them in Mara Venn's mouth and not in mine, and I am not asking you what we should do."

Mara Venn looked at him for a moment longer than she had looked at him in any of the four months they had known each other.

"Three marks to have anything entered at the counter and a day's walk each way for two people, so five marks and two days, and there is a third mark in it for the form itself and the form is free and the third mark is the man's time while he stands in a queue, so call it six and two days and be careful with it. Not to answer costs nothing in marks, and it costs fourteen days a month in the shape of the file, and the file is going to have a second seal on it by the spring and a third by the summer, and in about two years one of those seals is going to be read by a man in Veyr as a party that has never answered a request of that office, and that is a sentence about your yard that is not true, and neither of you will ever be able to say so, because the file will be a file." She stood up. "And in standing it costs me nothing, because there is none of it left to cost, and I would like that written down as well, in my own words and in your book, so that if anybody ever asks me what I did in the winter of this harbor I can point at a page instead of at a memory."

"Enter all of it," Nessa Pike said, "and the three in that order, and put that she said she was not going to be asked what to do in the line under it, because that is the first time in five months that anybody in this yard has written down a person refusing to be useful and it belongs in the same book as the money."

---

The boy with the cold left at half past five and went down the dock without his coat on, and the twenty-eighth was a Saturday, and the yard bought two marks of air and a mark of rope at the chandler's in the morning and the boy carried the figure back himself and read it out in the shed at noon, and the bag went from twenty to seventeen and the tin was still empty and the note still stood at thirteen and the fortnight's food was on the table for the Tuesday.

And Bevin Rook did one thing that nobody had asked him to do and then did not do it a second time, which was to take the second request out of the wallet and hold it up to the window and read the second sheet again, because there was a line on it that he had not read out loud in the room, and the line was in the office's own type and said that the copies of the public sheet to be delivered were ten.

"Ten," he said. "It says ten."

"It is dated the thirtieth of December," Mara Venn said, "and the count was ten on the thirtieth of December, and it is correct for its own date."

"It is correct for its own date," Bevin Rook said, "and the count is eleven, and a naval man wrote ten on a slate of his own in December, and the *Vigil* has four sheets on that wall with a captain's signature line on the fourth of them, and there is a second seal on this paper that was set by somebody in that office who had to decide today what to write at the bottom of a request that was written before we gave a copy away."

He put it down flat on the bench.

"I am entering three facts and I am entering no opinion," he said. "One: the office's paper of the thirtieth of December says ten, and the count is eleven. Two: the second seal was affixed today by that office, and the second seal carries the count in the office's own type. Three: I do not know whether that office has not been told, has been told and has not corrected it, or has been told and has left it standing, and neither does Calder, and neither does she, and I would like the entry to say that all three of us do not know, because in four years somebody is going to want to know when a record that this yard put on a wall stopped being a record of a thing and started being a thing somebody was watching, and I would like the date of the question to be in our book in our own hands rather than in a clerk's."

"Enter it," Nessa Pike said, "and put the three facts in a column and the not-knowing under them and nothing else, and no adjective anywhere in it, and if either of you two ever wants to write a fourth fact in that column you will bring it to me first and I will read it out loud before it goes in."

The boy read the entry back twice, and then read it a third time slowly, and then shut the book, and the wall in the fish market had seven things on it and a naval document four inches above one of them with a gap a man could put a finger into, and the number in the office's own type was ten, and it was the twenty-seventh of January, and the fourteenth day of a term that had begun on the thirtieth of December was thirteen days gone, and the second seal cost nobody anything at all, which was the first time anybody in that shed had been handed a piece of paper that was free and had a date on it.
