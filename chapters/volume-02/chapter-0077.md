# Chapter 77

*The Eighth Thing on the Wall*

The paper came down the fish dock on Saturday the eighteenth of March in the hand of a boy off the mole, and it was a fourth request from the same office and it was dated the sixteenth, and the first thing in it was a figure the yard had been waiting six weeks to see changed.

*That the said request stands unanswered in this office, and this office awaits the same* — with a ground received on the sixth, and a copy of an entry made in the hearing room of this harbor on the fifteenth instant, from which the office notes that the count of the copies of the public sheet in circulation is eleven, and the party is therefore required to deliver into the custody room of this office the said public sheet and the eleven copies of it now held, wherever held, together with the book of the party from the fourteenth of October to the date of this paper.

Nessa Pike read it twice at the head of the dock with the wind coming off the water and then went and got the book out of the chest in the shed, which is a thing she does about four times a year, and she found the count of the twenty-third of November on a leaf and read it out on the steps.

"It says eleven," she said. "It said ten in the type on the thirtieth of December and it said ten again on the second seal in January, and the reeve forwarded the vessel's paper to that office on Wednesday with our count in it, and they have set the count up in their own type in three days, and nobody in this yard told them anything and nobody in this yard has told them anything since the second of March. They are not wrong. They have simply been told, and it was not by us, and it is going in the book in that form, and I am not going to have one word of an adjective on it."

"Entered," said Bevin Rook, "and read the date at the foot and not the day it came."

"Eighteenth of nothing, and it is a Saturday. It is dated the sixteenth. The fourteen runs from the sixteenth and that is a Monday, so the thirtieth, and the thirtieth is the day before this yard's charter runs out, and it is the day on which the request wants a book delivered into a custody room in another city. Put both of those in one line and put the word *coincides* out of it, because a coincidence is a word a man uses when he does not want to write down that he arranged it."

---

They had decided it at about nine that morning, in nine minutes, and it was the shortest argument of the winter because both men in it had already made it.

The seven copies in the yard's keeping were not going to be delivered, and the reason was the yard's own ground of the second of March, and Calder Marr said it out loud in the shed at about nine at night with the stove going and made himself say all of it.

"We wrote four paragraphs a fortnight ago and the third one said that we cannot hand back a record that is in other men's hands, and the second one said that we tell the truth twice in public and strike out neither, and the first one said what the sheet is. That was a good ground. It is going up the coast on Monday for three marks and it is in a file. And if we send the same ground again, with a different date, on a delivery of the copies, then we are not answering. We are repeating ourselves, and a party that repeats itself is a party that has run out of grounds, and an office can tell the difference between a party with nothing to say and a party that has said everything once and is saying it twice for the term, and the second one is the one that gets a fourth seal."

"You could send it anyway," Nessa Pike said. "It is three marks and it is the same four paragraphs and it is true."

"And it is a man reading a file who sees the same four words twice with a fortnight between them," Calder Marr said, "and what he writes in the margin is *party has no further ground*, and then the twenty days run out and he does not need to write a fourth seal, he just stops asking, and the file goes up to somebody as a file that is finished, and there is no fourth seal and there is no end to it. A delay is free. A repeated ground is a finished file."

"So we do not answer it."

"We do not answer it this month and we do not refuse it, and we are going to be the same thing that office is, and I have been calling that a delay in this book since the twenty-seventh of January and I have had it in my own handwriting twice."

Then Nessa Pike said the thing that made it a decision instead of a mood.

"And we cannot pay the three marks," she said. "The bag has two. It has two because we bought a pump on a note on Wednesday and because the fortnight's eating came off the same bag on Saturday, and the air pot wants two more before the moon and the fortnights do not stop coming. Three marks is not a moral question this week. Three marks is four days of the air and it is the whole of what is in the bag, and a yard with nothing in the bag in March is a yard that has to take a herring man's ring off a fouled ring in April, and that is not a thing I am willing to do to pay a counter three marks to not say anything."

"So it is a delay and it is free," Calder Marr said, "and the only reason it is free is that nobody's boat is in a lighter over it, and I want that sentence in the book, and I want my name on it, because in two years somebody is going to write that this yard delayed a request of the circuit office in March and did so out of a bag with two marks in it, and it will be true, and it will read like cowardice, and it will be the first time in five months that a thing this yard did has been made to look like a thing this yard was."

---

**The eighth thing went up on the market wall at about ten in the morning on Saturday the eighteenth of March**, in Calder Marr's own hand, because a record that is being used against you should at least be in the hand of the man it is being used on.

The sheet named eleven copies. Two upon this wall. Five rolled in oilcloth in the bench chest of the Free Keel yard against the bond of its charter. Three upon the walls of crews who are not parties to any instrument of the circuit office, being those of the *Low Lantern*, of the *Grey Widow*, and of a crew on the third lay whose name is not written down here because that crew has not asked to be written down. One in the keeping of Amos Pryde, master, of no crew of this association, at his stall upon this dock, issued on the eighteenth of January at his own asking, to be kept and not returned.

And under that, in the same hand, four lines.

*That the party has been asked by the circuit office to deliver the said sheet and the copies of it, and has not answered, and has not refused, and is in that state of its own motion and not by reason of any office's delay.*

*That the party will not deliver the copies in its own keeping, and gives the reason in one sentence, which is that a record of which there is one is a thing that can be taken away, and that a yard which cannot pay a wage in March has nothing whatever to protect itself with except a thing in two places.*

*That the party will not ask any person holding a copy of it to deliver that copy, has not asked, and will not; and that the party names the four holders above so that any man who is entitled to ask them may do so without coming to this yard for the name.*

*That the party will deliver to the circuit office the entry of any single day of the book of this yard, on the written asking of the crew whose day it is, and will deliver it unshortened with its reason and its two columns entire.*

"That last one is a good one and it cost nothing and it is a refusal dressed as an offer," Nessa Pike said, "and Calder, you know that, and you put it up anyway, because the alternative is a sheet that only says no four times."

---

Amos Pryde read the sheet from about two feet back, with his thumb, the way Harl Dace reads a document, and he had a mug in his other hand and he had been standing there since the paper went up and he had not said a word.

"That is my stall you have named," he said, "and I have been hiring hands out of this market for nineteen years and I have never once had a thing said about me on a wall, and I have been called a great many things under my breath and I would rather have that."

"Nobody has asked you for it and nobody is going to."

"I know nobody has asked me, and I want it said on that sheet somewhere that I said it is mine." He put the mug down on the stall board. "I will tell you what I am going to do with it, and I will tell you in the street, because in four years I will not remember this and a man who is in the street in four years is a man I can be held to. I am a coaster's master. I am on a schedule and I have to be in a coast road on a Tuesday whether anybody is in a market on the Monday. And the difference between a coaster's master and every other man on this dock is that if I am in a place on a day and a thing has been said about that place on paper, I have got something to put down. I have not got anything else. So no, I am not giving it up, and I would not give it up to a magistrate either unless he asked in writing, and if he asked in writing I would give it to a magistrate and not to a party and not to an office that wants it single."

"Write that down," Nessa Pike said, to the boy.

"It is going up under mine on Monday," Pryde said, "in my own hand, and I will want my own copy of the sheet, and I will pay a shilling for the copying, and I am telling you that I will pay because a thing a man gets for nothing is a thing a man is given, and I have been given enough things by this harbor in nineteen years."

Then a man came out of the *Low Lantern*'s door, forty-one or so, with a rope round his middle, and stood in front of the eighth thing on the wall and read it twice and said that his wall was staying up, and that a woman had taken her cover off this association's slate in the last week of October on her own statement and in a net loft, and that there are eleven of these sheets in this harbor and if his went down there would be ten, and a man who has been given a thing in a hard month and has handed it back in a harder one has not got a record, he has got a story.

"That is the best argument anybody has made for a sheet of paper in five months and it was made by a man with a rope round him," Calder Marr said, "and it is not written down anywhere and it is going to be written down tonight, and I am putting the name of no man on it and the name of the boat on it."

---

They came down the dock about one and the eighth thing was on the wall with a nail through the top corner and a man in oilskins standing in front of it reading it, and there was a gap in the market where a crowd of about thirty had been, and a herring skipper of the third lay stood at the end of it with his hands in his pockets and looked at the yard's boat and then at the sheet and then at the yard, and said the one thing nobody in that shed had wanted to hear on the eighteenth of March.

"You have put your own number on a wall," he said, "and there is a form in a file in another city that says a party in this harbor is holding seven copies it has been asked for and has not given up. That is going to be read by a man who is looking for a reason, and I have been in a market my whole life and I can tell you what a man who is looking for a reason does with a thing like that. He does not read your figures. He reads the shape of it."

"That is correct and I have nothing to say to it," Calder Marr said, "and the sheet is up and it is not coming down, and the ninth thing on that wall from this yard will be whatever that man in another city writes about us, and he can have that for nothing, and I would rather he had that than the seven."

And the boy of the *Sea Wren* was standing at the end of the dock with his hands in his sleeves in the wind, and he was fifteen and he could not read, and he had come down the hill to find out what was happening on the fish dock because a man in a slip at the pile had told him there was a matter, and he stood there for about ten minutes while a market went on round him, and then he asked Nessa Pike, in a low voice, whether the paper with his crew's day on it was the one that took the *Sea Wren*, and she said that she did not know and that nobody in this yard knew and that the form had said *entries* and had not said which one, and the boy said that was all right, and that there was a man in the third lay out in the blow since the twenty-eighth of February who was not back on Wednesday the first of March and had not been back since, and that he was not saying it because of anything except that there were eleven of them out and nobody had gone, and that the *Sea Wren* was the boat that would have gone.
