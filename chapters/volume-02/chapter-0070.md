# Chapter 70

*A Statement of Capacity*

The hearing room over the tide-gang store had a stove in it and a bench for the reeve and a bench for the clerk and four benches for people who had come to be in a room, and on Wednesday the first of March at nine in the morning the slate wall behind the reeve's bench was blank and had been blank since the cloth came off it at eight on Thursday the twenty-third of February, and the ledger was on the table, and the ledger is the record, and everybody in that room knew it and nobody had to say so.

There were forty-one boats in the basin. The forty-first had come in at about six in the morning with her warp out and had anchored in the roads because there was nowhere for her, and the harbor authority's own clerk said forty on Thursday and a man in the market said forty-one yesterday afternoon, and neither of them had a book that settled it, and the difference of one boat between two figures in the same harbor in the same week is the whole of the difference between a berth short and a harbor that is short.

Ors Kest called the sitting himself, which he had not done since the twenty-eighth of November, and he had called it for two matters and he had told the clerk so on Friday, and the clerk had the two papers ready and the ledger open at a leaf with a rule on it.

"I am going to be short with the room," Kest said, "because there is a wind on the water and forty-one boats in it and a man in Veyr waiting for an answer, and I have two documents and both of them are mine, in the sense that they are on my table and my name is on them, and neither of them was written by this office, and I am going to read the first one and let a herring skipper read the second one, and then I am going to let anybody here who came to speak speak, and I am going to let it be short."

---

The first was the harbor authority's own statement of what the authority had spent since the twenty-eighth of November, which Kest had promised in his own barge on the seventeenth of January to a coast office, and which he had read in a room in November about a fence.

*Four sets of moorings, the fourth being the ninth seam's, bought out of this office's own repair account and out of the fender money, the timber fenders being returned to service on the first of December upon the narrowing of this office's chart claim to three seams about the fifth. A season of warp for the herring fleet along the north wall, ninety fathoms, bought out of the same account. One day of this office's tide gang on the wall on the twelfth of January at this office's cost, and a hand crane at two marks a day for two days, seven marks together, for a day's work this office did not get done. The ninth seam's mooring worked on the seventeenth of February, private work on the account of a herring skipper, at this office's cost, and this office has no power to lift the notice that closes that ground and did not lift it. Two goes with a hired diver on the seventeenth and the stone of this office's own foundation brought up in pieces. And forty feet of this office's own tarred marline in the sea, out of ninety, on the twenty-seventh, by a shackle of this office's own moorings gear put through a stone post that never had a shackle in it, which this office supplied because this office had no better iron and would not buy iron in the last week of February. And a ring and forty-one feet of warp belonging to a herring skipper, lost on the twenty-eighth to the change in the run of the water at the wall's head, at this office's cost, and it is a boat's loss and not a figure and it is entered here as a boat's loss. Fifty fathoms of winter warp lie on the north wall where ninety were to lie. Three men of this office's tide gang are stood off from the twenty-fourth of February until the fourth of March, being Rowlandike, Petch, and Wil Slee, and this office is out the days of three men in the last week before the fleet is in.*

"And this," Kest said, "is this office's account and it is in my own hand, and I have read it out because a reeve who writes a clever version is a reeve who has to be clever again in four years. Nineteen winters of herring dues are disputed and unpaid and this office has paid none of them, and this office's repair account was drawn down in November and December, and this office bought forty boats their moorings and did not buy a herring skipper nineteen winters of peace. Mr Voke can have this document and so can the coast office, and I will have said it first."

He put it on the table.

"That is a document this office wrote," he said. "The second one is not."

---

Elias Voke stood up off the bench at the back, which he had not done in a room in this harbor in six years, and took a sheet out of an inside coat pocket, and it was creased along four folds and it was not clean, and nobody in that room said anything about either of those things.

 "I wrote it," he said. "Not a clerk and not the reeve and not the man from the coast office. I wrote it at my own table on Saturday night, and I was not paid for it and I have not asked anybody to pay me for it, and I am going to read it out slowly because it is about boats and not about iron and the two do not go together in a coast office.

"The coast office asked, on the twenty-eighth of November, in four lines, for a statement of the capacity of this harbor to moor the fleet. That is the word they used. *Capacity.* I have had eleven weeks to find out what it means and I have not found out, and I have decided that a man who has not found out what a word means should say so in writing, and so the first line of this is that I do not know what the word means, and that what follows is what I know, which is forty-one boats and two berths and a mole.

"Two berths. The south lay and the second lay, and the third lay, and the third lay is a mud berth and it fills. On the first of March there are forty-one boats and two berths and a mole, and the north berth is out of service and has been since the first of December, and it is out of service because of a notice served on the twenty-eighth of October by the Registry that closes the mole's toe from the third seam to the eleventh to all work and all approach by all persons, which includes this office. I know that because this office told me so in a room in November and told me so again in January, and I have not asked why the notice says what it says, because I am a herring skipper and it is not my notice and there is nothing I can do with it.

"And the north berth is the one that takes a north-easter. It has taken three of them since I was a boy and it is the only wall in this harbor that does, and on the last three it took eleven boats' ground tackle off the men who could not pay for it and one boat off a man of sixty-two who has just paid nine winters and cannot pay the tenth. That is the capacity of this harbor on the first of March. It is a mole with a gap in it and forty-one boats.

"The ninth seam's mooring was bought back with this office's own money in January and it was not worked until the seventeenth of February, and it is in service now, and I want that in the document because the reeve paid for it and he is entitled to the line, and it is a mooring and it is not a berth, and a mooring is a place to tie a boat and a berth is a place to lie in a wind, and this harbor gained a mooring in February and a berth it has not gained, and those are not the same thing and I will not let them be the same thing in a piece of paper.

"And there is a hard at Ashtide. I applied for it on the ninth of November. There has been no answer and I have been up twice since and asked whether the application is in and both times a man has said it is in, and in eleven weeks nothing has come back and I have a fleet of forty-one and no hard to put a fouled hull on, and that is a thing I would like the coast office to read about because it is a coast office question and not a harbor question.

"And I have a hard here that nobody has money for, and the harbor's carpenters are on the authority's own account and are not doing private work in the three weeks before the fleet comes in, and there is a boat on that hard called the *Kittiwake* with her bilge blocked in and no cradle under her since the twenty-eighth of November, and I would like that in it, because this coast is poor and busy and short of a hard, and that is a fact and not an opinion, and I have not put the word *capacity* anywhere in this document except in the first line, where I say I do not know what it means."

He stopped, and put the sheet on the table face down, which nobody had asked him to do, and sat down.

"Three names," he said. "I am going to read the last paragraph and I am going to read it in this room and not hand it to a clerk, and there are three names in it and one of them is in this room."

---

"The ninth seam's mooring is in service since the seventeenth of February and it is a mooring and not a berth, and the north berth is out of service, and the fleet is one berth short on the first of March, and the tide gang is three men down and the authority's own repair account is empty, and the boy whose wage I have been asked about is called Bevin Rook and he is twenty-two and he has been in the crew of the Free Keel yard since the fourteenth of October, first for the reading of a book at four marks a week from that date, and then for a hand, and his hand is not a hand. He has had a hand since the twenty-ninth of October and a wrist since the twelfth of January, and he is entered in that yard's book as a hand at four marks a week from the first of February at his own asking, and the yard has paid three weeks of it up to Saturday last and owes him the fourth, and I have seen the book because I asked to see the book, and a skipper who has never asked to see a book in his life saw it in January, and that is the only recommendation I have ever had for a man I did not know."

The room was very quiet. The stove ticked.

"He is in this room," Voke said, "and he is the only one of the three of us in that yard who can read, and the yard has a wall in the fish market with seven things on it and a naval paper nailed over the top of one of them with a gap a man can put a finger into, and I have read that wall and I could not understand the half of it and I understood all of it. And I have told the coast office in writing tonight that the record of this harbor's own ground is kept by a yard that owes that boy forty marks and cannot pay them and has not asked him to take less, and a fleet that is going into a harbor in a north-easter ought to know whose figures are being kept, and I would rather the coast office read it from a herring skipper than work it out in four years from a plate nobody is allowed to touch."

He turned round in the bench.

"Bevin Rook," he said. "Your wage. The four marks for the first of February. Is it paid."

The boy stood up. He had a sling on his left arm and his left hand would not close and he had come in a coat with his right hand in the pocket of it, and he took the right hand out.

"All four of them," he said. "Three of the weeks were paid in the ordinary way. The fourth fell due on the twenty-first of this month and it was not paid, and I did not ask for it, and it was paid at seven o'clock this morning out of a column I worked out on a slate on Saturday night, and nobody in this yard told me it was coming and I did not ask anybody whether it was coming, and I would like that in the book in that order. And the forty marks stand and are not reduced and I do not want them reduced, and I am not going to say anything else about money in a hearing room, and I have said that and I would like the clerk to write down that I said it."

The clerk looked at the reeve.

"I have not got a box for that," the clerk said.

"Then enter it in the margin in your own hand and initial it," Kest said, "and do not summarize it, and if anybody in Veyr ever asks this office what a boy said in a room on the first of March, this office will be able to hand them a page and not a summary, and that is the fourth time this quarter that something has gone into this office's book in something other than a box, and I am not going to stop counting."

The clerk wrote it and initialled it and did not shorten it, and Mara Venn, at the back, on a bench with no name on it, said one sentence before anybody asked her anything, which was:

"If the room wants a conservator's opinion today it will not get one from me, because I have no standing in this office or in any office on this coast, and I am named in a copy of a request of the thirtieth of December as a person who has already declined a related request, and so I am a door that has been tried. Enter that I said so in advance, and I am not offended, and I would rather say it here than have a clerk turn round."

Nobody in that room asked Calder Marr whether he wished to have her asked, and Calder Marr noticed that it was not asked, and did not say so.

---

Then the other thing, which was the thing Kest had put in his own book on the twenty-seventh of January and had not been able to leave alone since.

"I am going to read one more thing into this record and it is not mine," he said, "and I am doing it on my own motion and no party has asked me, and if I am wrong a man may strike it out in a year.

 "On the twenty-seventh of January a second seal came onto a request of the circuit office dated the thirtieth of December. The request was entered and dated on the fifth of January for three marks, which is the cheapest thing that office sells, and it has not been answered since. The second seal is nineteen words and it says that no ground having been received the request stands unanswered in that office. It is unanswered, this office has no power to make it answered, and the party did not refuse it. In about a year that file will have a third seal on it, and a man reading about the capacity of this harbor in Veyr is going to read on the same page that a party in this harbor did not answer an office in a fortnight, and I would rather he read it from me in a room than find it in a column and think whatever he is going to think."

"Entered," the clerk said.

"Enter it with the date and the two dates of the term and not with the words twenty-ninth, which is a day and not a date, and if anybody in Veyr reads the twenty-ninth as the twenty-ninth of January he will be a man who cannot read a receipt and there is nothing this office can do about that."

Then he shut the ledger and put his hand on it.

"That is the sitting," he said. "Two documents, one of them mine and one of them a herring skipper's, and the word the coast office wanted has been answered. The coast office asked what this harbor can moor and it has been answered by a man with forty-one boats in it, in his own hand, in his own words, and the answer is that this harbor cannot moor them, and that the wall is a mole with a gap in it, and that a boat is on a hard with her bilge blocked in and no cradle under her, and that a coast which is poor and busy is also short of a hard."

He looked at Calder Marr.

"And I am going to say the last of it in this room and not in my barge, and it is the only thing I have got left to say this winter. Marr. You came in here in November and asked for a charter and got one and it was paid for in a herring berth and a mooring and nineteen winters, and I told the room the thing the yard wanted and the thing the town pays for are not the same thing, and I have said that twice since and I am saying it now for the third time about a piece of paper that is not mine. That paper is the answer to the coast office. Not this office's paper. A fisher's. And the yard has a wall and a book and a notice with a seal on a nail and three custody numbers in another office, and not one of those four things is a document anybody in Veyr asked for, and the yard cannot fix what the fisher wrote, and neither can this office, and neither can you.

"And what comes back from Veyr now decides whether there is a second season, and there may not be, and a second season is the whole of what a yard like this has, and you will find out about it in an office in a city you have not been in, and I have no part in it and no warning in it and no name on it. A one-season provisional working charter is not a permission to work when the holder feels like it. It is a date, and this one runs to the last of March, and after that it is somebody else's paper and a coast office in a city we have never been inside."

The north-easter came up outside with a bang and forty-one boats in the basin moved, and the harbor's tide bell went at half past nine, three strokes, in the road, and under the third stroke the water off the south end gave back three marks and a flick a hand's width of a second later, and the boy in the coat with his hand in his pocket knew what the delay was without counting it and did not write it down, because he was in a municipal hearing room with a ledger open on the table that was not his and a clerk writing in it who had not asked him for anything, and the twelfth occasion since the twenty-sixth of October was not entered anywhere at all.

Outside, on the mole, a hand lead was going over the wall's head every few minutes, and the north berth was out of service, and the ninth seam's mooring was in service and was a mooring and not a berth, and the first of March was a Wednesday and had arrived.
