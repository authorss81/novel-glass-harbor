# Chapter 88

*Two Marks and a Bar*

Harl Dace brought the *Grey Widow* out past the headland at a quarter past ten on Thursday the thirteenth with a fresh breeze in the north-east and a short sea outside the bar that the boat took without noticing, and Calder Marr found out on the way out what a working man means by *inshore*, because a man who has never been past the outer marks of a harbor thinks inshore means inside the mole and does not know that it means inside the water, and the difference between those two things on this coast is four miles of water and a gravel bar that a man can be aground on in a flat calm.

They had the yard's hand lead and the yard's slate and the boy to swing the lead, and no permit, because no permit exists and none was wanted, and no instrument of any office, because no office has anything to do with a bar of gravel. The whole of that water is inside the harbor line and inshore of the inner shoals, and the four-hundred-yard bar off the outer marks is a mile and a half further out with the cutter's claim lying over it, and nobody in that boat was near either and nobody in that boat was going to be. And there was no person in the water at any time in the day, and therefore there was no air limit upon any person and there was no seated weight and there was no line off a diver, and Nessa Pike said that out loud on the after deck before the lead went over, in a voice the whole boat could hear.

"There is no cutoff in this work," she said, "and I am not going to make one up so that this day looks like the other days, and if a man in a book in four years wants to know why this day has no cutoff in it, the reason is that the man swinging the lead is standing on a deck with both feet on it."

The lead was seven pounds and a line of fifteen fathoms marked with leather and bunting at every fathom, and the boy swung it from the boom with a technique nobody had taught him, because he had watched Tam Slee take one off the second seam on the Wednesday, a man who had been in that water forty years swinging over a fall into a flood that was running green over the stone, and because he had stood on a stone with a line in his hand since he was eleven, and the seventh swing was better than the sixth.

The first mark was where Harl Dace said it was.

The Old Custom House stands eleven feet above the rock and the men of that water call it a cairn because the stones are ballast out of a ship and not one man's work, and it dries at half-tide, and at half past twelve they lay alongside it and Calder Marr put a pole against the top course and read eleven feet out loud and the boy wrote eleven feet and the hour, and the weed on the bottom course was green and wet and the black on the top three courses was not weed, and Dace said nothing at all about it, which was the loudest thing that happened all day.

The second mark was where Harl Dace said it was, and the sand under it was white and hard, and nine fathoms of a ship's stem lay with its head up, and it is called the Accountant because it has been doing accounts on that coast since before anybody now working it was born, and the boy sounded round it four times: nine fathoms and a half at the north side, nine at the middle, eight and three-quarters on the seaward side, and the middle one is the one a man remembers.

Then the bar, and it was the reason for the day.

"It comes up to two and a half in the middle on a spring," Dace said, standing at the bow with his thumb. "Two and a half, and you will get two and three-quarters about an hour after the turn, and the two and a half is the figure in my head and the two and three-quarters is the figure on the water, and I have never once been wrong about which of the two a man wants."

At twenty past two the lead came up two and three-quarters. At half past it came up two and a quarter and the boy read it twice and Dace said nothing, because the ebb had begun and the bar was on the shoulder of a hard place and the difference between half past and twenty past was the whole of the argument.

At ten to three the lead fouled.

It went down and came up short, and the boy put his hand over the rail and felt for the line with the other and said fouled at four fathoms and a hand's width, and Calder Marr put a hand over his own and the two of them followed the line down by feel and could not get it, and Dace put the boat's boat alongside and they got a hook on it and brought up what was on it: a knuckle of kelp, a shoe, and a short length of marline with a splice in it gone hard as horn.

"That is a herring warp," Dace said. "That is somebody's four feet of winter warp and it has been in there long enough to grow a skin, and I will tell you what it means, and it is not what you came for. It means the bar is not clean, and it means somebody has lost ground tackle in it inside the last ten years, and it means that a boat that wanted to lie in the same soft ground at the bottom of a neap is going to have a fouled anchor in it and no bottom to let it go."

"Put it in the second column," the boy said, and put it in the second column, and the reason he gave was that a man on a mole in Morrow Quay cannot check whether a piece of marline is in a bar of gravel five miles north, and only one man in that boat can.

The third mark was not checked. The Kettle dries at low water springs and the date was not one of the three days in a month when it dries, and Dace said so before anybody asked him, and said that the yard could come back for it on a date in the next month or the month after, and that he would not come with them on a date they chose, because a man who chooses his own tide will come home with whatever he went out for.

"Say the rest of that for the book," the boy said, from the after deck, with the slate on his knee.

"The rest of it is that we have not surveyed a coast," Dace said. "We have gone eleven miles out and back and found two stones and a piece of timber and a bar of gravel, and every one of them is where a man told me they were, and that is a good day's work and it is worth exactly what it is. If you put *survey* on a wall about this, then in two years some man in Veyr will read *survey* and believe there is a paper somewhere with a chart of five miles of coast in it, and there is not, and there is a slate with three figures on it and a boy who cannot read well. There is no line here. There is a cairn and a stem and a bar and the rest of that coast is exactly where it was this morning, and the only man who knows the rest of it is the one standing at the bow of this boat, and he is a man of forty-eight and not a writer."

"And in four years," Calder Marr said, "he may not be."

"He may not," Dace said. "That is not a reason to write nothing. It is a reason to write down the day it stops being any use, and a man who does that can be trusted by anybody who comes after him, and a man who writes *survey* cannot."

Nessa Pike had the slate of limits on her knee with nothing on it, because there was nothing to write on it, and she said so to nobody in particular, in a voice that carried about two feet.

"That is the first day since the first of March," she said, "on which that slate was carried for nothing, and I would like it in the second column that it was carried for nothing, because in four years somebody will find a day with no limit on it and think somebody forgot."

---

At about half past three Calder Marr lost a figure.

Dace called a number across the deck at the third cast and Calder Marr did not have it, and asked for it again, and had it a second time and then a third, and Dace stopped and looked at him.

"You have asked me that three times," Dace said, "and the first time you were looking at me and the second time you were looking at the water and the third time you had your head turned, and I have sailed with men for thirty-one years and I know exactly what that is and I am not going to ask you about it on my own deck."

"You can ask me," Calder Marr said, "and I will answer you, and then it is in your book and I cannot get it back out, and I would rather it were in your book than in mine because yours goes nowhere."

"I have not got a book."

"Then it is in yours and nowhere," Calder Marr said, "and I will say it once and you may repeat it or not. There is a ringing in both ears, all the time, and a second sound under the first that arrives late, and on a good day I can tell which sound is in the boat and on a bad day I cannot, and it has been since the fourteenth of October and it is not going to stop. It is called a tide-sickness and I have got it from entering something I should not have entered, and I have not entered anything since, and it is the price of the one time I did."

Dace looked at him for a moment, and then said: "I have a man with it. He is fifty-one and he is a better hand on a lead than your boy and I do not put him on a lead, and he does not know that is why, and I have not told him, and I am not going to now, and I would thank you not to tell him either, because he would offer to do it and then he would do it badly." He turned round to the wheel. "So that is two of us on this coast who cannot hear properly and one of us put the other on the boat."

Nessa Pike had not turned round at any point and she said it to the wheel instead of to Calder Marr, which was the way she had said it to a harbor authority's man in a shed in February.

"It fills in when somebody says the sentence out loud and you can be told what it was," she said, "and a man who does that well has a trade and a man who does it badly has a disgrace, and there is no third thing, and it has not been named on this deck and it is not going to be entered in the book except in the words of the man who has it. Ask him at the top of the flood tomorrow and he will get most of it. Four days' work. Not a word more."

---

They were back on the mole steps at about six and the boy wrote the day in two columns and read it back, and it took twenty-five minutes and the light was going.

*First column, checkable by any man who can sail to that coast: the cairn on the Old Custom House, eleven feet by pole at half-tide, the stones ballast and not one man's work, the weed green on the bottom course and the top three courses black; the stem on the Nine Foot called the Accountant, nine fathoms, the middle cast of four, and the four casts; the bar at twenty past two, two and three-quarters, the figure the master gave as his own before the boat came out; the state of the water, a fresh breeze in the north-east and a short sea outside, and the hour of every cast; the lead, the yard's own, and the slate, the yard's own, and the man who swung it.*

*Second column, not checkable from a mole: what the shelf under the stem is doing; the state of the gravel on the bar's north shoulder; the fouling at four fathoms and a hand's width, being a knuckle of kelp, a shoe, and four feet of herring warp gone hard, which is a fact about somebody's loss and not about the road; and the bar's figure at half past, being two and a quarter, which is the ebb and which the man of the *Grey Widow* says is the shoulder and not the bar and which no man in this harbor can check because it was in the water for eleven minutes.*

The four who read it back were the boy, Dace, Pike and Calder Marr, and Dace read the second column twice and said one thing about it and it went in the first column because the man who said it was in the boat.

"It is a Tuesday's work," he said, "and it is not a survey, and I want that in your wall in my words, and I want it in your hand and not in mine, because a man who is paid for a survey says survey and a man who is not says what he did."

Then the money, and it was two marks out of a bag of five, and it was bread and a pot of tea and a hand's worth of coal in a stove in a boat, and the pot of air the day had run on was the one bought on the Tuesday before and the pot bought on the Friday was still standing in the shed behind the pump housing with the strap undone, and the two marks were the day's victuals, and the bag was three, and the note was thirty, and the tin had two crowns in it, and the pump had not been out of the shed in eight days, and the sea outside the bar had been short and hard all afternoon and the glass had been falling for ten days and still had not made up into a rain.
