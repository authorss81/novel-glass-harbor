# Chapter 52

*The Second Request*

The copying room at the Morrow circuit office was a long cold box with a counter down the middle of it and four stools on the far side and a window that gave onto a wall, and it was eight in the morning on Thursday the fifth of January and the room smelled of wet paper and paraffin and had been smelling of wet paper and paraffin since before Calder Marr was born, and the door of the room's own business stood open the whole time, which was a thing the room did in winter and did not do in June.

The copying clerk was a man of about fifty with a bad thumb and a habit of reading a document aloud while he looked at it, so that his mouth and his eyes stayed in agreement, and he put the second request on the counter face down, which Calder Marr noticed and did not comment on.

"It is a request," the clerk said. "It is the second one. The first was on the eleventh of November and it asked for two certifications and the tide conservator refused it in writing on four grounds, and that paper is in the file, and this one is dated the thirtieth of December and it came up the coast in an envelope with a conservator's address on it and it is not addressed to her, it is addressed to the party."

He turned it over.

"It is one page. It is in the office's own type. I am going to read it out with the door open because the door is open, and I am going to read it the way it is written and not the way it sounds, and if you ask me afterward how it sounds I will tell you, and it is not the same answer, and the difference between them is not mine."

---

He read it.

*The circuit office of the Lantern Registry, Morrow circuit, to the Free Keel yard of Morrow Quay, being the holder of a one-season provisional working charter of the twenty-eighth of November. The office has before it a public sheet in three parts, of the fourteenth of October, in a fair copy, and two crew copies of the same sheet printed without a name, and has noted that the sheet is in circulation in this harbor and in other harbors, and that six further copies of the same sheet have been carried by hand to persons and to crews. The office requires the party to deliver into the custody room of this office, for copying and verification, the said public sheet and the ten copies of it now held, or such of them as are held, so that the record of a contested material may be single and may be verified against the item entered as the third item on the custody log of this office.*

*The office further requires that a party declining this request shall state its ground in writing within fourteen days of the date of this paper, and that a party not so stating shall be treated as not having declined, and the request shall stand. A request returned without a statement of ground shall be returned with a second seal, and the second seal shall be a seal of delay.*

That is the whole of it. It is four paragraphs and a date and it takes about a minute and a half to read.

Calder Marr read it a second time off the clerk's mouth, which was the custom, and then stood still for a moment with his hands on the counter.

"That last paragraph," he said. "Read that last paragraph again."

The clerk read it again. He read it at the same pace, which was the trouble.

"Read it a third time and I would like you to say, between the sentences, what a man has to do."

"To state his ground in writing within fourteen days of the date of this paper, or be treated as not having declined, in which case the request stands."

"And if a man states his ground in writing, what happens to the ground."

"It goes in the file. It is answered when the office answers it, and the office does not give a time for answering it." The clerk put his thumb against the page. "And the second seal is a seal of delay, and a seal of delay is the only seal in this office that is not a refusal, and a man who has been delayed is a man who has not refused, and a man who has not refused has a request standing against him that he did not answer, and that is what it is for. I am a clerk. I fill in the box. I did not write it and I do not know who did and I would not put a name to it if I did."

"Who signs it."

"The seal line at the foot."

"And the seal line is the same one that has been on every paper this office has sent to this harbor since the fourteenth of October."

"It is."

"Mara Venn is not the addressee."

"No."

"And she is named in the copy."

The clerk turned the sheet over and read a line near the foot, and it said that the party is advised that the tide conservator named in the paper of the eleventh of November declined a related request in writing on four grounds, and that the party is further advised that the said conservator is not a person through whom this office can be answered, and that the office has not addressed this paper to her and will not.

"She is named in it as a door that has already been tried," Calder Marr said.

"She is named in it as a person who has declined a related request, and the office does not treat a refusal as a comment on a person," the clerk said, "and I will read you the exact words again if you want them, and they are the words on the paper, and I am not going to say a better sentence than the one that is written."

---

It cost three marks to have the request entered and dated, and Calder Marr paid it.

He had thought about it for eleven minutes on the walk, which is a quarter of a mile longer than the walk, and he had done the sum in his head twice: the bag had five marks in it since the first of December, and the yard had asked a man yesterday to write a worse figure into his own book, and the fee was the cheapest thing an office sells, and it is the only thing in this room that is a fact.

The clerk booked it, and the fee came back, and Calder put it in the inside pocket of his coat where the crew's book is not and where the note is when the note is with him.

"Three out," the clerk said, "and it is entered against you as the fee for the entering and dating of a request, and the request is dated the thirtieth of December, and the day you paid for it is the fifth of January, and both of those dates go on one line, and the line is in the book, and if anybody in this building ever tells you that the request is of today's making, you have a piece of paper that says otherwise."

"Read that to me again."

The clerk read the line back. Calder Marr wrote it into his own left hand on the back of his own sheet, and put the sheet in the book, and would read it to the yard in the evening and the boy would read it after him twice, which is the rule and is not going to be broken because a man has had two marks.

"The bag is two," the clerk said, not unkindly, because clerks in copying rooms are not unkind, they are employed.

"Yes," Calder Marr said. "The bag is two and the tin is empty and it has been empty since the eighteenth of November, and I will say both of those figures in my own shed tonight in front of three people, which is where they should be said and not at a counter in a room that belongs to the office that wrote the paper."

"You will do what you like in your shed," the clerk said. "But I will tell you what I would do, since nobody has told me to and I am putting nothing in a box about it. The cost of this paper is not the three marks. The three marks is the cheapest part. The cost of this paper is the fortnight, and every day of the fortnight you spend deciding, and on the fifteenth day, which is the thirteenth of January, you either have a ground in writing or you have a request standing against you that you did not answer, and on the twenty-ninth day, which is the twenty-seventh, the second seal comes, and then it is a month, and then it is a quarter, and the thing you are trying to protect is being taken away from you at the rate of one week for every week you spend in this room."

"You have been in this office a long time," Calder said.

"Twenty-six years," the clerk said, "and I have watched about nine men be ground down by a fortnight and I have watched two of them be ground down by a year, and the two were the ones who were angry, and I am telling you that because you have not been angry once in the eight minutes you have been at my counter, and a man who is not angry has a chance, and it is a small one."

---

Mara Venn was outside in the road, in the cold, on the wrong side of the street for the door, with her bag shut.

"You paid it," she said.

"Three marks, to have it entered and dated. That is the cheapest thing in that room and it is the only thing in there that is a fact."

"That is a very good reason and it is not the reason." She turned to face him. "Mr Marr, I am going to say four sentences in this road and then I am going to walk to the fish market and buy a thing I do not need, because I have not eaten since yesterday and there is a rule about the fourth of January that I have kept for eight years. You are not going to thank me and I do not want you to, so do not start.

"One. That request is not for me and I am not the addressee and I cannot answer it and there is nothing in the world I can do with it. Two. I am named in the copy as a person who has already declined a related request, which means the office has spent me once and cannot spend me twice, and that is worth a great deal to you and it is worth exactly nothing to me, because the spending was done in October and it was done by the office and I was the instrument. Three. I have no standing to advise you and I have not got one and I am not going to pretend to have found one this morning to be useful. And four: do not send that paper to a man who will want to know whether you meant to refuse it, and there is no such man in Morrow Quay, and if there is, he will ask you in a market instead, in front of people, and there will be no clerk present, and whatever you say there will be the record."

"You have told me three things I knew and one thing I did not," Calder said.

"I have told you four things and you had three of them and I would like credit for the third, which is that the spending was done in October." She shifted the bag. "And now I am going to ask you the question you came here to ask, because you have not asked it and you have been standing in a road for four minutes, and I would rather you asked it than stood there being honest at me."

"I am going to ask you what a certificate costs," Calder Marr said, "and I have no right to ask you that, and I am asking it."

---

A certificate of method, or of a thing's fitness to be moved or handled, was six marks and three weeks.

She said the six first, and then the three, and she said that the six was the office's and the three was the queue, and that neither of them was hers and that in October there had been a quarter of a standing left, which had made the three weeks feel like a formality, and that now the three weeks were the whole of it.

"Six marks," she said, "is eleven weeks of this yard's January. I have done the sum and I did it twice, and I want you to know that I did it, and I am telling you the number and not the sum, because a figure is not a refusal and a sum would be."

"You have given me a figure for something nobody has asked you for."

"Yes. A figure is a thing I can still give and it costs me nothing, and it is the last thing I can do in a Registry coat, and I would like it entered in your book as a figure and not as a favor, because a favor is a thing that can be taken back and a figure cannot." She put her bag down and took it up again. "And when a shipwright in this harbor wants a certificate of method in about four months, you will come to me, and I will not be able to say no, and that will be because of a word in a room in the west of this city and not because of anything I did, and I have decided in advance that I will not let that make me say no, because a woman who says no to everything is easier to write down than a woman who is expensive."

That is what a conservator is, Calder Marr thought, and then stopped himself from thinking the rest of it, because the rest of it was about the fourteenth of October and about a bench in a shed at four in the morning, and there is a rule in this yard that has stood since the eighth of December, and the rule is that a thing is told once to one person in a private scene at a price, and it may not be told a second time for nothing.

"Thank you," he said, and it was not about the figure.

"Don't," Mara Venn said. "Not for that. For a certificate, and not in a road, and with a day in it that I get to choose."

She walked off up the hill toward the fish market, and Calder Marr stood in the road with two marks in his pocket and a piece of paper in his book and the whole of a fortnight in front of him, and the arithmetic of it arrived at him in the road and was this: the yard had five marks on the first of December and had two marks on the fifth of January, and one of the three had bought a date, and the date was worth three marks, and the fortnight was worth whatever the yard could earn in a fortnight, and on the fourth of January it had been shown to him that the yard cannot earn anything in a fortnight.

The copy went in the book. The line was read back twice. And at the bottom of the page, in the copying clerk's own hand, the office entered one more thing, which nobody had asked for and which Calder Marr read twice before he left the counter.

*Entered, this office, the fifth of January. The said request is of the thirtieth of last month, was received by the party on the fourth instant, and was not answered on the day of receipt, and the fourteen days run from the date of the paper and not from the date of receipt, and six of them are gone, and six remain, and the fourteenth is the thirteenth of January.*
