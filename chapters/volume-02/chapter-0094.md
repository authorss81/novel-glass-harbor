# Chapter 94

*Friday, the Twenty-Eighth*

The clerk of the harbor authority came up the hill at eight in the morning on Thursday the twenty-seventh of April with a rule on a board and a page he had ruled himself, and he came up the hill because the yard would not come down, and both of those things were said out loud at the door before anybody was inside.

"I have two things and neither of them is a chair," the clerk said. "One of them is a claim window for tomorrow on this office's own ground, and the other one is that this office has received an application for a provisional writ and the sitting is on Monday the first of May at ten and there is a fee."

"Read them," Calder Marr said. "And read the fee before either of the other two, because the fee is the only one of the three that is a fact."

The fee was one shilling and sixpence. It was entered in the office's schedule of charges in the schedule's own hand and it was not returnable, and the clerk said out loud on the steps that this office did not waive it and could not if it wanted to, because the schedule was made by somebody who was dead, and that it was the only money that had gone into that office's account in this harbor since the middle of February, and that he would like the room to understand what that was: a mark and sixpence, and a reeve's coat that had been to a tailor in Veyr in February.

Nessa Pike went down the hill with it at a quarter past eight and came back at half past with the tin.

The tin had two crowns in it and it had held two crowns since the first of February, and it was the last thing in that shed that had not been opened since the year turned, and she took it down in front of three people and did it without saying anything at all about it, and put one shilling and sixpence on the step for the clerk and took the rest back inside and put it where it had been.

"That is a mark of the yard's out of a tin that was not a mark of the yard's," she said, "and the tin is the only thing in this shed that had not been touched since the first of February, and it has now been touched once, and there is a table in that office with a line on it and the line says *and that is all*."

---

The air was the argument, and it went on for an hour and a half, and the reason it went on for an hour and a half is that both of them were right and the thing could not be made right.

The pot had been standing in the shed behind the pump housing with the strap undone and nothing done to it since Friday the seventh of April, when Calder Marr had put two marks out of a bag of twelve into a pot in a chandler's shop because a day was coming on the Thursday of the following week that would want a pot filled, and a pot is filled before it is wanted, and there was no pot in this yard that was filled on the day it was wanted.

"It is three weeks old," Nessa Pike said. "Whatever is in it is three weeks old. Air does not sit in a pot for three weeks in a shed in April and stay the same, and I cannot tell you how it will be and neither can you and neither can the man who sold it to you, and if I write *air* on a slate tomorrow and four hundred pounds of that pot goes into a man on the bottom of the mole's toe at four fathoms, then the slate was a lie and the day was a lie and the yard is a thing that writes down lies and does it well."

"Then we go without it," Calder Marr said, "and the ground stays where it is, and the air that is in the other pot is the pot that ran the fifth of April and it has two thirds of a day in it, and I have done the sum out loud and a day of two divers is a morning."

"No," said Nessa Pike. "We go with it. And we go with it, and I am going to write the doubt down in the first column, in the yard's own hand, in Calder Marr's own words, and read it out at the mole at eleven o'clock in the morning in front of two divers and a tender and the clerk, so that every man who is going to be in the water knows what the air is, and so that in four years a man with a file can find out that the Free Keel yard had a pot that was three weeks old and used it anyway and said so out loud."

"That is putting the worst of it on a wall."

"It is putting the *shape* of it on a wall," she said. "The worst of it is that we had nothing. The shape of it is that we went anyway and said what we had."

The boy wrote it and it went in the reason, and the reason was on a sheet in Calder Marr's own left hand, and the clerk read it out in the hearing room over the tide-gang store at half past nine on the Thursday morning with the slate wall behind the reeve's bench bare and wiped at eight and the ledger on the table, and the ledger is the record.

---

*That on Friday the twenty-eighth of April instant the party is delivering its own air and its own pump to this office's own ground between the third and fourth seams of the mole, the party not being on this office's work and not going on it, the party standing on that ground as the owner of hired gear and not as a workman, and if this office requires a different reading of that the office's reeve will say so now and the party will stand somewhere else; and that the person in the water is proposed to be two men hired of the *Grey Widow* and of this harbor's own tide gang, being Tam Slee and Wil Slee, and that neither is of this party; and that the limit of each is his own and was fixed by him at the boat before anybody was wet; and that the tender is Neale, foreman of this office's tide gang, and is not a diver, and the cutoff is his to pull without asking anybody; and that the air is the party's own, out of a pot bought on the seventh of April and standing three weeks in the party's shed, and the party says so out loud at the mole before anybody is wet, in its own words and in these words; and that nothing is to be raised off the bedded material itself, which is inside the first clause of a notice this office did not write and which this office has no power to lift, and that the ground worked is the reach and the old ground tackle lying on the face and nothing under it.*

Kest entered the sixth of his own acknowledgements after it, in his own hand, and read it back, and it said that the first clause of the notice of the thirty-first closes the mole's toe and inner face from the third seam to the eleventh to all work and to all approach by all persons, which includes this office and this yard and any other crew in this harbor, and that this office has no power to lift that notice and is not lifting it and has never lifted it, and that this office is issuing an instrument that will put two men in the water and a tender on a boat over ground that first clause closes, and is treating the whole of the mole from the third to the eleventh as inside that clause whether the toe is inside it or not, because a conservative reading of a notice is not a narrow one; and that this is the sixth entry this office has made of putting itself inside a closure it did not create, the first five being on a permit of the eighth of January, a reason of the twelfth, a claim window of the seventeenth, a line under a reason on the nineteenth and a claim window of the seventh of April, and **that this one was asked for again by a party, which is the second time, and that the cost of it is his and always was and is never free.**

"I have a question that is mine and not the clerk's," Kest said, "and I am going to ask it in the barge and not on that page, and the answer is yours and it is short.

"On Friday morning at eleven o'clock there will be a boat on the mole's toe with two men in the water, and the tender will be this office's own foreman, and two men are going to be sent to that office out of this office's tide gang, and the yard is not on it and the yard's gear is on it. **The yard has the best of it.** In January, in February and on the seventh of April this yard put a hand lead on ground I had closed and I had to write a line in my own book about it. Tomorrow the yard puts its air on the same ground, and it puts it there for a fee, and I am the man who signs the order that pays the fee. That is not a favor, and it is not a breach, and I want you to be able to say in four years that you asked for a job and were given a bill, and that the bill was at your own rate and not at mine, and that the whole of it is in a book and not in a conversation."

"Then put that in the book as well," Calder Marr said, "because you have never once said a true thing to me that you did not write down after you had said it, and if you leave it in a barge it will be gone by June and you will have told me it once and there will be no trace of it and a man in Veyr will read five lines and not six."

Kest looked at him for a moment.

"That is fair," he said, "and it is going in, and I am going to write it in my own hand and I am going to be annoyed about it for about a fortnight."

---

They were on the mole's toe at five to eleven on Friday the twenty-eighth of April with the pot open and the pump running and the boiler lit at twenty past ten and the wind in the north-east at about three and the sea outside the bar up and white.

The air came out of the pot at half past eleven and the boy had it in a bottle before anybody breathed it, and Calder Marr read out what Nessa Pike had made him write, which was that the air was out of a pot bought on the seventh of April and standing three weeks in the party's shed, and that he was not going to pretend to a man on a mole that he knew what was in it, and that it was a pot and not a promise.

Nessa Pike said, "That is read out on a mole, once, by a man who is not the diver, and the man in the water hears it and the tender hears it and the man who will be in the boat hears it, and I want four men to be able to say in four years that it was read out on a mole on the twenty-eighth of April before anybody was wet, and I want the word *once* entered, because if we are going to do it on the ninth of May I would like somebody to know I only do it once."

The boy read the reason out twice on his own, because she made him.

At eleven the bell on the third lay's wall went, three strokes, and Calder Marr heard the water off the south end give it back a hand's width of a second later, and he turned his head for it out of thirty-one years of doing it and he was close, and the boy said "I could not take it" and Calder Marr said "I could not either" and they stood there for a moment with nothing to say about it, and then the boy wrote in the second column that the delay of the bell on the mole's toe on the morning of the twenty-eighth of April could not be taken, and gave the reason, which was that the air was about to be used and the pump was running and there were four men on a mole with a line in the water and a man on the bottom of it, and that the boy would take the delay at the top of the flood on a flat calm day with nothing else going on, and that he was not going to take it in a hurry.

The two went over at eleven and stayed down twenty-six minutes and came up with a chain shackle and a turn of old cable and a lump of the mole's own rubble with a bit of green glass in it, which was put in a bucket and not looked at, because the second clause of the notice of the thirty-first keeps recovered transparent mineral out of every shed in this harbor and the yard was not going to have a conversation about a lump of glass in a bucket in a boat on the mole's toe.

The second went down at ten past twelve and the two went to the bedded material and stopped on the edge of it and did not go under it, because the reason said nothing under it, and worked the face of it for an hour and came up with four hundred and forty pounds of old iron, in three pieces, and one of them was a bell-metal gun-port off something with a gun in it, and none of it was glass and all of it was heavy.

Neale held the tender's line and did not go in the water and said, at about one o'clock, to nobody: "I have read a tide-gang notice in my life and I have never once been the name on it. Thank you for writing me down before you wrote yourselves down."

The eighth and the share came off the top of a day rate, the air at the yard's own two marks and the pump at the yard's own four, and the harbor authority paid both on the office's own account and not out of any fund of the yard's, and the clerk entered the hire and the rate and the two lines of the reason that said the party was not on the office's work, and read them back, and the wall was bare and the ledger had it.

---

At four o'clock they were still on it and Calder Marr was on the mole's steps with a coil of line in his hand and the boy beside him with a slate, and the wind had gone round half a point and was coming heavier, and the pot was nearly empty and the boiler was low and the steam was making when a man came round the north wall on foot with a handbell in his hand and rang it twice, hard, and it was not a handbell in that district and there was not a fire in either lay that anybody knew of, and a man of fifty-one with a bad wheel came round behind him with his own bell and rung it too, because he had said in a market on the fifteenth of April that he would.

Neale came up out of the boat and did not say one word about it, and the fourth man's face was of a piece with the sky, and Calder Marr looked out along the north wall and found what it was.

The third lay's boat had a chain on her quarter, and it was not on a bollard, and it was not round a post, and it was not on anything at all. It was round the ninth seam's mooring pole, which is a mooring and not a berth, and it had been the mooring of a herring boat since the seventeenth of February, and there are sixteen feet between a bollard and a bollard on that mole, and whoever put that chain round that pole had put it round the only thing on that stretch of stone that was not going anywhere.

"She wants water on the other side," Ewan Tristram said, over the wind. "That is a boat in a half sea with the wind right on her beam and her chain on a pole, and she will come across that wall at the top of this tide and take the north wall off this harbor like a bucket, and there is thirty-seven feet of the harbor's own marline in the sea between that wall and the bar from the twenty-seventh of February and there will be seventy before this is over."

They were at half past five. The half-tide is at ten past six, and Calder Marr said it out loud for the yard and got the hour and not the tide, and then went down the steps at about two minutes past six with the two men and Neale and the clerk and the boy, and there was a foot on a boat between the second lay's slip and the ninth seam's pole at about a minute past six, and it was eight and a half feet, and it was a boy of twenty-two in a bad hand with a knife and a shackle, and he did not finish the shackle.
