# Chapter 82

*Six Hours of Flood*

Iven Toft came back up the hill at half past six on Wednesday the fifth of April with his twenty marks in the same leather bag and said that he had been to four yards and the harbor's own pump was spoken for by a man with money, and that he was not going to be quoted a different price for having been kept waiting, and that he wanted the same figure or he would go to a man with a lighter full of gear.

"Same figure," Calder Marr said. "Twenty, and the eighth and the share off the top of it, and that is the way it has been taken off a fisherman's own account in this yard twice in March and it is not a courtesy."

"The eighth and the share," Toft said, and set the bag down on the bench harder than he had meant to. "You have no charter."

"No."

"Then the eighth of what? The share of what? There is no paper in this harbor that says a man on the second seam owes a fifth of himself to a council that has not been told anything since the twenty-eighth of November."

"There is no paper in this harbor that says a man on the second seam owes the harbor authority a fifth of himself, and the notice of the thirty-first is a paper that says this yard is not to be worked on the authority's work, and I have read both of them and neither one of them is the reason." Nessa Pike had not looked up from the bench. "The reason is that the mole is the harbor's, the tide gang is the harbor's, the warp on the north wall is the harbor's, and the wall is standing up because men are paid to keep it standing up, and a man who argued this yard out of the eighth in April because the yard has no paper would be arguing himself out of a tide gang in November. I am not taking the eighth off a fisherman's own account to pay this harbor's wages. I am taking it because the harbor has been paid its eighth for as long as there has been a yard on this dock, and a yard with no charter is not a yard with no customs, and if you want the argument in a book I will write it out and you may put your name against it or not."

Toft thought about it for about four seconds, which was longer than most men on this dock gave it.

"Write it out," he said.

"Enter it in the first column," Nessa Pike said, "because the man who said it is standing in the shed and a man in this harbor can check it by asking him tomorrow."

The reason went into the yard's own book at eight in the morning, in Calder Marr's own left hand, in six lines, and the boy read it back. It named the second seam of the mole's own face, and said that the ground is landward of the third seam and is not within the first clause of the notice of the thirty-first, and that no instrument of any office is used, no permit is held and none is asked for, and that nothing is to be put in the water that is not named in the line, and that the work is a herring boat's own ring and about six feet of her own warp upon her own account and for no other person. And it said the party in the water is proposed to be Tam Slee, hired out of the *Grey Widow* and not of this party, and that the limit is fixed by the diver at the boat, and that the tender is Nessa Pike and is not the diver, and that the cutoff is hers to pull without asking anybody.

And in the third line Nessa Pike made the boy read out loud, by herself, twice: that no person is proposed to go under at any time in this work, that there is therefore no air limit upon any person, that the party is not asking this office for anything and this office is not named in the reason, and that the reason is written here because the yard keeps the practice in its own book and will not have the two confused.

"That is the third time I have made somebody read a line twice on its own," the boy said.

"Then it is the third time it was worth it," she said.

---

They were on the second seam's ground at ten minutes to eleven with the flood making hard up the mole's face, and the pump was on a cart with the housing that had come up the coast in a creditor's own handcart on the eighth of March, and the wheel turned true and did not stop, which is a thing a pump can do and people who have never had one find surprising.

Iven Toft was on his own ground with his own ring and his own about four feet of fouled warp gone, and he was fifty and he had a bad thumb and he stayed out of the way in the way a man does who has done this before, and at eleven o'clock Tam Slee went over the side on the guide.

Bevin Rook sat on a stone at the head of the mole with the slate tied to the book on his knees and a good wrist on the wall, and he called the hour and the state of the water every four minutes without being asked and was not told once that he was doing it wrong, and on the fourth call the flood had made from the west and was beginning to run green over the seam's face, and on the ninth there was a hard patch of kelp coming down on the ebb side of the ring, and on the fourteenth the water under the ring was going to three and a half and the diver said so and nobody repeated it back at him because he was not the tender.

Nessa Pike had the cleat, the fall and the slate of limits, and she said the thing out loud at the head of the mole before anybody was in the water, in a voice carried the length of the seam, because she had learned in February that a man hears a thing differently when it is said to him rather than written for him afterward.

"Twenty-six minutes, set by him at the boat, and he will not take forty off anybody, and the air is the boat's air and nobody else's, and I pull when I pull and not when he would like it." She turned round to the stone. "And if I pull it, nobody in this yard and nobody in this harbor gets to be angry with me about it afterward, including you, and you may enter that I said it in those words if you have a column for it."

They came up at twenty-two minutes with a shackle of the mole's own rubble in the eye of it and nine feet of warp, and the splice good, and the ring clear under a shelf of the mole's own stone with nothing fouled on it that had not been there on Saturday, and the pump stopped at two o'clock and the hose was coiled and the cart was up the slope by half past, and the whole of it took five hours and forty minutes and cost the yard a day it could not have sold at any price.

The entry went in two columns and it took forty minutes to write out, and the four who read it back were the boy, Tam Slee, Nessa Pike and Iven Toft, and Harl Dace came up the hill at about three and read it as the fifth without being asked and stayed twenty minutes and asked two questions, both of them about the water and neither of them about the ring.

*First column, checkable from the mole: the diver, Tam Slee, hired out of the *Grey Widow* and paid at the figure named in the reason, and not of the party; the tender, Nessa Pike, and the cutoff hers; no person in the water; in at ten minutes past eleven and up at twenty-two minutes, that being the limit he set himself at the boat; the moon, four days off new; the flood, hard, off the west, and the state of the water at every fourth minute; the pump, the yard's own, hired to the party for the day at four marks and worked for three hours and ten minutes, and the housing fitted on the eighth of March out of nineteen marks of iron on the yard's note; the eighth and the share off the top of a day rate, and the reason for taking them without a charter, in the party's own words, with the man who asked said to be against it; the four feet of warp and the ring as found; the men of this harbor's tide gang on the face, being Neale at the ring bolt and two others, the gang being three men short from the first of April out of an account that has been empty since the middle of February.*

*Second column, not checkable from a mole: what was under the shelf of the mole's own stone before the shackle went onto the ring, which is the only figure in this entry no man on this mole could have told anybody; the state of the inner face of the ring; what the water was doing under the shelf at eighteen minutes, which goes in the second column because a man in a boat can see it and cannot stop it; and the diver's own figure for the depth of the shelf, which is three and a quarter, and which is his, and which goes in as his.*

"Three and a quarter and my twenty-two minutes are in different columns and neither of them is scratched out," Slee said, "and I have been in this water forty years and I have never once had a man write down a number of mine and then not use it."

Toft read it twice more and asked for the eighth and the share to be set out at the head in a line of its own, which it was.

"So that is what I have bought," he said, when the pencil was down. "A shackle, four feet of my own warp, a pump I could not get anywhere else, and a page with five men on it."

"That is what anybody in this harbor buys," Dace said, from the back. "I have bought eleven of them since January and I have paid for every one, and I could show you the eleven in a chest in my own boat if you wanted to see them, and they are worth exactly what they are worth and no more, and the part that is worth most is the one where a man can read it afterward and say I have done this before and I have not done it the same way twice."

---

The boy's wrist was nine weeks old on the Wednesday and it had taken four hours of a slate and a stone to make it ache, and he said nothing about it at the time and said it all afterward in the second column, in his own right hand, and the reason he gave for it being in the second column was that no man on that mole could have looked at a boy's wrist at four-minute intervals on a flood and known anything about it.

Nessa Pike read that line twice and then said one sentence to the back of the boy's head, which was that if the bonesetter's book says a boat hand in June then the boy was to be off the stone and on something else by the middle of May whatever his own arithmetic said, and the boy said that his own arithmetic had nothing to do with a bonesetter's book and that the two of them would be kept apart in the yard's book the way the two counts of the note are kept apart.

---

The money was read out on the Wednesday night and the boy read it twice and then read the position, and it was the shortest column since the eighth of March.

"Gross on the board, twenty, and every mark of it is a herring boat's own money on her own account, and not a word of it is the harbor's. The eighth two, the share two, air two, rope a mark, Tam Slee two, and the pump four, hired and not lent. That is thirteen out of twenty and the yard's is seven.

"The bag was five this morning and it is twelve. The note is at twenty-seven and the three counts of its weeks have not moved and will not be moved by anything I do with a pencil. The tin has two crowns in it that went in on the first of February.

"And I am entering the ninth week of the wage in the column with no adjectives in it, and the tenth week falls on Tuesday the eleventh and will be entered the same way, and I have not asked and nobody has told me a date, and nobody in this yard is going to ask me about it."

"Read the owing back," Nessa Pike said, and the boy read it, and it is the part he reads slowest, and it is the part of a book that no employer in this coast would have had printed.

Then the last of the day, and it was the only thing in the shed that was not money.

Alden Marr was at the far bench when the boat came back and he was at the far bench when the money was read, and he had not spoken to anybody since the thirteenth of November, and nobody in that shed asked him anything, and Calder Marr did not go over to him. Down at the head of the dock a light was burning in the *Grey Widow*'s wheelhouse and the boy could see it from the mole steps, and the glass had been falling since Friday and had not made up into a rain yet, and there is a kind of weather on this coast that men look for in April and do not always find, and two boats of the herring fleet had gone out on the Friday and were not in, and the third lay was full, and the mole-head light was not on and would not be, because a light on the mole head costs oil and the harbor authority's repair account had been empty for seven weeks.
