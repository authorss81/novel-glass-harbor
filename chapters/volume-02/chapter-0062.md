# Chapter 62

*A Pump That Works*

The clerk came up the fish dock at eight on Monday the twenty-third of January with the cloth off the hearing room's slate wall and a bucket under his arm, and he did it whether there was a sitting or not, and the yard's three figures came off the board in about ninety seconds, and Calder Marr stood in the road and watched it happen and did not say anything clever about it, because a man who says something clever about a wiped board is a man who thinks the board was the record.

"It was never the record," Bevin Rook said, coming out with the book under his arm. "The board is a convenience and the book is the record and both of those are on the wall itself in Calder's hand. The first of March is the next wipe and there is no sitting called and I would like it written down that a figure on that board is a figure for a week and a figure in this book is a figure for good, and I have said this before and I am saying it again in front of a man who has never heard me say it."

"Who is that," the clerk said.

"That is the yard," Nessa Pike said, from the door, and the clerk went down the dock with his bucket and did not stay, and nobody in the yard was thanked by anybody and nobody asked to be.

---

Noll Tarner came up at half past nine on the Monday and again at four, and on the Tuesday at eight, and the third time he came with a sheet of paper in a waxed jacket which he put on the bench face down and did not explain.

"Two things and then I will let you work," he said on the Tuesday morning. "The first one is the reason."

"You cannot write a reason."

"I cannot write a reason. I have never written a reason. I have never been asked for one, because I have never been inside a harbor authority's book in my life, and on Thursday I am going to be, and if the book says there is a reason and there is no reason then I have put a man in the water with nothing against his name." He put his thumb on the back of the sheet. "So I have written one. On this. I want one of you to tell me whether it is a reason or whether it is a sentence I have made up because I have been frightened since Friday."

Bevin Rook turned the sheet over with two fingers of his right hand and read it, and he read it twice, and then he put it down flat and squared it to the edge of the bench.

"That is a sentence," he said. "It is a good sentence and it is true and it is not a reason. A reason says what is being done and who is doing it and how long and where. You have written *a fouled ring on a herring man's warp at the twelfth seam.* That is what is being done. It is one line and there is no one in it and no length in it and no tide in it, and if you put that in this harbor authority's book on Thursday morning the clerk will copy it out and it will be a record of a man who turned up on Thursday and did a thing to a ring, and it will read in four years in Veyr like a man who was not there."

"Then write one."

"No," Bevin Rook said. "I am not going to write your reason and I am going to tell you why, and then I am going to tell you what I will do instead, and you can decide this morning which of the two you want."

"Say the why first."

"Because I did it once," the boy said, "on the nineteenth of January, for a man in this room, and he wrote it in his own left hand at eight o'clock with the clerk standing there, and it is in that book with the hour on it, and I read it out four times. And it has a hole in it. It says what was done and it does not say why anybody wanted it done, and the reason is in his head and not in the paper, and I entered the hole in our book afterwards in my own right hand with the date on it, because a reason with a hole in it that nobody has written down is a hole, and a man who finds a hole in six months can write anything he likes in it."

Tarner looked at the boy for a while.

"You are twenty-two," he said.

"I am twenty-two and I have a hand that will not close and I am the only person in this yard that can read, and that is two facts and neither of them is why."

"Then what is the second thing you would do instead."

"I would come down to your boat on Wednesday evening," Bevin Rook said, "and you would tell me in your own words what is being done, and I would write it, and it would go in your hand and not in mine, because a reason that a man cannot write is a reason a man has not given. And when you have said it, if what you have said is thin, I am going to say so, and if it is a hole I am going to say that too and I am going to say it in front of Calder and in front of her, and it will be entered in this book with the date, and it will not be kept out of this book because it is yours and not ours."

"And if it is a hole?"

"Then it is a hole with a name on it," Nessa Pike said, "which is a different thing from a hole in a stranger's reason that a man finds in six months, and the difference is four marks and a day of a boat, and I would like it noted that I am the one who priced that."

---

The second thing was not the reason, and it took until about eleven.

"Whose book," Tarner said.

"Ours, on the day, for the labor," Calder Marr said. "The claim is yours, on your own window, and the harbor authority's ledger carries your claim, and the eighth and the share come off the top of the day because that is how this yard works and you have read the wall so you know it. But the air, the depth, the hour, the figures, and what came up — that is written by the crew that was in the water, and the crew that was in the water on Thursday is going to be this crew, and this is the only book on this coast that anybody reads out loud."

"So a man can come and read my dive in your book."

"Yes."

"And if the figures in it are bad, a man reads them against me."

"Yes," Calder Marr said, "and that is the correct answer and I am not going to soften it, because a book that a man can only read good things in is not a record, it is an advertisement, and this book has had an advertisement on a wall next to it since the seventeenth of December and I have spent a month wishing it would go away."

Tarner put both hands on the bench.

"You have a plate under a mole in this harbor that nobody is allowed to touch," he said, "and two plans for it, and both refused, and the reason is on your wall. I have read it. I read the sheet in the market on Friday with a man who was standing next to me and I did not understand the half of it and I understood all of it. So I know what a bad figure in a good book looks like, and I know that you have one, and you have it in the open, and the *Vigil*'s paper is nailed over the top of it with four inches of gap so a man has to lift a naval document to get at your own confession." He straightened up. "I am not asking you to keep my dive out of it. I am asking you the other question, and nobody in this harbor has asked me it and I have been up and down this dock for four days."

"Ask it."

"Whose day is it," Tarner said. "On Thursday. Twenty-six marks comes out of my pocket for a day that goes in your book, and the eighth and the share go to a harbor authority that has my ring and not my warp, and the mooring I am there to free belongs to a skipper called Pringle who has not paid me for it since November and knows exactly what he is doing. So: whose day is it. And when the man comes with the notebook, does he come to *Sea Wren* or does he come to this shed, and what does he write, and whose name is at the top of it."

It was Bevin Rook who answered, and he took about twenty seconds over it, which was longer than he usually took.

"Both," he said. "And I will tell you what I write, and then you can decide, and if you do not like it you can go to another reader in this harbor, and there is not one, and I would rather you knew that before Thursday than on Thursday.

"The top will say the claim is the *Sea Wren's* and the work was on her own window and the day was paid by Noll Tarner out of her account. Then the crew in the water and the crew tending and who held the cleat and who kept the book. And then it comes in two columns, and this is the part you will not like. The first is what I could check from the boat: the depth, the hour, the length of ground, what came up, who was in it. The second is what I could not, which is the bottom under a fouled ring, the state of the shackle below the ring, and what the water is doing at four feet on a rising easterly." He put his hand flat on the book. "In October a man in this yard worked out a rule and it has not been broken since. A diver may say his own number out loud, and it only goes in the book if a man on a mole can check it. Your count of that bottom goes in this book and the yard's count of the same bottom goes in this book, and if they differ they are both in and neither of them is scratched out, and in four years a man can put them side by side and know that two men were in a boat."

"That is a fair thing to have asked for and it is not a small one."

"It is not a small one and it is the only reason I would agree to it," the boy said. "Because if your dive is in my book and I ever want to be a man who can read, then a man has to be able to read me, and I would rather find that out in this shed on a Tuesday in January than in a room in Veyr in four years."

---

Then Tarner said the thing he had come to say twice, and on the third telling it came out.

"The cover."

"You have none," Nessa Pike said. "You have had none since the twenty-ninth of October, and the only cover this harbor has for any of us is Harl Dace's out of the *Grey Widow*, inside the line and not past the marks, and he has not been asked and this yard does not know whether it stands after the week in which a boy put a ruined arm into a fall. So we cannot give you cover and you are not being told no by a man who thinks you should not have it. We are being told there is none to have."

"I am aware there is none. I have been trying to buy one out of Lowfen since November and there is nobody to sell to." He looked at his own hands on the bench. "Which brings me to the part I have been ashamed of for four days. I have not been on the Free Keel's slate since November. My name came off it in a net loft with a line under, and I did not appeal, and two men in this harbor have told me not to trust you and one man has told me not to tell you what I am doing, and I have been sitting in a boat up and down your dock for four days working out whether you are the sort of yard that asks first."

"Ask," Calder Marr said.

"I took a job at Ashtide in November on a claim my own crew had not lodged," Tarner said. "I took it because we had eleven weeks of nothing and I had a mouth. I told them on the Thursday, after, because I was ashamed of saying it on the Monday. And a man called Fisk took me off the slate on the Friday, and Fisk was right, and I would do it again, and I did tell them, only afterwards, and those are two sentences and both of them are true and you may weigh them however you like."

Nobody in that shed weighed them.

"I have got a dredged housing and two and a half hands and one window on the twelfth seam," Tarner said, "and there is a rising easterly in the glass and it is going to be a blow on Thursday night, and if it blows I do not work, and I will have paid a man a day's money for a day I cannot use, and I am telling you now so that nobody is surprised on Thursday and nobody says afterwards that a man stood in a shed and did not say what the weather was going to do."

"Then on Thursday night there is no work," Calder Marr said, "and if I do not work I will not take the day off you, and you are not going to argue with me about it, because a man is not responsible for the weather he did not hide from."

"That is a very stupid thing to say to a man who has just offered to be reasonable about money."

"It is the only way I know to be worth knowing," Calder Marr said.

---

The reason was written out in the shed on the Tuesday afternoon and read back four times, because that is the rule in this yard and Bevin Rook does not break rules anybody other people made, and it went into the harbor authority's own book at eight on Thursday morning with the clerk standing there and Calder Marr's own left hand on it and the clerk not summarizing it.

It said the work, and the ground, and the two names, and the length, and the tide, and the fact that the ground was the twelfth seam of the mole's face and was not inside the closure of the first clause of the notice of the thirty-first, and that the work was a herring man's own ground tackle on a herring man's own account and not the harbor authority's work and not a raising, and that nothing was to be raised.

"Read the fourth line back," Nessa Pike said, on the Tuesday, in the shed.

Bevin Rook read the fourth line back, which was the line that named the twelfth seam and said it was not inside the closure, and he read it twice, and Calder Marr read it after him, and Tarner read it, and the mole's face at the twelfth seam is six hundred yards from the toe and the notice says nothing whatever about it, and the boy said so out loud both times, and then on the Wednesday morning Noll Tarner carried it up to the reeve's office on the west basin by himself, and asked for a hearing on it, and got one, and Kest read it and read it again and then did the thing he has done three times since the twelfth of January, in his own voice, at the reeve's own table, with a clerk sitting there.

"Marr," Kest said, and there was no Marr in the room, and he said the name anyway, which is what a reeve does when he is reading a paper. "I have written a line under four of your reasons and I am not writing one under this one, and I am going to say exactly why, in a room, with a clerk, because the not writing is as much of a record as the writing and I do not want a man in Veyr in four years thinking this office had stopped reading.

"The first clause of the notice of the thirty-first of October closes the mole's toe and inner face from the third seam to the eleventh seam. The twelfth seam is the twelfth seam. It is not in that clause. It is not the bedded seam in this office's own foundation. It is not the ninth seam's fouling. It is not under the town. I have named ground this office does not close, and a date, and a tide, and two men, and there is nothing in the notice that my instrument has to be reading around, and this is the first reason this office has had since the twenty-eighth of November that I have been able to sign without writing underneath it." He put the pen down flat. "And I am going to write one sentence in my own book anyway, and it is not an acknowledgement, and it is this. The yard has spent a month working ground my own notice closes and has made me put my own hand in a municipal ledger three times, and the reason the first three were necessary is that the yard had nothing else. And a harbor authority is not a place a man is to be sent for work when his own ground is shut. The ground at the third seam is shut to this office by a paper this office did not write, and I have said so in the yard's book and in my own, and I cannot close it, and I have stopped being able to think about anything else. So the twelfth seam is open, and I am telling the room it is open, and I would like a clerk to notice that I have said it out loud instead of writing it, because a man who writes it in a book can be found to have thought it up, and a man who says it in a room cannot."

Nobody wrote anything down except the clerk, who wrote the hour.

And the two crew copies of the reason went on the market wall on the Wednesday evening, under the naval claim, with the slip of four inches still under it, and the wall carried seven things where it had carried seven, because two of them are the same sheet in two hands and it does not count twice, and the boy wrote it in his own right hand with the date and the place, and under the two copies the boy wrote a line of his own, which is the first time in five months he has written under anything on that wall, and the line says that the reason itself is in the harbor authority's own book in five hands and that the wall carries a copy of a copy, and that a man reading it is reading a thing about another crew's day and not about a wreck, and Nessa Pike read the line and said it was correct and did not ask him where it came from, which is the second time in a fortnight she has not asked him where something came from, and the eighth of March is a Wednesday and a pump is to be fitted on it if the iron comes up the coast, and the two facts had nothing to do with each other except that a man who cannot buy a pump in March cannot hire one out in March either, and that is the whole of the winter.
