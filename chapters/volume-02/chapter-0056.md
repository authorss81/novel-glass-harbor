# Chapter 56

*Thirteen Minutes Past the Mark*

The reason went into the harbor authority's book at eight o'clock on Thursday the twelfth of January, at the reeve's table, in Calder Marr's own left hand, unedited, with the clerk present, and the clerk read it back and Kest read it back and nobody argued with it because there was no room and no reason to argue with a reason.

It said that on the twelfth instant, at the third seam of the mole's toe, on this office's own ground inside the harbor line, under a permit of the eighth of January, the holder proposes to get a hand to a stone in the foot of the mole's foundation which lies over a shackle bedded in the same stone, at the top of the flood, with a limit of twenty-five minutes on the slate, a named tender who is not the diver, and a cutoff the tender can pull without asking anybody; and that the holder does not propose to be in the water, and cannot be, and that this office's notice of the thirty-first of October names him in its third clause; and that the diver is a man of the *Grey Widow*, hired by the herring skipper Elias Voke on his own account for his own ground tackle and not by this office and not by the holder; and that the office has named the date and the ground and the limit and the rest of the crew, and that the first clause of the notice of the thirty-first of October closes the mole's toe and inner face from the third seam to the eleventh seam to all work and all approach by all persons, including this office, and that a permit of this office does not lift a notice of the circuit office and this office has no power to lift it, and that this office is putting a man inside that closure on the ground named above, and is entering the fact of it on the same sheet as everything else, in the holder's hand, unedited.

"Read the second half of the first line back," Kest said, from the other side of the table.

"The holder does not propose to be in the water, and cannot be, and this office's notice of the thirty-first of October names him in its third clause."

"I want that in the room in those words," Kest said, "because in about a year a man in Veyr is going to read that this harbor authority put a name on a piece of paper in order to keep a crew of its own out of the water on its own ground, and that is what has happened, and it is the reeve's own hand and not the yard's, and I would like it in the ledger in the yard's hand and not in mine."

The clerk wrote it out again on a separate line and read that back too, and Kest initialed it, and then he said the thing that was the price.

"I am going to write into this office's book, at the end of this entry, that the ground is this office's, the stone is in this office's own foundation, the shackle is this office's own fouling, the mooring it belongs to is the ninth seam's, and that the mooring has been out of service since the first of December because the fouling on it cannot be narrowed by anybody in this office. And I am going to write that the reason it is not being narrowed is that a notice this office did not write names three men and stops them from the water on this office's work, and that this office's own answer to a piece of paper of the eleventh of November was that a fouled shackle bedded in stone is not a seam, and that this office has been holding that position for seven weeks, and that on the twelfth of January a man of a herring boat has paid a diver out of another crew's locker to shift a stone on this office's ground while three of the best men in this harbor watched it from a wall."

"That is a worse reason than the one we wanted," Bevin Rook said.

"It is a worse reason than the one you wanted because the one you wanted was about a mooring," Kest said, "and this one is about this office. I have had since Monday to work out how to write a version of it that does not put my own hand in a file in Veyr, and there is not one, and I am going to write the true one and let a man in Veyr have it, because a reeve who writes a clever version is a reeve who has to be clever again in four years."

He wrote it, and the clerk read it, and Calder Marr's own reason went on the page above it in his own hand, and the two were on the same sheet, which nobody had asked for and which Bevin Rook read back twice.

"And there is a second thing I want on that sheet before anybody leaves this room," Calder said. "In November this office refused to countersign a plan of mine on four grounds, and the first of the four was that this office will not put a person inside a closure its own notice created. That is in a book, in my own hand, out of the afternoon of the eleventh of November, and I have never had an answer to it."

"I know what I wrote in November," Kest said, "and it was correct on that morning, and this morning it is a permit of the eighth putting a man of a herring boat's inside a closure this office named, and both are going into the same book in the same year." He put the pen down flat. "So write the clause on it and let the clerk read it back and do not soften it by a word, and when a man in Veyr reads this file in four years he will have a reeve who said no in November and yes in January, and the reason for both will be on one sheet in two hands, and I would rather he had that than have a clerk's slip in a net loft telling him this office did not know what it was doing."

---

The glass had been falling since the previous night and by ten o'clock on the Thursday the southerly had got into the road with the cold in it, and the mole's toe was going white at the top of the flood and had three feet of run on the stones, and the harbor's tide gang was stood on the mole in two places because the reeve had sent a man to see it done, and a second cutter's boat that was not in Morrow Quay in October was lying at the naval pile with her oars up.

Elias Voke had paid nineteen marks for the day and had said in the road at eight that he was paying it because a mooring he cannot lie on is a mooring he loses a boat on, and that the notice was not his and the stone was not his and the fouling was the authority's and that he was not going to argue with any of those three facts in January.

Tam Slee, who was forty-one and had dived out of the *Grey Widow* for nine years and had a way of looking at a fouled thing before touching it, went over at five minutes past ten in about four and a half feet on the slack with the stone about the size of a hip bath and its top a foot under water at the top of the flood.

Calder Marr had the line. Nessa Pike had the board and the fall and the slate with the limit on it in her own hand. Bevin Rook had the book and the air column and the whistle, and his left arm was across his chest in the sling, and his right hand was on the fall, because a fall has to be led and a boy with one hand can lead a fall.

At eight minutes the boy read the depth. At thirteen he read the air.

"Four foot four, and thirteen minutes."

"Thirteen goes in the book."

"Thirteen is not what I would have said if you had not called it, and you have not asked me twice today."

"That is the arrangement and it is not going to be broken by weather," Nessa said.

At sixteen minutes Slee had his arm under the stone and reported it up the line in a flat voice with the words *it is bedded in and it is not loose and there is a shackle under the near side of it and the shackle is in*. At nineteen minutes the stone moved a quarter of an inch and the fouling came off the top of it in a slab, and the chain came tight.

It came tight in about two seconds and it came tight the way a chain comes tight when a man under a stone has put his weight on the wrong side of it, and it came tight on the fall, and the fall was in Bevin Rook's right hand, and the boy did exactly what a boy of twenty-two does when a load comes down a line he is holding: he turned his body and put his other arm into it.

The sling was on his left arm. The left arm had been held across his chest against his body since the twenty-ninth of October, and he put it into a fall, and the wrist that took it is the wrist of the hand that was crushed in a bight in October.

There was no sound on the water that anybody heard afterwards. There was a sound on the barge.

The line came up hard enough to lift the slack out of the water and the boy came up off the thwart onto his knees with the fall still in his right hand and his left arm not in the sling any more, and he said, in the flat voice, and everybody who was there has said since that it was the flat voice:

"Nineteen by mine. Four foot four. The shackle is in and the stone is bedded and I have got it round and it is not loose."

"Down," Nessa Pike said.

The line went slack. The tender had the cleat in her right hand and the word in her mouth before the slack was a foot.

"Enough."

She did not raise her voice. She had not raised her voice on the tenth of December and she did not raise it now, and she said the word the way a person says a word that has been in a book since the thirteenth of October, and she said it while she was coming down the board toward the boy, and she did not say it twice.

Slee came up at twenty-two minutes with his right arm in a line's loop and no other harm done to him that anybody found out about that day, and the stone was four inches higher in the mud than it had been and it was not enough, and the shackle was fouled and had not come free, and the permit's limit was twenty-five minutes and the work was not done.

Nessa Pike got the boy's wrist in both her hands in about two seconds and did not look away from him while she did it, and he pulled, and she did not let go, and the boy said something that the book does not have.

"You are not going to be angry at me and be right about the air at the same time," she said. "That is the correct answer and I taught you it in October and you have now used it against me three times, and I would like a fourth, and I am not going to apologize and I am not going to soften it, and I am going to say one more thing and then I am going to stop talking about it for the rest of the winter. I gave you twenty-five on the slate and the tender gave you nineteen and you were at nineteen when that came down, and the difference of six minutes is the difference between a man who is fit and a man who is not, and it is going to be the difference between a hand that works in June and a hand that does not, and that is not me, that is the air, and you may hate me for the rest of the month and I will still have been right at nineteen minutes and I would rather you were alive to be angry at me about it."

Then she said the other thing, which is the part that belongs in this chapter and not in any speech.

"And that is six times since the twenty-fifth of October that a person in this yard has overruled you, and it is six out of six, and I have won every one of them, and this is the first one that has cost somebody a hand, and I want it written down in the book in my own words and not in his, because in a year the two of you are going to remember it differently and I am not going to be one of the two of you."

---

They carried the stone to the mole head in the end, on the Saturday, with a hand crane out of the net loft at two marks a day, and the crane was hired and paid for by the harbor authority out of its own repair account and not out of this yard, because a yard with three of its men named in a notice does not hire iron, and because there is no clause in a charter that says a harbor's crane comes out because a boy is in a sling. Kest entered that he had paid for it and the hour, and the harbor's clerk wrote it down, and neither of them said anything about it on the mole.

The money went into the book on the Saturday night and the boy did not read it out, and Nessa Pike read it out for him, which is the second time in eleven weeks that she has read for him, and she made Calder Marr read it back.

"Gross on the board, nineteen, from Elias Voke, for one day's work on his own ring and his own ground tackle, with a diver hired out of the *Grey Widow*'s locker at four marks to the diver, and the diver is a man and not a boy and he was in the water twenty-two minutes of twenty-five and came up with nothing broken that anybody knows of, and it is not in the book that he came up with nothing broken and it is in the book that nobody has asked him."

"The eighth and the share."

"Two and two. The hand crane is not in this list, because the office paid for it and the office will read that out in its own boat, and this book does not carry another office's money twice. Air, two marks, and the pot is a third gone and a third of that pot is a hundred marks a third and two marks has not put a mark back into it. Rope and the three days' victuals, three. The diver's four, which go to Tam Slee and not to this yard, and are entered as a share of the work and not as a wage, because he took them as a share. That is thirteen out of nineteen and the yard's share is six, and out of the six there is a bonesetter's mark of one and the first writing of a second time, and the bag is thirteen and I am not going to say a word about what that is."

"And the note," Bevin Rook said, from the bench, with his right hand in his lap, and his left arm lying along it and not in anything.

"And the note," Nessa said, "stands at eighteen since Saturday the seventh, and nothing has gone on it since, and I have been going to the chandler's on Tuesdays and Fridays with a list of what this yard has eaten and I am not going to do that in front of him, because a man who buys bread is a man with bread and this yard is a man with a list."

The bonesetter came at four in the afternoon on the Saturday and looked at the wrist for a while and did not ask whose it was, because she had asked in November and had been told and had not wanted to be told twice.

"November," she said, when Calder Marr asked what she had to say, because he had to ask. "In November I said hold and leave it, and I said it in this room and it is the right word and I would say it again. In January I am going to say a different thing and I am going to say it in front of the boy because he is twenty-two and he is not a child and he is owed a forecast.

"It will close. It will not be worth what it was, and there is nothing in that hand I can put right now, and if I said it was worth what it was I would be telling a man what he wants to hear about his own body in a month when he is going to find out. And I am not going to promise you June, because I promised June in October and I have been wrong about that twice in my life and once in a shop, and the thing I wrote in my book in October is *a boat hand in June*, and that is what is in the book, and it is not a promise and it is not a lie, and it is the only sentence I have."

"A boat hand in June," the boy said.

"A boat hand in June," the bonesetter said, "and if a man asks me in May I will tell him what I tell every man in May, which is that I do not know, and if a man cannot work on *I do not know* then he should not be in the water, and that is a thing to be said about a man and not about a wrist."

---

The permit ran from the eighth to the fourteenth and the work was done on the twelfth, and the fourteenth was a Saturday and the boat went out again on the Saturday at the top of the flood with a hand lead and a slate, because the permit said the dates and the permit did not care what had happened on the first of them, and Bevin Rook wanted to see the stone with a rule on it and was told by Nessa Pike that he was going to stand on a board and hold a slate and not go within a foot of a fall.

He read the entry from the twelfth out twice, in the flat voice, sitting down, with his right hand on the page and his left arm along it.

"The reason entered in the harbor authority's own book at eight o'clock on the twelfth, in Calder Marr's own left hand, with the clerk present and not edited, and the reeve's own addition under it, and both on one sheet, and both read back twice. A limit of twenty-five minutes on the slate. A named tender who was not the diver. A cutoff the tender could pull without asking anybody, and the word was the word, and the tender used it at nineteen minutes and did not use it twice and did not look away and did not apologize, and none of those four things is mine to enter and all four of them are mine to say happened.

"Twenty-two minutes in the water, the shackle fouled and not free, the stone up four inches and not enough, the permit's dates were the eighth to the fourteenth and the work was done on the twelfth and the fourteenth was spent sounding.

"And I put my arm into a fall that came tight and the arm I put in was the one in the sling, and Calder Marr has written that in his own hand and I have read it back twice, and I am not going to write it in mine, because it is the only sentence in this entry I would have to write twice and the rule is that it goes in the hand of the man who did it and the man who did it is not me."

Nobody argued. It went in the book, and the boy read it back a third time because it was his own, and Nessa Pike countersigned, and Calder Marr signed underneath her, and the book went onto the shelf with its spine out and the lamp left on it, which is where a book lives in a shed in January.

The yard had been in the water on every date the harbor authority had named to it since the sixth of December, and it had now been hurt on one of them, and it had not been paid for one of them.
