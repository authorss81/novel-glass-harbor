# Chapter 86

*Nine Joins in a Ring*

The clerk had come out of the hearing room at eight that morning, and the slate wall behind the reeve's bench was bare and had been bare since eight that morning, because it is wiped every Monday at eight whether there is a sitting or not, and nothing had been put on it, and the clerk said so without being asked and then said that his own page had wanted two sides in March and had wanted one and a half this morning, and that he was going to keep it whether the wall was up or not.

Then the six of them went up the mole steps to the crown between the sixth and ninth seams, which is above the face and not the toe, and which this office had agreed on the Friday to treat as inside the first clause of a notice it did not write, and Calder Marr read the fifth entry of the reeve's own hand out loud on the stones at about a quarter past eight so that a man standing on the mole steps below could hear that it was being done.

The wind was off the water and hard enough to be a nuisance and the sun was out. Nothing was in the water. The clerk had the ledger on a board and a folded sailcloth and a flat board, and Mara Venn had come up on her own legs, with her book under her arm and her calipers in a case, and she was thirty and had been cold since the fourth of January.

"Before I touch it," she said, "I am going to say four things into that ledger, and they are mine, and none of them is a courtesy.

"One. There is no diver in this examination and I am not a diver and I do not go into anything, and there is no water in it and no air in it and no ship in it, and if anybody in the next four years finds a page of mine with a dive on it, it is not from this morning. Two. I am not going to describe what is in that panel. There is a thing in it — it is made to be heard from and not entered, and it was made by a man who was careful, and that is all I am going to say about it in daylight on a mole, and if a man asks me what a fisher saw out of a window at Ashtide I will tell him I have not looked and could not tell him. Three. What I say will be about the glass and about the mark, and a mark is a material thing and an office has a form, and I will keep the two in the order they belong in. Four. I have no office. I have not had one since the fourth of January. Anything I say this morning goes in a yard's book and in this office's book if this office will take it, and nowhere else, and my name goes on it in my own hand or it does not go on it at all."

"Enter all four," the clerk said.

Then she put the sailcloth on the board, and the object on the cloth, and it was warm again in the sun and cloudy in the middle and clear at the rim, and she looked at it for a long time before she put a hand near it.

---

"First the object, because you have been talking about the mark all morning and nobody has described the thing.

"It is a listening panel. It is a sheet of worked glass with an iron collar round its edge and a hand-hole at one end and a loop at the head of it, and it is made to be hung in a boat's rigging and listened to from, and it is not made to be entered and it would be dangerous to enter and I could tell you why and it is not relevant. The working in it is not thirty years old because I have dated it, it is thirty years old because that is how that edge is dressed and that is a thing a working hand does and it has not been done since a bellwright I trained under was alive. I will not name a shop and I will not name a man and both of those refusals are proper and neither of them is modesty."

"You trained under somebody," Dace said.

"I was apprenticed for two years and paid for the privilege," Mara Venn said, "and that is the whole of what I am going to say about it."

She came near the corner then, and did not touch the mark, and put her calipers across the ring on the face without touching it either, and looked at the whole of the seal for a long time.

"Now the mark. It is a claim seal. A claim seal is a Registry instrument and it does three things and no more: it says who is holding the thing, it says where the thing came from, and it says what a man may do with it. Those three are what is cut on this one and they are in the office's form, in the office's order, with the office's name spelled the way that office spells it, and the lettering is good. Whoever made it knew the form. I mean that as a compliment and I mean it as the first half of what I have to say.

"It says: *Custodian, the sloop Grey Widow of Morrow Quay. Provenance, one panel worked at the quay of Ashtide. Limit, route use.*"

"Route use," Calder Marr said. "That is a permission. That is a permission to carry it on a route."

"It is," she said, "and here is the second half, and I am going to say it in this order because I have had it in my head since Friday night and I would like to get it out of my mouth in the right order and I am not being dramatic, I am being a woman who works in a cold wind.

"A claim seal is only true if the custodian on it can be asked things. The Registry will name a person or it will name an assembly, and either of those can be told yes or told no, and either of those can be put a limit on, and either of those can be found next year and asked whether it still means it said in the spring. **The custodian on this seal is a boat.** A boat cannot be asked anything. A boat cannot consent to anything. A boat cannot refuse anything. It cannot be found and it cannot be asked whether it still means it said in the autumn, and there is a seal cut into a sheet of glass on a fishing boat out of Morrow Quay that says a thing about the sea may be done with this, and the thing that is supposed to be able to say no is a hull with a mast on it."

Nobody said anything for about a minute, and the wind came round the corner of the crown and the sailcloth lifted at one corner and Mara Venn put a hand on it without looking.

"That is the forgery," she said. "Not a spelling. Not a form. The one clause that would let anybody use it."

---

"And I am going to say the rest of it now, before anybody asks me, because if I wait to be asked then it will be an answer and I want it to be a statement.

"**I can tell you the seal on that panel was not struck by that office. I cannot tell you who held the tool, and I cannot tell you who made the glass, and I cannot tell you why a man who wanted a permission wrote the name of his own boat down as the thing that owns a record of the sea.** I am not being careful. I am telling you the exact size of what my trade can do, because you have spent five months finding out what my trade cannot do and I would rather you had the figure in a morning than in a hearing in two years."

"How do you know the office did not strike it," the clerk said.

"Because the ring has nine joins in it." She put the calipers down. "A Registry striking is cut once, into steel, by one man, and the ring around the office's name is part of the same piece of steel and it is one continuous curve from end to end, and a man in a Registry house could put that ring in a mark a hundred times and it would be the same ring every time. That one has nine joins in it, which means it was cut in something else first — a paper, most likely, or a copper plate — and then struck into the glass by hand. A paper striking into glass takes the mark. It does not take the wear."

"And the wear," Dace said.

"Thirty years of a thing in a wheelhouse and a dry locker and a man's coat," Mara Venn said, "and the wear is coming off the outside of the ring and not off the inside, which is what does it when a man has a thumb on it. So the mark is thirty years old and the glass it is on is thirty years old, and both of those are the same thirty years, and there is nothing in that which tells you who did it, and a man in a Registry house who told you a Registry seal was never struck by hand would be telling you a thing about steel and not about this."

---

"Calder," she said, before Calder Marr had got a word in. "You are going to ask me what it costs to be right and what would have to be true, and then you are going to ask me whether I am sure. I have given you a figure twice and been right about the price both times and been refused the asking both times, and I am not going to give you one this morning, and you are not going to stand there and tell me it is a change of heart, because it is not a change of heart. It is a change in what you want. Twice you wanted a number you could put on a wall. This morning you want a number that would let a man in a city write *a conservator of Morrow Quay certifies*, and I will not be certified by a party, and I am not an office and I am not yours."

"I was not going to ask you whether you were sure," Calder Marr said.

"You were going to ask me whether it was a good one," she said, "which is the same question with the coat off, and I have refused that in a hearing room and in this shed and I am refusing it on a mole for the third time and I would like the clerk to enter that it is the third time and that the principle has not changed, because a thing that changes its details every time and its principle never is a rule and not a mood."

"Entered," the clerk said.

"And one more, and then I will get off this stone, because I have been standing in a wind for fifty minutes and I am not a woman who stands in wind for pleasure." She looked at the object on the cloth. "If a man on this mole asks me on Tuesday what that glass carries, I will tell him that it was worked about thirty years ago by somebody careful, in a shop on a quay, and that it has a claim seal on its face that is false in the one clause that matters, and that the false clause names a boat. I will not tell him what is inside it. I have not been in it, I am not going to be in it, and a thing that is not in me is not a thing I can describe to a man who is standing in the sun."

---

The clerk did what a clerk does, which is to say what a box can hold, and the box could not hold a conservator's finding, and he said so at about eleven.

"This office cannot enter an examination of an object. It can enter that the object was on this ground on this date and that nobody went in the water, which is the claim window, and it is in the ledger already. What you have said this morning, and I am not saying it is not good, I am saying there is nowhere in this office's book that it goes."

"Then it goes in ours," Calder Marr said, "in her words, with her name on it at her asking, and under it I am going to write that no person in this harbor with any office at all has entered it, and that the reason why is the fourth of January, and that the yard is not going to pretend that a page in its own book is a certificate."

"I will put my name to it," Mara Venn said, "and I will want the word *unsigned* under it, and I will want anybody who comes to it in four years to know that the man who wrote the seal's words into the market on Saturday is a man with no office and no fee and nothing to lose by being wrong and nothing to gain by being right, and that is not a certificate and it is the only thing this coast has."

Nessa Pike would not put her name to it either, and said why in one sentence and had it entered. "I am a party. A party does not witness. I will read it back and I will not sign it and I would like that written down, because in four years somebody will want to know whether a ship's wife stood up for the seal and she did not stand up for anything, she stood on a mole."

The four who read it back were the boy, Dace, Pike and Calder Marr, and the fifth thing on the page was not read back because it was a conservator's statement and not the yard's day, and the object went back into its sailcloth and into Dace's own hands on the mole steps, and the sun was still out and the wind was still hard and the glass had been falling for seven days and the tide was two hours from the top of the flood and the bag was ten and the note was twenty-seven and the tin was not touched and the tenth week of a wage at four marks a week fell due on the Tuesday and was going to go into a column with no adjectives in it.
