# Chapter 55

*The Going Out*

It was a fine day, which is the whole point of it. The glass had been up since the second and there was no wind in the road and the mole face at the top of the flood went to a sheet of glare the way it goes in this harbor and the reason a crew goes out on a fine day rather than a bad one is that the work is on a *ground* and not in a weather, and a bad day does not stop it, it only stops the getting to it and the getting off it and the boat coming back.

What the work was, and the reason it could not wait for a committee, was this: the core had been hung on harbor gear in eight and a half feet off the south end on the twenty-fifth of December on a ground line of the authority's own, and it had a fouled waist and a tongue half worn through and a year's rope on it before somebody came and gave it a new one, and on the fourth of January Calder Marr had stood in a shed with a boy and worked out that the reeve's chart notation had committed this yard, in front of a town, to keeping it.

There was a line of oilcloth in the bench chest and there was four marks of hired warp in Harl Dace of the *Grey Widow*'s locker and there was the harbor's pump, which would be three a tide and which the yard could not do without, because the yard's own pump had a cracked housing and a sleeve packed with the reeve's tallow and no man's hands on this coast can fit a new one before the spring.

Nessa Pike said the name out loud on the quay at eight in the morning, in front of the two men and the boy, and she said it the way she said everything, which is as a fact about a building.

"Corvin Sarn sent for a woman and not a man on the sixth of December and came to the top of the bank and told us what a bell is, and he has not been in this yard since, and nobody in this yard has asked him a single question in four months, and he has been the harbor's own bellwright of Morrow Quay since the twentieth of October. He is not the Free Keel's. If we want him on a boat today he comes because this is the harbor's ground and he is the harbor's man, and if we offer him a share of what we lift he will say no, and I would like the offer made anyway so that the no is on the record in front of Rook and not a thing we decided afterwards."

"Corvin Sarn," Calder Marr said, when the man came down the steps at half past eight with a canvas bag and no coat, "I am going to ask you a question and Bevin is going to write it down, and if you do not want it written down I will take the book out of his hands and you can have the answer in the open like a man and not in a record, and either is proper and I would like you to pick one now."

"Ask it in the record," Corvin Sarn said. "I have never been asked one in four months and I would like there to be a piece of paper with a question in it, because in about three years somebody is going to be able to say the Free Keel yard had the harbor's bellwright out on the eleventh of January and never wrote down a word he said, and they will be right."

He came out to the barge and looked at the water for a while.

"You are going to ask me what it costs," he said. "Everybody asks me that in a different order and they all ask it wrong, because they ask it as a question about iron. It is not a question about iron. Ask it properly and I will answer it once and it will be in your book and I will not be asked again for a year."

"Ask him properly," Nessa said.

"What does it cost the yard to keep that thing answering."

Corvin Sarn took the canvas bag off his shoulder and set it down on the boards of the barge and did not open it.

"A rope a year," he said, "because rope in this water is rope for one year and not eighteen months, and not because anybody has been careless. A fathom and a half and twenty feet of it and a good whipping at the eye, and if you buy a cheaper rope than that you will be out here in October instead of January and you will be doing it in a northeast blow with four men who are not me, and one of those four men will be in the water where I am not."

"That is one."

"That is one, and it is the small one. A man's day every four or five years for the tongue, for as long as that bell hangs on that ground, and that is a day of my trade and not a day of yours, and the day is a day in a shed with the fire in it, and it is the only part of this that anybody in Morrow Quay can see." He crouched and put his hand flat on the boards. "And then there is the going out. A person, in a boat, in the weather. Not today. Not in a gale. In whatever the water is, at the time the water is, on the day the rope wants looking at and the fouling wants reading and the ground tackle wants feeling over with a hand, and the fouling cannot be cleaned and must not be, and I will tell you why in one sentence and then I will not say it again in my life."

"Say it."

"If you get water in a gudgeon you write the whole bell off," he said, "and if you get water in the lead between the glass and the iron you have four pieces of glass that go cloudy in a year and there is no way back from that but a furnace two hundred miles off, and that furnace is Crown work and costs more than this yard is worth. So it hangs as it is, with the tongue as it is, and the fouling stays on it, because fouling is not the enemy, and a bell with a clean waist is a bell that has been handled by somebody who did not know what it was."

"You said on the sixth of December that you would not open a bell in the wet," Bevin Rook said, "and the reason you gave was the gudgeon and the lead, and I wrote it down, and I have got it here."

"Then you have got it and you do not need me to say it again," Corvin Sarn said, "and the man who sent for me in December got a woman to write it down and I have thought about that more than I have thought about the bell, and I would like the yard to know that I noticed and that I have not decided what I think about it."

He stood up and looked at Calder Marr.

"Offer me the share."

"The crew's share of anything we lift goes to the crew and there is no share in a bell that is already hung and already answered, but I am going to offer you the share of the day. Four marks for the warp and three for the pump and a mark of oilcloth, and if you take a share of it you take it today and I will write that you took it."

"No," Corvin Sarn said.

"You have not asked what the share is."

"One half of a day on a hired line and a hired pump is four marks and a half, and I have a trade in this harbor and the trade is paid in days and not in shares, and if I take a share of your day I am a man who sells his going out, and a man who sells his going out is a man whose going out belongs to somebody, and the day after that somebody sends him out in weather he would not have gone out in." He picked his bag up again. "I am the harbor's bellwright. It goes on your chart and it goes in your book and I will read the book when it is written, and that is the arrangement, and I would like the offer in the record with the no in it, and then neither of us ever has to do that again."

"Enter it," Nessa Pike said.

---

They went out at ten past ten at the top of the flood with the warp on the fall and the lead going first and Corvin Sarn in the bow of the barge with his thumb on the ground line where it came through the water, and Bevin Rook in the middle with the book open on his knees and the slate on the top of his knee with the pencil tied to it on a string, in the way a man does a thing he cannot afford to have wet.

In eight and a half feet off the south end, at the top of the flood, the core was on its ground line and its fouled waist was under a hand's breadth of the water and the harbor's tide bell went at ten minutes past ten, three strokes, in the road, where it has always hung.

Under the third stroke, a hand's width of a second later, the answer came up out of the water: low, even, flatter than anything a harbor grows, three marks and a flick, and it stopped when the bell stopped.

"Nine," Bevin Rook said. "Nine logged since the twenty-sixth of October, and this is the ninth, and the water is at the top of the flood, and the harbor's three went at ten minutes past ten, and the answer is out of the core a hand's width of a second under the third, and the second is the same second as the sixth of December and the same second as the twenty-sixth, and there is no source, and it is not written as a voice, and the span is from the twenty-sixth of October and not from the fourth of November."

"Read it back," Nessa said.

He read it back.

"And the state of the water," Nessa said.

"The state of the water is a slack run off the mole with a light southerly and no swell in it, and the fouling on the waist of the core is hard and grey and has been on it thirteen years, and the tongue is where it was in December and the eye is sound and the ground line is good, and there is nothing in this answer but the note," the boy said, "and I am writing that it is only the note because that is what came, and not because of anything else."

"It was only the note on the twenty-sixth of December too," Calder Marr said, "and the twenty-sixth of December had a call in it, and the sixth of December had one, and the eighth of November had one and a naval rating wrote it on a slate at the same minute I did, and today there is a note and no call, and that is the first, and I am not going to say anything about it in front of you because you have written what you heard and I have not heard anything I can put in a book."

Bevin Rook looked at him for a second.

"I have written what I heard," he said, "and you have heard a thing you will not write, and I am not going to ask, and we have been in this yard five months and I am the only person in it who is not going to ask, and I would like it noted that I did not ask in front of a bellwright."

"Enter that too," Nessa said. "Enter that he did not ask."

---

Then Corvin Sarn put his hand over the side and brought it up and looked at it and said nothing for long enough that Calder Marr thought he was not going to.

"That is the same fouling," he said at last. "Hard grey slate-skin with the worms still in it, and the same worms I saw on a plate of iron two boat lengths along this slope in November when three of you were standing over it in a boat shouting at each other about a depth. Thirteen years of this coast on both, and both put on in the same decade, and nobody has been in this water since the year the Cut happened."

"Two objects with the same thing on them does not make them the same thing."

"It does not, and I did not say it did, and if you had answered me with anything else I would have got back in the boat and gone home." Sarn wiped his hand on his thigh. "Now look at the other side of it. Put your lead over on the seaward face."

The boy put the lead over. The face on the seaward side came up out of the water with a pale clean streak down the middle of it about a foot wide and the length of the bell, and no weed on it at all, and the stone under it scoured white.

"Now you know why it answers and the plate never did," Corvin Sarn said, "and I have been waiting four months for somebody to ask me that question and nobody has, because all four of you have been waiting for a different question."

"Tell us anyway."

"That is the gravel," he said. "Every flood that comes across this ground takes a layer of stone off the seaward face and takes the weed with it, and the scour is clean and hard, and the weed is what does it. Your plate is under the root of this mole with fifty yards of the authority's own stone on top of it, and nothing has moved over that plate since the year she went down, and a thing that has nothing moving over it does not answer." He knocked the side of the barge with his knuckle. "A bell only speaks while somebody keeps the weather working on it. That is the whole of the craft, and it costs a man a day and a rope a year and a bad week every four or five years, and nobody will ever know that you do it, and if you stop doing it this thing stops answering and it starts being a rock, and everybody on this coast stops knowing where the water is."

Nobody said anything on the barge for about a minute.

"That is the answer to the question you were all not asking," Sarn said, "and it is a poor answer and it is the true one, and I have told it to a man who writes books and to a boy who reads them and to a woman who has a cleat, and none of you is going to get a better one out of me this year, and I am going to sit down in the sun."

The cost went into the book on the Saturday morning, and the boy read it out, and then read the whole of it back a second time because the pump was in it and a pump is a figure nobody in that room could check.

"Hired warp from Harl Dace of the *Grey Widow*, four marks, for one day, and he is not paid out of the share and it is a hire and it goes in as a hire. The harbor's pump, three a tide, one tide, hired, and the harbor's crane and the harbor's barge are the harbor's and the harbor is paying their hire out of the authority's own account on the authority's own ground, and neither of them came out here today because the crane and the barge are for the authority's work and this was not the authority's work, and the only money in this boat this morning was ours. Oilcloth and candles, a mark. That is eight marks out, and the whole of what this yard has in the world is not a figure I am going to say in a boat, and you can have it in the shed tonight and you can have it twice."

"Five less three on Thursday was two," Nessa said, "and the job on Saturday put fifteen into the yard and one of it went on the note at the chandler's, so the bag was sixteen on Saturday night, and eight of that has gone this morning to a rope and a pump and a candle, and the bag is eight, and if you read it out twice it will still be eight."

The going out cost eight marks and a day of a boat and it will cost eight marks and a day of a boat again in a year, and the same again the year after, and the day of the tongue every four or five years on top, and the only thing about it that anybody in this harbor will ever see is a line in a reeve's hand on a chart saying *a signal, kept by hand, at the cost stated in this office's hearing record of the twenty-eighth of November*, which is a sentence that will be read in Veyr in about four years by a man who will have no idea that it is a bill.
