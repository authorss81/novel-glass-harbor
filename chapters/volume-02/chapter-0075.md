# Chapter 75

*What the Yard Will Not Strike Out*

Noll Tarner was sitting on the bollard at the head of the fish dock at eight in the morning on Wednesday the twelfth of March with his hands round his knees, and he had been there since the market began, and when Calder Marr came down he said the thing he had come to say before anybody had said good morning.

"I want the twenty-sixth of January struck out."

"Not off the wall first," Nessa Pike said. "You are going to come into this shed and say it in a room with four people in it or you are going to say it on a bollard, and if you say it on a bollard then in three months a man will say you said it in a market and you did not, and that is how a yard gets a reputation it has not earned. Which one."

"The bollard is what I have got," Tarner said, and got up off it, and came in.

---

He had it in his own hand. He had written it out on the back of a lighter's tally on the Sunday night in a hut at the pile, and he put it on the bench face up, and it was four lines and it was better written than anything anybody in that shed had produced since January.

"Strike the twenty-sixth out of your book," he said. "Take it off your wall. Write under the day that the party withdraws the entry and that the *Sea Wren*'s work on the twelfth seam was not entered by the party and is not the party's record, and send that to the office that has my boat, and I will not say a word about it anywhere else and I do not want any money for it and I am not asking you for any."

"Why," said Calder Marr.

"Because on Saturday morning a form came up my river with my gear in a lighter, and the ground of it is *entries concerning a contested material entered in the fish market of Morrow Quay*, and there is one entry in that market about my boat and it is in your hand under my name, and I wrote it out in a street on the fourth of March and I said where I got the two columns from and I put a crowd behind me on purpose, and now the crowd has a use for what I said." He turned the tally round. "I did that. I want that written down first, because in about a year there is going to be a version where the yard is a thing that was done to and a version where a man in a market said a sentence out loud on purpose and it turned into a paper, and I do not know which of them is true and neither will anybody else in a year, and I would like the one that is true to be in your book with my name on it."

"That is the first sentence anybody has said to me in five months that I am going to write down exactly as it was said," Nessa Pike said, "and I am going to write it down now, and then you are going to be told no, and I am going to be the one who tells you, because I wrote the rule and the rule is mine."

---

"Nessa."

"I have not finished. Two columns stand and neither is scratched out, and that is not a rule I invented for today, it is a rule a boy worked out on the nineteenth of January about a hole in a reason of ours and I countersigned it in my own hand. And here is why it exists, and it exists because of a thing you have not heard about, and I am going to tell it to you in this room and you are not going to like it.

"On the nineteenth of January the holder wrote a reason of two hundred and sixty-nine words and it had a hole in it. It said what was being done and it did not say why anybody wanted it done, and the reason he wanted it done was in his head and in no book. And that is not anybody else's business and I will not have it in a book. But a hole that nobody has written down is a hole, and a man who finds a hole in six months can write anything he likes in it. So we entered the hole. In his own hand and in the boy's, with the date on it, and it is in the harbor authority's book and on that wall and it says *an omission*, and it says what is omitted is the purpose, and the purpose is still not written down anywhere because it is his to write or not to write.

"So that is what a scratched-out line is. It is a hole you have invited a man into. And on the fourth of March an instrument of a vessel of the Crown Navy put a boat into a lighter on the strength of a sheet of paper in a market, and I have not slept a whole night since, and I am not going to hand that same office a hole in a book to put anything into."

Tarner looked at her for a while. The stove ticked.

"That is a good answer," he said. "It is a good answer and it is not an answer to me, because I am not asking you to put anything into a hole. I am asking you to take something out."

"Which is the same act with the door open."

---

Calder Marr had not said anything for nine minutes, which is longer than the rule allows and which nobody in that shed corrected.

"No," he said at last. "We will not strike it, and I will say why in a way that is not Nessa's way, because Nessa is right about the rule and I am the man who put the day in.

"Your day is true. That is the whole of it and it is the reason I will not take it out and it is not a defence of you, it is a fact about a wall. The twelfth of January you went down under a stone that had been there since the year the mole went out, and the stone had a shackle cast in it, and you came up with a shackle and a ring and four feet of warp in one piece, and there was nothing in your hands that looked like glass and nothing that is going into a ledger in Veyr, and it took twenty-one minutes by your own count and nineteen by the boat's, and both of those are on that page, and your nineteen is in the first column and your twenty-one is in the second, and neither of them is scratched out. Nobody has ever struck out your nineteen. I have watched a man try to explain the difference between them for an hour and a half and he has not once asked me to take his smaller one out.

"And if we strike yours we strike ours, and not because it is fair. Because a book with one hole in it that a party can fill by asking is a book that tells a man in four years exactly which entries this yard gives up when it is asked nicely. We gave up a hole on the nineteenth of January without being asked, in our own hand. That is the only thing in five months I would put in front of a man from that office and I am not going to take it out to keep a crew in a lighter."

"You have put it in front of nobody," Tarner said. "It is in a book on the fish dock."

"Yes," said Calder Marr. "That is the whole of it. It is in a book on the fish dock and it is going to stay there and I have not got a better answer than that and I have had nine minutes to look for one."

---

Tarner read the rule for a while with his thumb on the edge of the bench, and then he asked for the next thing, and he asked for it flatly, the way a man asks a price.

"I want your leaf. Not my book. My book is in a lighter and in a naval file and it is my own business and a man's own book in a naval file is a man's own business, and it names me and the day and the ring and the time, and it does not name Wend Crale and it does not name Piet Marrow, and Piet Marrow is fifteen and cannot read and if his name is not on anything then in six months he is a boy who was in a market on a Saturday and nobody can prove he was ever in a boat.

"Your leaf names all three of us, and the reason, and the ground, and the two columns, and the readings, and the fourth paragraph. And a man's own log is a man's own account of himself and a third party's record of his crew's day is a different kind of paper, and I know that because I have been a third party for five months and I have read what you have written about my own work and it is not what I would have written and it is better."

"Make it," Calder Marr said. "Make it out of the book in his hand and put the date on it and the place it is going, and I will countersign it under him, and the two crew copies that are on the wall came off a sheet in a shed in the same way in February and there is nothing novel in this."

"Nothing novel in it at all," Tarner said. "That is why I am asking for it and not for something."

---

Bevin Rook made it out between about eleven and one, in his own right hand, and it took two hours because he copied the entry and the reason and the fourth paragraph and the two columns and the five signatures' worth of reading-back, and at one o'clock he read the whole of it out in the shed, once, and then Tarner read it, which is two, and then Tarner asked him to read the fourth paragraph again on its own, which is three, and Calder Marr read the whole of it after him, which is four, and the four were a boy, a rival master, the holder, and a shipwright, and Tarner is the only one of the four who is not in that yard's wage column and the only one who has been to a naval slip in a lighter's tally.

"Twenty-two marks," Nessa Pike said, when it was done, "for a day's work on a fisherman's own account at the second seam, on a Tuesday in March, refused and not paid, cause absent in a city. The day is the seventh, the date is the tenth, and both are going in, and I want the word *refused* in the first column because a man on this dock can check it by asking Sella Brigg, and I want it in the first column and not in the second because it is the one thing in this book this month that everybody can check and everybody will."

"It went in the first column," the boy said, "and Calder has not been told a date for it, and I did not ask him for one, and it is the same as the fifth week and it will be the same as the sixth."

---

They shook hands at the head of the dock, which neither of them mentioned afterwards, and Calder Marr walked back up the fish dock alone and then stopped and came back, because there was one more thing and it had been standing in his chest since the fourth of March and it came out badly.

"I do not know what the record is for," he said. "Harl Dace asked me that in a net loft in January and I told him the truth, which was that I did not know, and I have not got a better answer in seven weeks and I have got a worse one. A fortnight ago the best answer I had was *it is for a man with a book, and a man with a book can check it*. And on Saturday a form came down this river with a boat in it and the ground of the form is that a record in a market is a thing somebody may use, and there is no clause anywhere in five months of my own arguing that says *by us and not by them*.

"That is your fault, Mr Tarner, and it is not a complaint, and I would like it written down in your hand and not mine, and if you are not going to write it then I will."

Tarner had his own copy of his own day rolled inside his coat, and he was a man who had lost a boat, a pump, two warps, a hand's share of a good week's work, and the only crew he had left that anybody in a form could call a crew, and he took the sheet out and gave it back, and said: "No. You wrote that one. I will not sign a thing of yours to be polite, and I will not take a pen off a man who came up a dock to say a hard thing to me at one o'clock on a Wednesday."
