# Chapter 57

*The Bill for Thirteen*

The long business of a supply problem is never the part where somebody says the word supply.

It began on Sunday the fifteenth of January at seven in the morning with the boy sitting at the bench with his right hand flat on a page and his left arm along it, doing the only kind of work he could do, and with a pump standing against the wall with a sleeve packed in it and a web cracked in the housing, and nobody in the shed would say the word *pump* for about an hour because they were all thinking about it and the thinking was the same.

"A cracked housing is not a cracked housing," Nessa Pike said, at about twenty to eight. "It is a pump that cannot be hired. Say the second half of it."

"You said it on the fourth," Bevin Rook said.

"I am saying it again because it has got worse, and I would like it in the book both times." She put her thumb on the wall. "We cannot work it. We cannot hire it out, and hiring it out is how a yard in this harbor pays for a bad winter, and we cannot hire it out, and that is four months. And a new housing cannot be had on this coast before the spring, and the spring is in eight weeks, and the eighth of March is a Friday and there is a moon on the Friday after that, and anybody who tells you a pump is a thing you get in a week is telling you about a harbor where the iron is on the quay."

"Three a tide is the harbor's rate," Calder said.

"Three a tide is the harbor's rate, and the harbor's rate is what the harbor charges a man who has no pump, and this yard has no pump, and the harbor's crane and the harbor's barge are the harbor's and the harbor is paying their hire out of its own money on its own ground, and we have been paying our own way for five months and this is the first week it has cost us a working day." She took her hand off the wall. "So here is the whole of it in one line. A fouled shackle is fouled shackles. A hand is a hand. And a pump that cannot pump is a boat with no engine, and you cannot hire a boat with no engine, and you cannot turn around in this harbor without somebody else's."

Then the boy's mother came at eleven, on the Monday, and she came to the shed because she had asked to be told things in her own front room and had been told a thing in her own front room and had a question about a date.

She stood in the doorway with the cloth over the apron pocket and did not come further in.

"Saturday," she said. "The bonesetter says the twelfth, and the writing has the hour on it. Was a boy in the water on the twelfth."

"A man was in the water on the twelfth," Calder Marr said. "Tam Slee, out of the *Grey Widow*, hired by Elias Voke, on Voke's own account, because the notice of the thirty-first names me and Nessa and Bevin and stops all three of us from the water on the authority's work. Bevin was on the fall. The fall came tight and the stone came down the line and he turned his body and put the arm in the sling into it, because it was the arm nearest the fall, and a boy of twenty-two does not stop to think about which arm is ruined."

"And you were standing on the line."

"I was standing on the line and I had the cleat in my hand and I did not see the stone come, and that is the whole of my part in it and I have written it in the book in my own hand and the boy has read it back."

She looked at her son for a while.

"You have been in the water twice since the twentieth of December," she said, "and one of them is the one that has put a mark on you, and I told you this morning that I will not be told nothing."

"You told me on the eleventh of February and not this morning," the boy said, without looking up, "and I have written the date down, and you said I might tell you no."

"You may tell me no on the eleventh of February and not before it," she said, "and I am going to the market now, and I would like one thing said in this yard while I am standing in the door, and it is for Calder Marr and not for my son."

"What is it."

"That the woman with the cleat said a thing to me in November in a cold shed and it has been true every day since, and on Saturday she said a different thing, and the different thing is worse to hear and better to have, and I would like whoever keeps the book to write down that a bonesetter changed her word in front of me in this harbor between the thirtieth of October and the fifteenth of January, and that nobody in this room treated the change as a trick."

Nessa Pike wrote it down, in her own hand, with the date on it, and read it back, and the mother read it over her shoulder before she went out into the road.

---

Harl Dace of the *Grey Widow* was in the net loft on the Monday afternoon with six men in it and coils on the floor and a fire going, and he had asked for the sheet, and the sheet was there, and he had it in his own hand and had a corner of it folded over.

He was a broad man with a burned ear and he read a document the way other men mend a net, slowly, with his thumb, and he got to the fourth of the six lines and stopped and looked up at Calder Marr, who had come down at four because a man who asks for a sheet in a net loft is a man who is going to ask something.

"Read the last line of the fourth part," Harl Dace said.

*That this record is of an object and of measurements, and is not a witness, and is not to be read as the content of anything.*

"That is a good line, and I have had that sheet in a net loft since November and I have not once been able to make a man understand it, and I have wanted to ask a question about it since November and I have not asked because I did not have the person to ask."

"Ask it."

"If a man reads that sheet, and he is a boat's master, and he reads the figures, and he hires on to me for a season, and I lose a boat, and a naval officer comes up the road and asks that man what he was told by the Free Keel yard—" Harl Dace turned the corner over and back. "Is that man bound by anything? Is he standing on a promise? Does that paper do a single thing to him?"

"No," Calder Marr said. "Nothing whatever. It is a record of an object. It has no power over anybody, it carries no consent, it names no man and binds no man, and a man who has read it may go and work for the *Vigil* on the morrow and no man in this yard could say a word about it, and we would not want to."

"That is what I thought, and I have been waiting three months for a man to say it out loud in a room, because I have got fifteen hands and I have been telling them to read a piece of paper for three months and I could not tell them what reading it did."

"Then what is your question."

Harl Dace put the sheet down on the coil he had been sitting on.

"Who is it for."

The loft was quiet for a moment. Somebody moved a coil at the far end.

"That is not a small question and I know it is not, and I have asked it of three men in three months and two of them told me it was for the record and one of them told me it was for the public, and both of those are words for a thing and neither of them is a person. So I will ask it the way I would ask it about rope. Who is it for. Not who is it about. Who is it for."

Calder Marr stood in a net loft with six men in it and a fire going and found that he had nothing.

"I do not know," he said. "I know what it is not for. It is not for the Registry, and it is not for the vessel, and it is not for a court. It is a record of two objects and the measurements and the custody of them, in a form that survives the loss of the objects, kept by a crew that paid for it out of a day's wage, and it is on a market wall and it is in your loft and it is in three other crews' walls and it is in five copies rolled in oilcloth in a chest that stands against the charter's bond in a shed on the fish dock, and any man who has a copy may do with it what he likes, and that is the whole of what I know and I have known it since the fourteenth of November."

"That is not an answer," Harl Dace said, kindly.

"No," Calder said. "It is not."

"Then I am going to keep the copy," the skipper said, "and I am going to keep asking, and I am not going to pretend to you that I am asking for myself, because I am not. I am asking for a boy of nineteen on my starboard side who cannot read a manifest, and if you ever find out who it is for I will come up the hill and take him off this coast inside a month."

He put the sheet in his coat.

---

What Calder Marr took out of the net loft and up the hill was not an answer, and it was not a charter question either, and that was the change.

He stopped asking the yard for paper. He had spent five months asking offices for things — a claim window, an undertaking, a charter, a note on a chart, a reason in a book — and every one of the six times he had asked, the thing that came back had been a reason why, and four of the six had cost somebody who was not the yard something. In the shed on the Monday night at half past nine he wrote out what he was actually going to ask for, and it was four lines long, and then he took the slate to Nessa Pike and asked her what the yard could do about it, and she took the slate and the pencil and did not answer for a while.

"You have asked me what we can do," she said at last, "instead of what you want, and I have been waiting five months for that and I am not going to let it pass as a small thing. So here is what we can do. Not what we want. What we can do.

"The north wall, and the first and second seams of the mole's face, and any private work on harbor ground landward of the third seam, and every tide that a skipper's own account will pay for, and the pump is not that work and the pump will not be ours before March. The ninth seam's mooring if the stone comes off, which is not ours to do and which the third clause stops us from doing, and which will be somebody else's work in a fortnight or it will not happen this winter. And one day a week in a boat with a hand lead and a slate, off a ground somebody else has named, on a date somebody else has named, with a reason in that man's book at eight in the morning and Pike on the cleat and no diver unless the reason says so, and there is no diver in any of them and there is not going to be one in this one, and the name of the man who dreads is not in any of these four lines and I have not asked you for it and I will not."

"That is smaller than what I wanted."

"It is exactly what there is," Nessa Pike said, "and it is a working week, and I have been in five yards and it is the first one where anybody ever asked me the question in that order, and I would like you to notice that I have just given you a smaller thing than you asked for and that you have not argued with me once, and if you argue with me now I will put the pencil down."

"I am not going to argue with you now."

"Then write it down and read it to Rook and let him read it back, and then go to bed, because it is twenty to ten and you have not eaten since the morning and I have watched you not eat since October."

---

The bench was reassigned in writing on the Monday night, and it went into the book in Nessa Pike's hand and with her name on it and no other name on it at all, because she would not have Calder Marr's name on it.

*The cleat stays with Pike. The book stays with Rook, who is the only person in this yard who can read it and who writes in his own right hand. The pump is not ours and is not to be written into any plan as though it were. The boat is not ours. The air pot is a third gone and a third of that pot is a hundred marks a third and no amount bought since the fifteenth of October has put a mark back into it. The note stands at eighteen marks and has had nothing on it since the seventh of January. The wages of the boy stand at four a week from the first of February and the claim of forty marks stands whole and is not reduced. Forty marks to Mara Venn stand, first after food, and are not paid and are not to be put in any plan as a thing to be found. The twenty marks of shoal iron on the stack are the bond of the charter at a clerk's thirteen and a third of the sale is entered to Sella Brigg for February and none of it has been sold.*

*And this is entered with a date, in the hand of the person who is losing it, and the losing of it happened on the twenty-ninth day of October and was not entered until today: the rescue cover of the *Low Lantern* inside the line, offered by Sella Brigg on her own statement and withdrawn by her on her own statement, is gone for the winter. The only cover this yard has is Harl Dace of the *Grey Widow*, inside the line and not past the marks, and it was not asked for and has not been asked, and this yard does not know whether it stands after a week in which a boy put a ruined arm into a fall, and that is the first sentence in this book that ends in a question and it ends there on purpose.*

That was the third time in four months that a woman in that yard had signed away something the yard needed, and she was not thanked and did not want to be.

And on Monday afternoon, with the harbor's crane going over on the fish dock and the tide making, Calder Marr lost a sound.

It had been going for five months and he had arranged for it the way he arranged for everything, by asking four other people to tell him what the bell was, and it had worked all that time, and on the Monday it did not. There were three people in the shed and the harbor's crane was going outside and a gull was on the roof and he could not put the crane where it was in the room, and the crane was on his left, which was the side the boy usually spoke from, and the crane was not on the left, and he stood in the middle of the floor with a slate in his hand for about four seconds without moving.

"Nessa," he said. "Say the last sentence again."

She said it again.

"Say it again slower."

She said it slower, and he wrote it down after her, and then Bevin Rook wrote it under his own, and then Calder Marr read his own back, which is the rule, and it came out right.

"That is four times since the fourteenth of October," Nessa Pike said, "and I have been counting, and there will be a fifth and there will be a twentieth, and there is nothing in this harbor or in Veyr that puts a mark in a book which takes it out of a man's head, and you are going to be a man who asks people to say things again for the rest of his working life, and if he does it well that is a trade and if he does it badly it is a disgrace, and there is no third thing."

"I know what it is."

"You know what it is today," she said. "I am telling you what it is in a year, because you are going to have a bad week in a year and I am not going to be standing in this shed being kind about it at the time."

She read the money out at four o'clock on the Monday and made the boy read it back twice, and the money in that week was the only money that moved in that week.

"Two marks of air on the Monday, and the pot is a third gone. A mark of rope. The bonesetter's second mark, a mark. And the week's bread and oil and candles for three men and a half, which is five marks, and which went onto the note at the sign of the Drowned Bell on the Tuesday and not out of the bag, because the bag is the only thing in this yard that a man in a shop is not allowed to look in.

"The bag was thirteen on Saturday night. It is four this afternoon. That is smaller than the figure on the sixth of December, and it is the first time the figure has been smaller since the eighteenth of November, and the note is at thirteen, and the note was at six when the yard was twenty marks richer in iron and a man with a hand that closed and a boat with a cradle under her, and I would like all three of those facts entered in this book tonight in that order, and then I would like the book shut."

Bevin Rook read it back twice and put the pencil on top of it, and Calder Marr took the slate off the shelf with the heading and the six paragraphs and the one false line on it and put it back in its place four feet from the book, and it gained nothing and it was not moved, and the first of March was six weeks and two days off, and it was a Wednesday, and the yard had four marks in a bag and nothing in a tin and a boy with a wage that started on a Tuesday in three weeks, and Calder Marr asked for a specific thing on a specific date, and was told a smaller thing, and took it.
