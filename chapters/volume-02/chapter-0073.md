# Chapter 73

*The Counter at the Morrow Circuit Office*

They were on the coast road at twenty-five minutes past five on Monday the sixth of March in the dark, because a day's walk starts when the light does and a day's walk that starts at nine is a day's walk that arrives at four with nothing done.

Calder Marr carried the ground in the inside of his coat with the copy Bevin Rook had made on the Sunday against it, and Nessa Pike carried nothing at all, which was her way of saying that a shipwright on a coast road is a woman walking and a paper is a woman carrying nothing.

They did not talk about the ground for the first three hours. At about two hours in Calder Marr asked her whether the counter would be open at eleven, and she said that a copying room is open when the clerks come and the clerks come at nine and a counter at eleven is a counter full of men who have been sitting down since nine, and Calder Marr said that a man who has been sitting down since nine is a man who has had a cup of something and is in a mood, and Nessa Pike said that this was the first useful thing he had said since five o'clock, and neither of them said anything else until the town.

---

The counter was a counter. There was a grille and a man behind it and a window about a foot high, and the man behind it was not old and was not the man from the fish market, and Calder Marr had been in this building before in November and had not been past the counter, and the room behind the grille had a smell of wet wool and a press and about nine feet of floor with a bench on it.

"Three marks," the man said. "The ground will be entered in the file and a receipt goes in the book. A copy of the entry goes to the party and the party keeps it. If the party wants the original it may have it and the office keeps a copy, and I have known parties want the original."

"Keep the copy," Calder Marr said. "The original is going on a wall in a market in three days and I would like the thing on the wall to be the thing you have entered."

The clerk looked at him for a second and then took the four pages and read all of them, and he read them the way the harbor authority's clerk reads things, which is to say that he read the whole of a thing before he answered any part of it, and it took him longer than it needed to because the fourth paragraph is long and the fourth paragraph is about somebody else's boat.

When he came to the end he squared the pages and put his thumb on the second one and said: "You have written that a crew called the *Sea Wren* is not a party to this instrument."

"That is correct."

"I have entered that crew's day in your yard's book on a matter of this office's file before now," the clerk said, "and I do not remember doing it and I do not remember refusing it, and the file will have it, and a file is a file and it does not keep what a man said to a man across a counter. I have written your paragraph into the file as it stands. I have not agreed with it, I have not disagreed with it, and I am not going to, because a counter is not a room."

"No," Calder Marr said. "It is not. Thank you."

"You are welcome and it is three marks."

---

The receipt said that the said ground had been received into the file of the request of the thirtieth of December at eleven o'clock in the morning of the sixth instant, that the fee of three marks had been taken, and that a copy of the entry was given to the party.

And then the man behind the grille took a second sheet off a spike to his left and turned it round and pushed it under the glass, and it was the request of the thirtieth of December, the four paragraphs, the same type, and a seal on the second sheet, and the seal was not the first seal and it was not the second seal.

"That came this morning," the clerk said.

"Read it out," Nessa Pike said, from Calder Marr's left shoulder.

"It is nineteen words and it is in the office's type and I am not going to read it out through a grille, and neither is the office, and you may read it yourself and if you have a question about it you may ask it now while I am at the counter and not on Friday."

Calder Marr read it.

*That a ground having been received, the said request stands unanswered in this office, and this office awaits the same.*

"That is the same nineteen words," he said, "with the first clause changed."

"It is the same nineteen words with the first clause changed," the clerk said, "and I will tell you one thing about that, because you have come a day for it and because nobody in this building is going to tell you otherwise. That line has been set in this office's type since before the year turned and it goes onto a request when an answer is not on the file, whichever way the answer failed, and it is not a decision and it is not a refusal and it is not a judgment about you, and in four years there will be a man in Veyr who reads three of those on one file and draws a conclusion, and he will be drawing it correctly from the paper, and there will be nothing on the paper to stop him."

"Read the date at the foot of it."

"It is dated the sixth of March and it is dated at the office and not at the counter, and the fourteen runs from the date at the foot and not from the day you had it in your hand, and that is on the first sheet of the request in a line the office set itself, and I am not going to be the man who explains a line of our own type to a party twice."

---

Calder Marr did the sum out loud, standing at a counter in a building he had never been past, and Nessa Pike checked it, and then he wrote it on the back of the fee receipt in pencil because there was no other paper in his pocket that was his.

The sixth of March, and fourteen days from the date at the foot, and the twentieth of March. A Saturday. Sixty-six days after the paper of the thirtieth of December, the second seal had been the twenty-seventh of January, and the third had come on the sixth of March, and in sixty-six days a file has three seals on it and no answer in it, and the answer is going to be a page in somebody's book and it is going to be in this drawer.

"How many of those has that file got on it now," he said. "Three."

"Three that I have seen this morning," the clerk said, "and I am a man who fills in a box and I will tell you what a box does. A box goes on a file. It does not go on a page. Nobody in this building reads a file unless a file has to be read, and a file has to be read when the office decides it, and if the office decides it then a clerk copies the file and the copy is what gets quoted, and the copy will have three seals on it and they will be set out one under the other in the order they were affixed, because that is what a copy is for."

"So the three lines are the part of this that survives."

"The three lines are the only part of this that anybody will read in two years," the clerk said, "and I have told you that for nothing, because you are the first party in four months to have stood at my grille and asked me a question about my own type, and I have had four months of parties coming in here and wanting a thing put on a page, and there is not one of them who ever once asked me what a page does."

Nessa Pike said, "That is the second man in a month who has told this yard what an office's own instrument is for, and the first one was a reeve in a barge, and I would like it entered somewhere, and not by either of them."

"It is entered in this yard's book on Wednesday morning," Calder Marr said, "in a boy's hand, and neither of these men will be named, because a man who does his trade and says one true thing to a party is not a party to anything and is owed nothing for it."

---

They were on the coast road again at twenty past ten with three marks spent and a receipt and a copy of the entry in a coat, and the fee had come out of a bag that had nine marks in it on Friday morning and had six marks in it by the time they were on the road, and they walked all day with nothing to say, and they came into Morrow Quay at about nine at night on the Tuesday with the third seal in the coat and the receipt with a pencil sum on the back of it.

The shed was shut and the boy was in it, alone, with the book, and he had not asked anybody to be sent for and had not asked anybody to come back, and he had had two days in a shed with a stove and a bench and no crew, and he had written nothing in the book for two days, and the first thing he said to Calder Marr when the door opened was:

"Sella Brigg came down on Tuesday at eight with twenty-two marks for a day's work on her own account and two men of her own, and I told her that this yard had nobody and no boat and would not be back until dark, and she said the cause out loud, which was the truth and not me, and she went up the hill. I put the whole of it in with the hour and there is no figure on it because I do not know what twenty-two marks is, it is hers and she said it out loud and I did not write it in her book."

"You should have written it in her book."

"I cannot write in her book, I can only write in ours," the boy said, "and ours is the record of our days and not of what we failed to do for a woman, and I have thought about that for two days and I would like Calder Marr to say whether I have it right or whether I am hiding behind a rule."

Calder Marr put the coat down and took the receipt out of it and looked at the pencil sum on the back of it for a while.

"You are hiding behind a rule," he said, "and the rule is right, and I am going to say the other half of it out loud because a man in this yard who only says the half that suits him is how a notice with four clauses happened. Enter the day, the hour, the cause, that the party was absent in a city on an instrument of the circuit office's, and that the holder does not dispute that the party might have been on this dock. Do not enter her figure. And put the entry under the Monday and not the Tuesday, because the day we lost is Monday, not Tuesday, and if anybody ever comes to this book about it I want the day to be the true one and not the day that looks better."

The boy wrote it and read it back, and Nessa Pike read it after him, and Calder Marr read the third seal's nineteen words out of the coat, and the boy wrote those under the entry, in his own right hand, with the date at the foot of the paper and not the day he had it, and the date was the sixth of March, and the fourteenth day from the date at the foot was the twentieth of March, and it was a Saturday, and Bevin Rook said the day out loud twice, because it was the kind of day that gets said out loud, and then he shut the book and put the pencil on top of it.

There were six marks in the bag, the note stood at one mark, the pump's iron was on the coast and would be fitted on Wednesday, the fifth week of a wage at four marks a week had fallen due on the Saturday and was not paid and was in a column with no adjectives in it, and the *Sea Wren* was at the pile under the *Vigil* with her pump in a lighter and her warps in a lighter, and in ten days a wall in the fish market was going to carry an eighth sheet, and the fourth of March had taken a pump and a boat and a day's work out of a harbor that already had one berth short.
