# Chapter 84

*The Fifth Entry*

The reeve's barge on the west basin had a stove in it and a ledge to write on and the ledger on a board across the knees of the clerk, and Ors Kest did not offer either of them a chair, because there were two chairs in that barge and both of them were in the way of the tide.

"Sit where you like," he said. "You have not come down here to be comfortable and neither have I, and it is half past eight on a Friday in April and there is a fleet in this basin that will be here until June."

Calder Marr had a reason written on a sheet of paper in his own left hand and he did not hand it over, and the clerk had the pen out and a page he had ruled himself and both of them knew what the order was.

"Read it out," Calder Marr said, "and put it in before you put anything else, and if I have said a thing in it you would rather I had not said, it goes in anyway, because that is the practice and you are the clerk and not me."

The clerk read it out and wrote it in, and it was a claim window, and it was the first thing of that kind Calder Marr had asked for since the seventeenth of January, and the difference between this one and that one was in the first line.

*That the party applies for a claim window upon one object, being one panel of worked glass of undetermined provenance brought into this harbor on the sixth of April instant by Harl Dace, master, of the *Grey Widow*, of no crew of this association, and held by him and not by the party; and that the object is claimed by nobody, is entered in the custody room of no office, has no number in this office or in the circuit office or in any other, and is not one of the three objects entered in the circuit custody room on the fourteenth of October; and that the purpose of the window is that the object be examined in daylight, above ground, on one date, being the tenth of April instant, on this office's own ground between the sixth and ninth seams of the mole, being the crown and not the toe; and that nothing is to be entered in the water at any time; and that no person is proposed to go in the water; and that no permit is asked for and no dive is proposed; and that the party is not asking this office to say what the object is, and that the party has no ground of its own upon that question; and that the party would like a conservator to be at the examination who has no office, and that this office is not asked to find her and will not be.*

Kest read it over the clerk's shoulder and then made the clerk read it to him, which is not usual and which the clerk remarked on.

"You read it to yourself and then the office reads it to the office," Kest said, "because in about two years a man in a city is going to read that page and I want him to know whether I read it or whether I was told it. Now. Four things, and then I will write the fifth thing down, which is the one I am going to write whether you asked for it or not, and you did ask for it, and I heard you ask, and I am not going to pretend otherwise in a ledger."

"Ask the four."

"First. Whose object is it."

"His," Calder Marr said. "It is in his own keeping and it is going to stay in his own keeping, and the reason we are here is that a conservator cannot be asked to look at a thing in a sailcloth on a fish dock in the rain, and the harbor authority is the only office in this coast that can put a date and a place and a daylight on a thing being looked at."

"Then no bond."

"Then no bond."

"That is not a decision of mine," Kest said, "and I want the ledger to have it in my words and not yours. A bond is pledge of the party's own. The party's own is nothing, and the object is not the party's, and so there is nothing for this office to hold and nothing for a man to be surety for, and a yard with no charter and no bond and no instrument may examine one object in daylight on one date provided it does not go in the water. That is a nicety. It is the whole of your right to stand on this mole this month and you did not earn it and I have not given it to you, and if you want it in writing in your own name on your own wall I will not stop you and I would think less of you."

"It goes in the ledger in your own words and not on our wall," Nessa Pike said, and she had come down the mole steps without being asked and stood at the barge's side with her hand on the gunwale, and Kest looked at her for a second and did not comment, which is a thing Kest has never once done and which she had noticed.

Harl Dace had come down with them and had not been in the barge, because a man who is not a party is not put in a barge, and he was standing on the mole steps above the gunwale with his hands at his back.

"Mr Dace," Kest said, without turning round. "The window says the object is in your keeping and claims nothing. In this office's language that makes you a holder and not a custodian, and a holder may be asked for a thing and may not be made to give it up, and a custodian may be asked by anybody and must account to somebody. You are the first. Do you know the difference."

"I do not know the difference," Dace said, "and I have been carrying that thing in my boat since the autumn because a man told me to, and I did not know I was one of the two and I would rather be the one that may be asked and cannot be made."

"So would anybody with sense," Kest said, "and it is the only sentence in this morning's page that I have not written myself, and I am having the clerk enter it, and enter whose mouth it came out of, because in four years somebody will want to know that this office said a thing to a man and the man agreed with it."

"And I am not going to be named in your ledger as being in agreement with a reeve," Nessa Pike said, "so read the next question out loud and let the boy hear it, because he is the one who will have to write it down and he should hear it before he hears it."

"Second. What is the examination for."

"To find out whether a mark on the face of it is what it says it is," Calder Marr said, "and by a person whose trade that is, and the person has no office and I am not asking your office to find her."

"Third. What is to be entered."

"Nothing is to be entered," the clerk said, before Kest asked, and Kest let him.

"Fourth." Kest shut the folio. "You have written in it that no person is to go in the water, and that is a thing I want in a municipal book about a claim window, because a man reading this page in a city in four years is going to assume that a party with a claim window went under something, and if he assumes it then the office that let it happen is the office that let a man go under a closure this office is inside. So the fifth thing is mine, it is long, and you are going to let me put it in."

"Go on," Calder Marr said.

"The first clause of the notice served on this harbor by the circuit office on the thirty-first of October closes the mole's toe and inner face from the third seam to the eleventh, to all work and to all approach by all persons, which includes this office and this yard and any other crew in this harbor. **This office has no power to lift that notice and is not lifting it and has never lifted it.** This office is issuing an instrument that will put a party, an object and a clerk on the mole's own ground between the sixth and ninth seams on the tenth of April, and this office is treating the whole of the mole from the third seam to the eleventh as inside that first clause for the purposes of this instrument, whether the crown is inside it or not, and it is treating it that way on purpose, because a conservative reading of a notice is not a narrow one and this office has spent three months being told in its own words what happens when it is not. This is the fifth entry this office has made in its own book of putting itself inside a closure it did not create, and the first four were on a permit of the eighth of January, a reason of the twelfth, a claim window of the seventeenth and a line written under a reason on the nineteenth, and a man in Veyr is going to find five of them in one ledger, and he is going to find that the fifth is the only one that a party asked for. I have the cost of it and it is mine."

He said that at a fair speed and he did not read it twice, and the clerk wrote it and read it back and did not shorten it, and there were four of them in the barge and three of them heard a reeve put the fifth of a thing on the page in his own voice and say he had asked for it.

---

Then he shut the book again and did the thing he had actually come down for, which was a warning, and he gave it out loud and not in any form.

"Two things and then you can go up the hill. First. This office cannot seize that object and will not pretend to any power it does not have, and I have said a sentence like that in this basin before and I will not say it twice in one morning. Second, and this is the one you came for whether you know it or not." He put one hand flat on the ledger. "A claim seal on a sheet of glass is not a piece of paper. It is a thing an office can act on. The circuit office of this coast decides provenance and custody and quarantine, and it has wanted the yard's record since the thirtieth of December and has asked four times, and it is now correct about the number of copies and it is wrong about a great deal else, and an office that has been asking for a party's book since December is an office that would be extremely interested in a piece of glass with one of its own instruments struck on the face of it, sitting in a harbor where a party is holding nothing at all."

"You are telling me they will ask for it," Calder Marr said.

"I am telling you that they will ask for it and that you will not be able to say no in a way that reads like a no. That is the position. A party with an instrument says no inside an instrument. A party with nothing in its hand says no out loud, in a room, and a man in a city writes down *the party declined* and puts it in a file, and a file is what gets copied, and what gets copied is what gets quoted. I have watched that happen in this harbor to a man's answer four times this winter and I am not going to watch it happen to a sheet of glass." He looked up. "Your protection is not holding it. Your protection is never having had it."

"So what do I do with a claim window."

"You do the one thing you have done since the fourteenth of October," Kest said, "which is write down the day, and let the four men who were there read it back, and then give the thing back to the man it belongs to, and be able to say in four years that it was never in your keeping at all."

Nessa Pike said, "That costs the yard the only claim it has ever had to a real object."

"Yes," said Kest. "It does. And I am not going to pretend to you that I know which of the two of those is worth more to you in April, and I am telling you on the Friday and not on the Monday, and you will not be asked me again."

---

Calder Marr went up the hill at ten and the clerk came up the fish dock at half past four with the page, and the boy read the window into the yard's book in two columns on his own, the first column being the object and the date and the place and the four names, and the second column being the thing that the object is not, which was a wreck, and a claimed thing, and a thing in any custody room, and the reason for that column being that no man on a mole can check where a thing has been kept since October.

And air went on the slate at two marks out of a bag of twelve, because there was a day coming on the Thursday of next week that would want a pot filled and a pot is filled before it is wanted, and the bag was ten, the note was twenty-seven, the tin was where it has been since the first of February, and the fifth of a reeve's own breaches of his office's notice was in a ledger on the west basin in a hand nobody in this harbor could improve on.
