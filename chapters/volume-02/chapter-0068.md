# Chapter 68

*Tarred Marline*

The wind came round hard in the night of Saturday the twenty-fifth and on Sunday the twenty-sixth of February it sat in the north-east with the glass still falling, and Calder Marr put the going out on the top of the flood on the Monday because the rope on the core was bought on the eleventh of January and a rope lasts a year and the ground line wants feeling over with a hand twice a year, and because Corvin Sarn is the harbor's bellwright and this is the harbor's ground and neither of those facts had changed since the eleventh.

The going out cost eight marks and a day of a boat.

It was paid out of the same money the yard was going to lay ninety fathoms of marline with, on the same day, in the same weather, and Calder Marr made the boy write the two on the same line in the book, under one another, with a rule between them, and then made him read the eight back twice, because it was the first time since January that the going out had come out of the yard's own pocket instead of the harbor's, and it had been eight marks and a day of a boat on the eleventh of January too, and the figure had not moved in six weeks and would not move again in a year, and the whole of the point of a signal is that it is the only line in a ledger that is the same on every page.

Corvin Sarn came out in the boat because the ground was the harbor's and the gear was the harbor's and the fouling on the waist could not be cleaned and must not be. He had his thumb on the ground line where it came through the water and he said nothing at all for about twenty minutes, which was the most anybody in that boat had ever heard from him.

"Line's good," he said. "Stone under it is the same stone. Waist is the same waist and there is more of the weed on the seaward face than there was in January and the scour is white and clean down the middle, and the tongue is where it was, and the eye is sound." He wiped his hand on his thigh. "And in a year this ground line wants feeling over again, and in two years the rope wants looking at, and in four or five the tongue wants a man's day, and if this harbor ever stops sending a boat out here then it stops being a signal and it starts being a rock, and the only person who will ever know the day it stopped is a man in Veyr in four years reading a book and finding nothing in it."

They were off the south end at ten minutes past nine, at the top of the flood, with a light sea running inside the harbor and a hard one outside it, and the harbor's tide bell went at nine minutes past ten.

Under the third stroke the answer came up out of the core a hand's width of a second later, and it was low, and it was even, and it was the note, three marks and a flick, and it stopped when the bell stopped, and there was something in it.

"Eleven," Bevin Rook said, without being asked, and his right hand was already on the page. "Eleven logged since the twenty-sixth of October, and the span is from the twenty-sixth of October, and the water is at the top of the flood with a hard north-easter outside the harbor and a light one inside, and the harbor's three went at nine minutes past ten and the answer came a hand's width of a second under the third, and the second is the same second as the nineteenth of January and the eleventh and the sixth of December, and there is a call in it and it is longer than the one of the twenty-sixth of December and it is further off, and it is a grown man's and it is not written as a voice, and there is no source, and I am entering that there have been two occasions logged with no call in them, the eleventh of January and the nineteenth of January, and that this is not one of them, and that the count of those is two and it is a fact and it is not a question, and nobody in this boat has asked me a question about it and I have not got one."

"Read it back," Nessa Pike said.

He read it back, and Calder Marr read it back after him, and Corvin Sarn read it back, and the fourth man read it back after Sarn, and that is four, and the rule is that four men read the same day it is written, and the four were the diver's tender, the boy, a shipwright and a bellwright, and not one of them could place the call and not one of them asked, and Bevin Rook entered that they had not asked, in his own right hand, with the date, and did not say what it was about.

"That is a call in it," Corvin Sarn said, when the four had finished, "and I have heard that thing answer nine times in five months and I have never heard a voice in it, and I am a man who works with bells and I am telling you that I heard one and that I do not know what it is, and I would like it in the book in my words."

"Enter it," Nessa Pike said. "In his words, in his own hand, and not shortened, and put the nine and the five months in it."

---

The marline came out of the authority's store at eight on the Monday morning in a handcart, ninety fathoms of it on a reel, tarred and stiff, and it was the best thing in that store and there was nothing else in that store worth having.

Nessa Pike laid it out along the wall's foot in four bights before anybody touched it, because ninety fathoms of tarred marline in a heap in a north-easter is a thing that puts itself in the sea, and the boy marked the bights in chalk with the numbers on the wall so that the figure of the wall and the figure of the book were the same, and there are forty herring skippers' marks in chalk on the north wall of Morrow Quay and every one of them was read by a man with a book before anything was moved.

Crale was on the wall at half past nine with the shackle and the heaving line, and Calder Marr had the lead, and Nessa Pike had the coil and the cleat, and Bevin Rook sat on a stone at the head of the wall with the book on his knees and the slate tied to it and the tide in front of him, and he called the time and the state of the water and the depth of the float at the wall's head every four minutes without being asked, in a flat voice, for four and a half hours, and by the afternoon he had called it sixty-eight times and had not once been told he was doing it wrong.

The shackle came from the harbor authority's own moorings gear and Calder Marr had asked for a better one on the Thursday and had been told in the reeve's own barge that there was not a better one, and that what there was was what there had been since the fenders, and that this office was not going to buy iron in the last week of February with a fleet coming in.

"It is the office's own iron," Calder said, on the wall, to nobody, at about eleven. "If it goes, it is the office's. Enter that in the first column and not in the second."

It went at twenty past twelve.

The surge came up the harbor on the ebb and came hard against the wall's head with a north-easter behind it, and the shackle took the load the way a shackle takes a load when it has been in salt water since before a war, and it opened where the pin had been, and forty feet of the harbor authority's tarred marline went over the wall head and out into the harbor in about two seconds with a sound nobody on the wall could describe afterwards except one of them, who said it was the sound of a rope being told something.

It was gone in about four minutes. The harbor was three-quarters of a mile wide and the ebb was setting hard to the seaward and nobody in Morrow Quay was going to see forty feet of marline again.

Nobody shouted, because the thing to do was done inside four seconds, and what was done inside four seconds was that Crale sat down on the wall where he had been standing, and Calder Marr took the heaving line's tail and put it round the stone post and took a turn on it with his right hand and his left arm across his chest, and the line came tight with the whole of the rest of the reel still on the wall, and the wall did not move.

Then they had to get the reel's weight off the shackle, which was a new shackle, off a stone post, in a surge, with four men and a boy on a stone.

"Call it," Calder Marr said, to the boy on the stone, and the boy's voice came down as flat as it comes.

"Twelve twenty-one. Half past the flood, and the ebb is half an hour old, and there is a hard northeast against the wall and the float at the head is a foot and a half and it has been a foot and a half for four minutes and it is going to three."

"Three what."

"It is going to three and it is going now. I cannot check it from a boat. I can see it off the float line and I cannot check it and it goes in the second column."

"Put it in the second column and keep calling it."

It took nineteen minutes and the reel came off the wall and went into the boat and Ness got a new shackle through a stone post that was never meant to have a shackle through it, and the forty feet of the harbor authority's marline was in the sea, and the rest of it lay along the wall's foot in four bights with the numbers chalked on the stones, and the fleet had fifty fathoms of winter warp on a wall that wanted ninety.

"Enter it," Nessa Pike said, at ten past one, when the wall was quiet again. "Hour, the number, the state of the water, that the shackle was of the harbor authority's own moorings gear and was not of this yard's and was not new, that the yard asked for a better one on Thursday the twenty-third and was told by the harbor authority in its own barge that there was not one, and that this office was not going to buy iron in the last week of February. Put all of that in the first column, because all of it can be checked by asking a man who was on this wall or in that barge."

"Put the loss in the second column," Bevin Rook said. "Forty feet is forty feet and I could not check it. I have got his mark on it, because he is the first man I have ever seen in five months put a mark in a book for a thing he did not want to sign, and it is going in with the forty feet, and if anybody comes back to this page in four years the two are on the same line."

They laid the remaining fifty fathoms by four in the afternoon, and the last shackle held, and the boy's slate had eighty-one calls on it and the last of them was the state of the water at the head of the north wall, a foot and a half, a north-easter, and the ebb gone slack.

---

The money went in on the Monday night and the boy read it out and read it back twice, and the column on the slate of the twenty-third of February came true, to the mark.

"Gross on the board, twenty-six, from the harbor authority, for one day, laying ninety fathoms of its own warp on its own wall on its own account, and nothing raised, and the eighth and the share off the top of the day.

"Three and three. Air, two marks, and the pot is a third gone. Rope, two marks, eight fathoms hired of the net loft. A hand, four marks, two marks a day for two days, to Wend Crale of the *Sea Wren*, hired out and not lent, and the second day is the twenty-eighth and it goes in the twenty-eighth's column and not in this one, because a man hired for two days is hired for both of them and the yard is working on the twenty-eighth and will pay him on it out of that day's column.

"That is fourteen out of twenty-six and the yard's is twelve, and the bag had one mark on Thursday the twenty-third and it has thirteen, and the tin has two crowns in it that went in on the first of February and have not been spent, and the note stands at four, and on Tuesday the twenty-eighth a fortnight of bread, oil and candles goes onto that note in the creditor's own hand and comes out of this bag, and the figure on the note will be one and the figure in the bag will be ten, and I am entering both of them tonight so that nobody has to do it in the morning."

"Read the last of it back."

"And the going out," Bevin Rook said, "eight marks and a day of a boat, paid out of the same column, entered under a rule because it is not this office's money and not this yard's ordinary work and it is the second time since the sixth of December that this yard has paid for going out of its own pocket, and the figure is eight and it was eight on the eleventh of January and it will be eight in a year, and I have entered the six weeks between the two eightes because somebody is going to ask in a year whether the going out got dearer and the answer is that it did not get dearer, it got the same, which is what a signal is."

They read that back too, and the book went on the bench and the pencil went on top of it, and at about half past seven the north-easter dropped half a tone and the fly in that shed went dead for the first time in three days, and the yard had thirteen marks in a bag, and a note at four, and two crowns in a tin, and fifty fathoms of the harbor authority's winter warp lying along the north wall with the fleet's marks in chalk above them, and forty feet of it in the sea somewhere between the wall and the bar with nobody in this harbor who will ever see it again, and the first of March was a Wednesday and three days off, and there was a north berth out of service and a berth short.
