# Chapter 91

*A Wharfinger and a Handbell*

The eleventh week of a wage at four marks a week fell due on Tuesday the eighteenth of April and Bevin Rook wrote it in the column with no adjectives in it before the kettle was off the stove, and the column was seven lines deep and twenty-eight marks in it, the fifth of March the oldest of the seven, and on the Monday the twenty-fourth he read the whole position back at four o'clock in the afternoon to a man who was standing in the doorway of that shed with water running off him onto the boards, and the man was not listening to the position and the boy read all of it anyway, because that is the rule and because he had been eleven days without an occasion to read it back to anybody.

Three marks in a bag. Thirty on the chandler's note, and it went up three on Thursday the twentieth for a fortnight of bread, oil and candles in the creditor's own hand and out of that same bag the same afternoon, so that the note is at thirty-three and the bag is at nothing, and those two figures are the same three marks and not two sets of them. Two crowns in a tin, and they have been in it since the first of February. Seven lines in the wage column and twenty-eight marks in it, and on Tuesday the twenty-fifth it goes to eight lines and thirty-two, which is the deepest that column has ever been in this yard's book. Forty marks of claim standing whole. Forty marks to a conservator with no office, standing, first after food, unpaid since the fifteenth of October, and not one mark smaller because a shop on the quay at Ashtide refused her four for an opinion she gave in a market on the twelfth. Eight marks and a day of a boat to this harbor's bellwright, owing since the twenty-seventh of February, and there is no eight marks in this harbor before October and there is not going to be. Eight shillings and tenpence to Sella Brigg in her own name, undiminished, unpaid since the sixth of February, and the stack of iron it came off is still on the yard's own ground, unsold, worth twenty marks at a fair sale to a clerk's thirteen, with a saw kerf in one crown shackle that nobody has accounted for. A pump standing on its cart in the shed with the hose coiled, and a pot of air bought on the seventh of April standing unopened behind the pump housing with the strap undone and nothing done to it, and a second pot two-thirds gone.

"That is the position," the boy said, "and you did not ask me for it."

"I did not," said the man in the doorway.

---

He was the wharfinger of the second lay and he was about sixty and his coat was heavier than it needed to be for an April afternoon, which is how Calder Marr knew he had come out of the water and not off a quay.

"My name is Ewan Tristram," the man said. "I take the dues on three wharves and I keep the keys. There is a man called Neale and he is my wife's brother and he will tell you I have come on an errand of his office and he will be telling the truth and it will not be the reason.

"The reason is a shed with shavings in it, the coal store at the second lay, and the third lay's boat went alongside that wharf at eleven this morning with forty men on her and she is still there, and at two o'clock this afternoon somebody saw smoke coming out of the roof of that store, and it has been raining glass since Friday and it does not matter whether the glass has been falling since Friday or since Candlemas, a store with shavings in it does not want a man in it.

"Neale got the handbell off the mole head and rang it and got a fire party, and here is where I am. This harbor authority has no chart of a fire in the second lay. It has two hose carts, one on each wharf, and neither of them has ever been pumped, and the cistern behind the engine house is charged on a rota by a man who walks out to the sluice on a Monday and a Thursday, and the man did not walk out on Monday because this office cannot pay a man to walk out to a sluice, and there is about a cart's worth of water in that cistern and no more than that. And the third lay's boat is lying against the wharf where the fire is, so the party cannot get to it with a cart, so the party is going to have to go round to the other side of her and carry it, and forty men are standing in a line in six inches of water in the harbour with buckets already, and I have been in this harbor fifty-one years and I have never seen a harbor do both at once."

"Have you sent for anybody," Nessa Pike said.

"I have sent for the third lay's boats to come off that wharf and four of them cannot move for the hose and the cart, and I have sent for the man at the engine house, who is a boy of nineteen who does not come out for a handbell because a handbell means the mole head and the mole head is four hundred yards away and he is not on the mole head, and I have sent for a man called Garrow who is the first man I have ever paid to come when I sent for him and he has not come, and I have sent for a pump and a man to work it, and there is one pump in this harbor that is not the harbor's and it is standing in a shed on this hill with the hose coiled and a man who has not been on the water since February because a notice of the circuit office has his name in it."

He stopped there, and put his hand on the door frame to hold himself up, and it was not a performance; Calder Marr could see that it was not a performance, because a man performing does not use the door frame.

---

The boy went down the hill with a slate and came back in eleven minutes with a number in it.

"Neale asked me," he said, at the top of the steps, in the way a man reports a thing he has been sent to find out. "Not you. Me. He said, in front of the fire party, that he wanted the third lay's strength out and did not want to send round for it because every boat in that lay is a herring boat and a herring skipper will tell you how many men he has got and a handbell is not an argument. And I said the number, and the number is nineteen, and Neale wrote it on the back of his own hand and has read it back once to a man of the second lay and once to a man of no lay.

"Nineteen, out of the third lay's ordinary thirty-one. Twelve are here, on the bucket chain or carrying the cart round. Five are at the hard with the *Kittiwake*'s cradle that is not under her. And two are not here and one of the two is not anywhere anybody in this harbor knows of, and he has been out since the twenty-eighth of February, and his name is not one I am going to say in a fire, and Neale has known which one he was taking off the count since the twenty-first of March and has not said it either."

"Write nineteen and the hour," Nessa Pike said. "Write that the count is a count of men on the ground at the time of asking and is not a list, and that the boy did not give a name and was not asked for one."

"Write that the fire party asked the boy and not the yard," the boy said, "and that the yard did not send him."

"That is in the first column or it is not in the book at all," Nessa Pike said.

Then she said the thing she had come down to say, and she said it to Calder Marr and not to the wharfinger, in a voice that carried about two feet and no further.

"I am not having this yard's men in that fire. Not one of them, and not you, and not the boy. **The third lay's fire party is not the yard's fire party and the yard is not the town's fire party and the day it starts being one is the day this shed is a thing people run to.** Neale has a party and a handbell and a hundred and forty years of other people's houses between him and the water. We have a boy who cannot hold a pencil properly after four hours and a pump that is worth four marks and a man of fifty-one with a bad wheel who was in this harbor on Friday. Go and take the pump."

"Take the pump to the second lay," Ewan Tristram said.

"Take the pump to the second lay and leave it on the wharf with a man in it, and that man will not be of this party, and he will be paid out of what the harbor authority pays, and I want all three of those things in a book before the pump leaves this shed." She was already untying the coil. "And Calder, the air is not going. The pot on the seventh has been standing three weeks and we cannot know what is happening in it, and if I stand here and hear about a cistern I am going to say it out loud, and it is going to be in the second column, and the reason is that no man in the second lay could look inside a pot in a shed on this hill."

"Write it," Calder Marr said.

---

The pump went down the hill at twenty past four on the cart with Neale's own boy to steer it, and the yard's own air pot stayed where it was, and the boy wrote the whole of the afternoon afterwards in a shed that was already losing its roof.

The fire was in the coal store at about half past four and it was in the roof before anybody had the cart round the third lay's boat, and by five o'clock the store was going and the rain was going into it and the wind was round the corner of the second lay doing the thing a wind does in a harbor with one berth out of service and forty feet of a warp on the north wall that ought to have been ninety, and every cinder in that harbor that could be lifted was lifted and went on the second lay.

"Water in the bulkhead before the bulkhead is reached," the boy said, at the top of the dock, to nobody.

Nobody answered him and he wrote it down, which is what he had said he would do, and then the wind took the fly off the shed and by nine the rain had found the bench at the far end and a man of sixty-one had to move off it, and he moved it himself and did not speak to anybody, and Calder Marr did not go over to him.

---

The thing that made the day was a length of tarred hemp on the ground between the third lay's wharf and the second lay's, and it went from the wharf's own eye in a coil that had been there since the winter, and it was four hundred feet of herring warp that had cost a man twenty-four marks in February and had been lying on a wharf in the open because a harbor authority's repair account bought four sets of moorings and a season of warp in January and there was nothing left to lay a warp on anything else with, and the hose cart could not get round the third lay's boat without it moving.

It was moved by two men of Edric Pringle's.

"Take my warp off and take that chain out of my wharf," Edric Pringle said, at the head of the dock, at about ten past five, in oilskins with the water standing in the wrinkles of them. "And I want the cause said by you and not by me, because I will say it badly. I am fifty-four and I have been difficult about nine marks with three men in this harbor and one of them is this one, and I say the cause first and I say it in your own book and I do not want it softened and I do not want to be thanked for it either, because I have not done a thing for you, I have done a thing for forty men standing in six inches of water.

"I want it written that I said it first and that the cause is the fire party of this harbor authority and its own order and not the yard, and that the chain and the cart came out of the second lay, and that the third lay's boat was lying against the wharf with forty of her men in a bucket chain at the time, and that about four hundred feet of my own warp was taken off the wharf and most of it is in the harbour and not on it, and that the water in the harbour is where I can see it from this step and it is not coming back tonight and I know exactly what a warp in harbour is worth in the spring, which is nothing, and I know exactly what it is worth to me now, which is everything, and I have written the winter figure on the end of a coil for nobody and I am not going to write it for you either because it is a lie by the ninth of May."

"Write the cause first and in her words," Nessa Pike said, "and the hour, and that the reeve's own hand is on the order and that she is not named by the office because she has not asked to be."

---

The hose was canvas and the canvas went in about six minutes, and it was the yard's hose and it had been re-made twice since November out of the same coil, and by six o'clock there was no pressure in the second lay at all and the fire party was working round the back of the third lay's boat in the dark with buckets and a wet mill and a line of men to a pump seventy yards off.

It was out at about half past nine. Nobody was hurt. It was not said in the market that this was a near thing and it was said in the book, with the hour, that the water reached the bulkhead at seven and the store's roof came in at ten past five and the party got round the third lay's boat's quarter at about ten past five, and that the third lay's boat did not move and could not, and that the reason she could not move was the warp, and that the reason the warp was on that wharf at all was an account that had been empty since the middle of February.

Nobody read that page back that night because there was no fourth man in that shed and a page about a fire is not a page four men have to be answerable for, and the boy wrote it and put the pencil on top of the book, and the wind dropped about eleven and the rain went on, and the glass that had been falling for twenty-one days went on falling, and the position in the yard's book on Monday the twenty-fourth of April was **no instrument, no claim, no paper of any kind, and the ninth seam's mooring in service with a pole against it, and a handbell rung on the mole head at four o'clock by a man whose own tide gang has been three men short since the first of April.**
