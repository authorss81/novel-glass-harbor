# Chapter 67

*The Bill for the Ninth Seam*

The wind came round in the night of Friday the twentieth and on the Wednesday the twenty-second of February it had gone into the north-east, and Calder Marr was on the mole steps at half past eight in the morning with the tide making and the harbor's crane standing over the fish dock doing nothing at all, and he lost it.

It had been going for five months. He had done what he always did with it, which was to arrange for other people to tell him what the sound was, and it had worked all winter because the winter had been made of men who would stop and say it again. On the Monday the crane was over on his right, where nothing in that yard ever stood, and the wind was in it, and a gull was on the roof of the net loft, and Calder Marr stood on the top step with a slate in his hand and could not put a crane in a room for about five seconds without moving.

"Nessa," he said. "Say the last thing again."

She said it again. He wrote it down and the boy wrote it under his own and Calder read his back, which is the rule, and it came out right.

"That is five times since the fourteenth of October," Nessa Pike said, "and I have been counting, and there will be a sixth and there will be a fortieth, and there is nothing in this harbor or in Veyr that puts a mark in a book and takes it out of a man's head. What is in that man's head is a hole where a bell used to be and it fills in when somebody says the sentence out loud, and that is not a malady you can be got rid of and it is not a malady that makes you a worse diver. It is the price of two entries in a body that a man's body was not built for, and you paid it, and the coin went to somebody else's pocket and not to yours, and the somebody else is an office that will never write your name."

Nessa Pike said the last of that without softening it, and then she went down the steps to look at the mooring, and Calder Marr stood on the top step for a while and did not follow her for about a minute, which Bevin Rook entered in the book with the hour on it and without a question, and which was the fifth time in five months that a sound in Calder Marr's head had been written down as a fact in a yard's book rather than as a thing its owner had been advised about.

---

The fouling off the ninth seam went into the harbor authority's store on the Friday afternoon in a basket, and it is a piece of the mole's own face and it is not in any shed in this harbor and it does not go into a yard, and the notice's second clause has never once in five months been argued with by anybody, including the three men it names.

What came out of the water on the seventeenth was a shackle in a sleeve of the mole's own foundation stone, and a mooring of the harbor authority's own, made up on a new ring of the fourth set and in service from four o'clock in the afternoon, and Neale wrote the time on a slate of his own and the clerk entered it in the authority's book and the boy wrote the whole of the afternoon in two columns in the authority's book in his own right hand and the clerk initialled the foot of it and did not amend one word of it.

What did not come out of the water was the fouling on the ground line between the third seam and the ninth, which is the reason the harbor authority is out one mooring it owns, and which the fourth paragraph of the reason of the seventeenth named in full and which nobody had touched and nobody was going to touch in February, and which is now in the reason of a work in the authority's own book in Calder Marr's hand as a fact of the authority's own account and not of the authority's own work, and it will be read by whoever reads that file in four years, and there is no instrument in this harbor that can turn it into a piece of work.

And the yard was paid nothing for the whole of it, which was correct, and the harbor authority had paid Neale's gang a day and a hand crane out of its own repair account at two marks a day, and Calder Marr read that figure out of Kest's own book on the Wednesday morning at the reeve's table and entered it in the yard's book in the yard's column under other people's money, and the entry said, in the boy's hand, that the authority had paid a day of a gang and four marks of iron for a day's work on the authority's own ground, and that this yard had stood on the crown of a mole at a hundred yards from it in the rain and had written it down, and that this was what this yard was now for, on this ground, and that no office had told the yard so and the yard had arrived at it in five months and did not like it.

---

"You can write it down and you can hate it and both of those are yours to do," Nessa Pike said on the Wednesday, "and neither of them is a job. So write the next thing instead, which is what we are doing on the twenty-seventh, and whether we can afford it, because the money for the seventeenth is the money that is not in the bag."

The warp was on the authority's own shelves and had been bought out of the authority's own money on the seventeenth of January along with the fourth set of moorings, and it was ninety fathoms of tarred marline, and the herring fleet's winter warp had to be cut and laid along the north wall before the first of March or the forty boats come in with a wall that has had nothing on it for a season.

Neale said it on the Wednesday afternoon at the crane with the north-easter behind him, and he said it without being asked, which he had never done in twenty years of standing on that wall.

"I cannot lay it," he said. "I have three men stood by on a Friday and a Monday with nowhere to put the days, and the money for the warp went on the moorings and the moorings went on the mooring that your office's own notice closed, and I have got nine years of old warp on my shelves and I cannot use a foot of it under a fleet, and the three men are off on the twenty-fourth and they are not on again until the fourth of March because the first of March is a Wednesday and the week after it is a Wednesday and the men are on their own gear."

"Rowlandike and Petch and Wil Slee," Calder Marr said.

"That is the three of them." Neale shifted his feet. "And I would like the yard to hear it in my own mouth and not out of a book. I told the reeve on the seventeenth of January what that money would cost and he wrote it down and did not pretend it was somebody else's, and it has cost me three men in the last week of February, and the men have not been stood off for idleness, they have been stood off because a harbor authority spent its own repair account on four sets of moorings and a season of warp in the fortnight before it promised them in a hearing room. And one of the three is a man whose brother went down twice on Friday under my eye and came up both times, and he has not said one word to me about it, and I am not going to make him say it, and I am telling you because you will write it in a book and I would rather you had it from my mouth than from mine afterward."

"Enter all of it," Nessa Pike said. "And enter that we asked him and he answered in a straight line and that the three men are named and that the week they are off is the week before the first of March."

---

Then the second half of it, which was what the yard could actually do, and which took another two hours because the crew was a crew of three and a half and a boy with a wrist five weeks old.

The authority could hire the work. The authority had no money for the labor, which Kest said in his own voice in his own barge on the Thursday morning and did not apologize for, and the rate was the authority's rate, and the rate was twenty-six for the day, and the eighth and the share came off the top of the day because they followed the yard in whatever it did.

"That is a bad rate for a bad week," Tarner said, when Calder Marr told him, "and I know it is a bad rate, and I am saying so before you find out, because my whole point in this harbor is saying things before somebody finds out."

"You have a hand who can lay marline."

"I have a boy of fifteen called Piet Marrow who cannot read and a man called Wend Crale who can lay marline as well as anybody on this coast and who owes me nothing and who will come for two marks a day and his own victuals, and I am not lending him, I am hiring him out, and I am entering the two marks as a hire and not as a kindness, because that is the sentence you used in this shed on the twenty-sixth of January about my pump and I have thought about it since."

"And I owe you for him on the day," Nessa Pike said, "and not on a Friday, and if the yard has nothing on the day then he is not paid on the day and he is paid on the next day with the reason in this book, and I would like that written down now, in front of him, so that a man who has come from up the hill and taken two days of a blow for a yard with one mark in it is not surprised on the twenty-eighth."

"It is written down," the boy said, from the bench, and he had it written down, and it was the first time in five months that he had written a line in the yard's book that was a promise to somebody outside the yard about money that had not arrived, and he read it back twice, and nobody in the shed told him not to.

The arithmetic went up on the slate about two o'clock on the Thursday and it was the boy's arithmetic and not Calder Marr's, and the boy had put the three figures in a column and the three outgoings under them, and it is in the book in his own right hand on the twenty-third of February.

*Twenty-six gross for one day, on the authority's own account, laid by this yard, at the rate the authority's rate is and not at a rate this yard would have asked, which is thirty-one and would have been refused.*

*The harbor's eighth, three. The harbor's share, three. That is six off the top of a day and not off a raising, and nothing is to be raised, and there is nothing to raise on the north wall because the warp is already the authority's.*

*Air, two marks. Rope, two marks, being eight fathoms hired of the net loft for the hauling line and a coil of the yard's own for the heaving line. A hand, two marks a day for two days, four marks, to Wend Crale of the Sea Wren, hired out, not lent, at two a day and his own victuals, and the two a day is his rate and not ours and I have written it at his rate and not at a cheaper one.*

*That is fourteen out of twenty-six and the yard's is twelve, and the bag has one mark in it, and one and twelve is thirteen, and on the twenty-eighth a fortnight of bread, oil and candles goes onto the note at the sign of the Drowned Bell and comes out of this bag, and I am writing that the figure on this page on the twenty-third is thirteen and the figure on the note on the twenty-eighth is four, and if the twenty-eighth goes the other way then this page is wrong and somebody will have to come back to it and correct it with a date on it.*

Nobody in that shed argued with a column of arithmetic done by a boy, and Calder Marr looked at it for a long time and then said the thing he had come in the door to say and had not said for two hours.

"You have cost us two marks a day and two days," he said. "I would like it entered that I would not have hired him, and that I would not have found him, and that the yard does not have anybody else and that I have been sitting in this shed since the fourth of January treating a crew of three as a crew of three and a half, and the half is a boy who is owed a wage and cannot take a warp, and the arithmetic in front of me has known that since the fifteenth of January and I have not."

"Enter it," Nessa Pike said. "In his own right hand, with the date, and the boy will read it back twice."

And then, on the Thursday evening, on the mole steps, with the wind in it, Calder Marr stood next to Bevin Rook while the boy shut the market wall's copy of the day's figures into the wall and did the thing he did every week, which was check the wall against the book.

And Calder Marr came within four inches of asking him what had come out of a man's mouth on the eighteenth of January.

It had been six weeks. The boy had written *not written as a voice* in a column on the nineteenth, and had said out loud in this shed on the eleventh of January that he was the only person in it who was not going to ask, and had entered the not-asking again on the nineteenth, in his own right hand, and had on the twenty-second of January entered a third of them beside a column of figures about a boy who is not a hand, and had done all of it at nobody's asking.

He did not ask. He stood on the mole steps in the wind and he watched the boy put the wall sheet flat and check the figures against the book, and what he said instead was:

"You are owed four marks on Saturday that you have told me you are not going to ask for."

"I have not asked."

"You have not asked, and you entered that you had not asked, in a book, on a Saturday, which is the cleverest thing anybody in this harbor has done since the eighth of December and you did it in a shed with a pencil and nobody in the room except me."

The boy put the book under his arm.

"You did not ask me either," he said, "on the seventeenth, on the mole, in the rain, at about half past ten in the morning while a man was in the water under a stone, and you have been not asking me for six weeks, and I want it entered that I noticed and that I am not going to do anything about it, and that if you ever do ask me I will answer you, and that you have not asked me, and that I have not asked you, and that there are two of us now keeping a thing out of a book and that is twice as safe and half as honest, and I would rather it was half as honest than twice as safe, and I would like somebody to write that down one day when there is not a wind."

"Enter it," Calder Marr said, "in your own right hand, with the date, and the fact that I did not ask is mine and not yours."

The boy entered it, and read it back twice, and did not say one word about what was in it, and the wind came round off the mole and took the words out of the yard's own ground the way it does, and down at the south end the core was under eight and a half feet of water with a fouled waist and a tongue half worn through, and the harbor's tide bell went at ten minutes past five in the road, and a hand's width of a second under the third stroke the water gave back three marks and a flick, and Calder Marr heard it with his left ear and did not write it down and did not ask anybody to write it down, and the one person who could have taken it into a book was standing beside him with the book shut under his arm and his hand flat on the cover, and the two of them stood on the top step of a mole in a north-easter and let it go by, and it was not an occasion, because an occasion is a thing that a man goes out in a boat to find out and neither of them had gone out for anything.
