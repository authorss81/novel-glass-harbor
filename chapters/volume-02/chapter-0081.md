# Chapter 81

*A Job Without a Paper*

The ninth week of a wage at four marks a week fell due on Tuesday the fourth of April, and Bevin Rook wrote it in the column with no adjectives in it before the kettle was off the stove, and the column was nine lines deep, and not one of the nine had ever been argued about in that shed, which was the whole of the practice and was also, that morning, the whole of what the yard had.

The market was not open yet. Down on the boards there was a man standing at the head of the fish dock with his back to the water and a hand lead coiled over his shoulder, and he had been there about ten minutes, and he came up the hill at half past seven and put twenty marks in coin on the bench, in a heap, and did not sit down.

"Second seam," he said. "Six hours of flood after eleven, and I will take the noon whatever it does. My own ring, my own warp, my own account, and I have asked four yards and two of them are not crews any more and one of them is a man in a lighter at the pile."

"Sit down anyway," Nessa Pike said, and did not touch the money.

He sat down. He was about fifty and had the back of a neck that a north-easter had worked on for thirty years, and he said his name was Iven Toft and that he had a boat called the *Mercy Ledge* in the second lay, and Calder Marr wrote all of it down because a man who does not state his own name is a man whose twenty marks may be somebody else's.

"Twenty is the figure I was quoted on Friday morning," Toft said, "by a man on this dock who went up the hill to ask you, and who came back down and said you would not do it, and he was not wrong about the not doing it. I want to know whether I am being quoted a different price for a different day or the same price for the same refusal."

"You are being quoted the same refusal," Calder Marr said, "and I am going to tell you why, and I am going to tell you the second half of it before I tell you the first, because the second half is the part that will make you cross the yard and I would rather you had it in front of you than behind you."

"The first half is that we have no instrument," Toft said. "I have been in this harbor thirty years and I know what a charter is. It ran out on Friday. I did not come up here to be told it."

"The second half," Calder Marr said, "is that the man who came up on Friday wanted a day, and I would have had to start a day this morning and be in the water at eleven with it, and I was not going to open a job on the last afternoon of a charter and let it run into April with a boy of twenty-two on a fall and no paper behind me. That is not a rule. It is a thing I decided about myself in a shed at about two o'clock on Friday afternoon and I have not slept properly since and I am not going to pretend to you it was the law."

"That is better than the law," Toft said, "because the law would have let me have you."

"Read the first column back," Nessa Pike said.

The boy read the position out before the man was given his money back, and he read it the way he read everything, slowly and in the order it was written.

"Five marks in a bag. Twenty-seven on the chandler's note and the three counts of its weeks are all exactly what they were on the thirty-first of March and none of them has moved. Two crowns in a tin that went in on the first of February. Nine weeks of a wage at four marks a week, the first four paid, and the fifth, sixth, seventh, eighth and ninth unpaid, and that is twenty marks in that column and there are no adjectives in it. Forty marks to the same boy for the reading and the hand, standing whole. Forty to a conservator with no office, standing, and not asked for and not to be asked for. Eight marks and a day of a boat to this harbor's bellwright, owing since the twenty-seventh of February, and there is no eight marks in this harbor before October. And eight shillings and tenpence to a woman in her own name, undiminished, unpaid. And a pump that is the only gear in this harbor a man can hire that is not the harbor's, and that has not been out of this shed since the twenty-first of March because a man has not wanted it."

He read it and put the pencil down and did not look at anybody.

Toft looked at the bench for a while.

"You will not take my twenty," he said, "and you have told me why in two sentences and one of them was about yourself, and I have to say that a yard that refuses me for a reason it does not like is worth more than a yard that takes me for one it does."

"That is the whole of what I have got," Calder Marr said, "and it is not enough to eat on."

---

Two men came up that hill in one morning and got two different answers, and the second one is the one this chapter is for.

The second of them came at nine and he was a master of no crew of this association, about sixty, with a bad thumb, and he wanted the mole's toe at the third seam, where his own ring had gone with the flood in February, and he had twenty marks in a leather bag and he had been told in a market that a yard in this harbor would still take a job at the toe.

"No," Calder Marr said.

"You are the yard that sounded the mole's root in January."

"That is a different ground and it was a different office that gave it to us and that office has not given us anything since the twenty-eighth of November," Calder Marr said. "There are two reasons and I will give you both and you will not like either. The first is a notice of the circuit office that closes the mole's toe and inner face from the third seam to the eleventh to all work and to all approach by all persons, and it says in its own words that it includes this office and this yard, and there is no power in this harbor or in Veyr that lifts it. The second is that the work would be on this harbor authority's own ground, and the thing that let this yard stand on this harbor authority's ground expired on Friday, and I have nothing in my coat this morning except a page with a figure on it that I wrote myself."

"Could you not do it as my own work," the man said. "As a man doing a thing for himself, on his own account, with no paper, like the ring on a fisherman's ground at the second seam."

"That is the whole of the trick this yard has," Calder Marr said, "and it is the only reason there is a yard on this dock, and I am not going to argue it up the mole's face at the third seam to a man who has twenty marks. If the notice does not close the second seam and the work is yours, then there is no office involved in it and no paper is required and I will take your money and do your work and you may read my figure afterward and check it. If the work is the harbor's, or on the harbor's ground, then the office is in it whether I have a page or not, and the notice is in it whether I have a page or not, and a piece of paper I write myself does not move a seal."

The man put his bag back on his shoulder and stood there a while, and then he said the thing that went into the book in the first column because any man on that dock could check it by asking him again.

"Then I will say what I think, and you may write it down or not. I think you only work when a man cannot go to the office."

"Write that down," Nessa Pike said, before Calder Marr could.

"Enter it in the first column," the boy said.

"Enter it in the first column with the hour," Nessa Pike said, "and enter under it that the man said it at the head of this dock and not in a room, and that he is a master of no crew of this association and is not named because he has not asked to be named, and Calder, do not argue with it, because it is true, and a man who argues with a true thing on a Tuesday morning has not read his own book."

"Calder has not argued with it," Calder Marr said.

---

Toft went up the hill to find a pump and the second lay had six boats on it and the harbor's own tide gang was off from the first of April, three men short, because the harbor authority's repair account had bought four sets of moorings and a season of warp in January and had been empty since the middle of February. The third lay was full. The *Sea Wren* was at the pile under the *Vigil* with her gear and her pump in a lighter and her master's own logbook in a naval file, and there was a copy of a leaf of this yard's own book in his coat, and none of it had come back, and nobody had come from a naval file to ask the yard for anything at all, which was either good news or the other thing.

The air pot was not a third gone. It was a third gone and a little better than a third gone, because the pot had been bought twice in March, and the crew had spent a fortnight in a shed in the last week of the month with nothing coming in, and the eighth thing on the market wall had been up since the eighteenth of March with a naval paper nailed over one of the sheets it sat beside and a four-inch gap a man could put a finger into.

Alden Marr was at the far bench with his coat on and a piece of work in his hand that he had been holding for an hour, and he was not asked for anything, and Calder Marr did not go over to him.

At about four in the afternoon a man came up the hill who was not on this dock and had never been on it, and he was big and he had a burned ear, and he had a slate under his arm and a coil of line over his shoulder, and he stood in the doorway of that shed and put the slate down on the bench and said his name was Harl Dace and he was master of the *Grey Widow* and he wanted to put a coast down.

"You hired the pump on the twenty-first of March and read the entry back before you paid it," Calder Marr said.

"I did, and I told you why on the mole, and you put it in a book." Dace did not sit down either. "I have a coast about five miles north of this headland, inside the water, inshore of the shoals, that I have been working for thirty-one years. It has three marks on it and a bottom and a way in and out of weather, and I have never once been asked for any of it, by an office or by a man, in thirty-one years, and I am forty-eight years old and I am not going to be asked when I am sixty."

"What do you want with this yard."

"I want your slate," Dace said, "and I want the book, and I want the two columns you keep, and I want it written the way you write it and not the way I would write it, and I am not asking you to believe a word of me." He put his thumb on the slate. "And I am not asking for money. I have a boat of my own and I know what a day of it costs and I am not a charity. What I am asking is that if I am wrong then it is written that I was wrong, in your book, with your hand on it, and if I am right then it is written that I was right, in the same book, in the same hand, and I do not care a farthing which."

Nessa Pike put her hand flat on the bench.

"He is asking for the yard to be a witness to a man who is not a party and has not hired us and is not of this association," she said. "I have to say that out loud, because that is the thing this shed does and I will not have it done quietly."

"He can be a witness to a thing he has seen himself," Calder Marr said. "That is what a witness is. It is not a party and it is not a crew and it is not a man in the wage column, and if you write it in the second column with a reason against it, then in four years a man can come here and read that a man of the *Grey Widow* said a thing and was not paid for saying it and was not of this yard."

"Then ask him what he has, and ask him for the order of it, and I will write down that a man of the *Grey Widow* set out a coast in this shed on the fourth of April in the afternoon on his own account and was not paid and was not of this association and has asked for his figures to be in our book in two columns with a reason against the second, and you will read it back and he will read it after you, and Calder will read it after him, and that is three and there is no fourth man in this shed today."

Bevin Rook read it back. Harl Dace read it after the boy, slowly, the way a man reads a document he intends to be held to, and then he said one thing into the book at the end and made Calder read it back a fourth time.

"Enter that I have asked to be in this yard's book and that I have been told the second column is for what a man on a mole cannot check," he said, "and that a coast is not a mole, and that I do not know yet which column any of it is in, and that I would rather it went in the second one and that somebody had to write a reason against me."

"That is the first thing anybody has ever asked me for that I have to think about," Nessa Pike said, and the pencil went on top of the book.
