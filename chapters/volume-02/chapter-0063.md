# Chapter 63

*The Twelfth Seam*

The top of the flood on Thursday the twenty-sixth of January was a quarter to ten and the *Sea Wren* lay off the mole's face at the twelfth seam from about seven, because Noll Tarner had wanted an hour in which nothing was being asked of him, and Calder Marr had let him have it, and the two of them had spent that hour not talking about the work.

The reason had gone into the harbor authority's book at eight o'clock in Calder Marr's own left hand with the clerk standing there, and the clerk had read it, and the reeve had read it, and the reeve had read out loud one sentence about a line of his own that he was not writing underneath for the first time in four months, and the sentence was in the book because a clerk writes hours.

There was no diver's limit on the slate for the *Sea Wren's* day, because a limit is the harbor authority's instrument and not a herring skipper's, and so Tarner had set his own, out loud, at the boat, and it was twenty minutes, and Bevin Rook had written *twenty, set by the diver, at the boat, at seven minutes past eight* in his own right hand, which is the first time in this yard's book that a limit has not been set by anybody with a coat on.

Nessa Pike had the cleat. Calder Marr had the line. Bevin Rook had the book and the slate and the pencil tied to the slate on a string, and the reason for the string is that a boy with a hand that will not close cannot be trusted to a loose pencil, and he had the slate on his knee and the line over his shoulder and the whole of the job of being a bottom man available to him and no use whatever.

---

The warp of a herring boat called the *Anna Beth* had come off its ring somewhere in October and had gone down under the mole's foot at the twelfth seam, and the ring had followed the warp, and the shackle had followed the ring, and the three of them had been under about two feet of the mole's own rubble for eleven weeks, and a herring skipper called Edric Pringle had wanted them out since the first of December and had not paid Tarner for the work of wanting them out.

"She is my mother's cousin and she is a good woman and she is being difficult about nine marks," Tarner said, at the boat, with his goggles up, "and I am going to be difficult back, and neither of us is going to lose anything but a temper over it, and in eleven years of this neither of us has lost anything but a temper, and it is how we both eat."

The water at the top of the flood was a foot and a half over the rubble at the twelfth seam and the swell coming round the mole head was a northeast one, four inches and running, and it came under the boat's quarter in a way that made the *Sea Wren* lift and set every fourth time, and the thing about a fouled ring under a mole is that a fouled ring does not care what the water is doing to the boat above it.

Tarner went over at twenty past nine with the line on a shackle, and he was in for twenty minutes and came up at nineteen, and he was not in a hurry and was not hurt and had not been in a hurry and had not been hurt.

"Say it," Nessa Pike said, "in the words you used, and Calder will write them and Rook will read them."

Tarner hung on the fall with his goggles up and water running off him into the boat.

"Ring is not there," he said. "Ring's gone. What is there is the shackle and about four foot of warp still bent round it, and the warp is round the shackle and the shackle is under the rubble, and there is a stone over the shackle the size of a bread loaf and it is not bedded, it is sitting, it came off the face in the autumn and landed on top of it. And the warp is not snagged. It is not under anything. It is round the shackle and it is free at the other end, so the whole thing can come out at once if somebody will take the weight of four foot of tarred marline off it while I turn it."

"Can somebody take the weight of it," Calder Marr said, "with a hand that will not close."

"There is a heaving line on it and I will put a bight through the bight and take it up on the ring bolt and then the whole thing comes to me at once. It is a boy's work and it is a thirty-second job."

Everybody in the boat looked at Bevin Rook.

"No," Bevin Rook said.

"It is a heaving line."

"It is a heaving line and I have a hand that will not close and I would have it in the water holding four foot of tarred marline in a northeast," the boy said. "And if I hold it and it goes, it goes into my left, and I have put that arm into a fall already on the twelfth of January and there is a bonesetter in this town who has changed her word once since October and will not promise me June, and I am not going into the water on Thursday the twenty-sixth of January to find out."

"Accepted," Nessa Pike said, in the voice she used when something had been settled and was being written down. "Enter it. Enter that he asked the boy and the boy said no in front of five people and the reason he gave was his own hand and not a rule of this yard, and enter that I entered the rule nowhere, because there is not one and we are not making one at ten past ten in the morning on a Thursday."

"I will take it," Calder Marr said. "With the right hand, on the ring bolt, and Rook reads the depth and the hour, and you call the shutoff and I am not the diver and I do not get a vote."

"That is the arrangement and it is in the sheet already."

---

The stone came off in about a minute and a half, and it did not go anywhere, because Calder Marr had a hand under it and put it on the boat's bottom boards instead of letting it go, and Tarner came up after it with the shackle and the four feet of warp in both hands and the whole of it came out of the water in one piece, and there was nothing about it that looked like glass, or like a vessel, or like anything that would be put in a ledger and read in Veyr. It was a shackle and a ring and a warp and a stone, and it was eleven weeks in the mud, and it went down on the boards of the *Sea Wren* with a sound like a dropped anchor.

The warp was cut where the bight had started to seize. Tarner cut it himself.

"Twenty-one minutes by my own count and I want it in that column, not the other one," he said.

"The first column is what I can check off the boat," Bevin Rook said, and he had the slate on his knee and had not written anything since the stone came off. "Nineteen came up, and I counted it, and it is in the first column. Twenty-one is in the second column with a reason against it, and the reason is that there is no clock where the diver is and no man on a mole, and I am the bottom man and I was on a boat, and I can check nineteen and I cannot check twenty-one, and both of them are in the book."

"You are a hard young man to work for."

"I am a boy with a bad hand writing down what happened, and it is not the same as being a man who is not there."

Nessa Pike had the shutoff in her hand the entire time and had not used it and said afterwards, when Calder Marr asked her whether she had wanted to, that she had wanted to at eighteen minutes and had not, and that wanting to and doing it are two different jobs and she had been doing the second one for four months.

---

The tide gang stood at the twelfth, which is not in the closure of the first clause because the clause stops at the eleventh, and there is a seam marker cut into the mole's crown at the eleventh with a ring of paint round it that has not been repainted since the thirtieth of October, and Neale had put three men there because Neale puts men where the law is and not where the work is, and one of the three was Rowlandike and one was Petch and the third was a man called Wil Slee, whose brother Tam had gone down out of the *Grey Widow* on the twelfth of January and come up whole.

They wrote down what they saw on a slate of their own and gave it to Calder Marr at the mole steps at two o'clock, and it was Calder Marr who read it out in the shed, because a thing a man has written about you in your own yard is worth reading where four people can hear it.

*Herring boat's ring, shackle and warp, fouled, under rubble, mole's face at the twelfth seam, brought up whole. Two men and a boy on the work. No diver out of this gang. Moorings and fenders on the face at the twelfth in order. Nothing in the water that was glass.*

"Enter it as theirs and not as ours," Nessa Pike said. "Enter the last line twice, and enter that they wrote it and we did not, and if a man reads that book in four years he is going to want to know whether this yard put a word in a tide gang's mouth, and he will be able to see that it did not."

---

The money came on the Friday and Bevin Rook read it out and read it back twice, and the yard's book has the column and the check.

"Gross on the board, twenty-six, from Noll Tarner out of the *Sea Wren's* account, for one day, at the rate he stated out loud in this shed on the twenty-third of January before he could be cheated, and not one mark of it is a raise and nothing is to be raised.

"The harbor's eighth, three. The harbor's share, three. That is six off the top of the day and not off a raising, because the eighth and the share follow this yard in whatever it does and were not argued in a hearing room in November and are not going to be argued on a wet mole in January. Air, two marks, and the pot is a third gone and two marks has not put a mark back into it. Rope, a mark, and we used the yard's own for the lead and hired eight fathoms of the net loft's for the heaving line and the hire is a mark. The *Sea Wren's* pump, a mark, for one day, and it is a hire, and it goes in as a hire and not as a kindness, because Noll Tarner said a pump is a thing that wears and a man who lends his for nothing has a pump that is everybody's, and that is the correct sentence and it is in the book in his words.

"That is sixteen out of twenty-six and it is the yard's, and the yard has four marks in a bag, and four and sixteen is twenty, and the tin has been empty since the eighteenth of November, and the note stands at thirteen, and on Tuesday the thirty-first a fortnight of bread, oil and candles goes onto that note in the creditor's own hand and comes out of this bag, and I am telling the man who reads this that on the thirty-first the figure in this book will be twelve and the figure on the note will be eight, and both of those are the same five marks and not two."

Nobody argued with it, and the boy read it back a third time because it had two figures in it and two figures is where mistakes live.

Then Calder Marr took a slate and wrote four lines on it and gave it to Noll Tarner, and Tarner read it standing in the doorway of the shed with his coat off.

*Copied, this twenty-seventh of January, from the Free Keel yard's book, in the hand of the man who keeps it: the crew at the twelfth seam on the twenty-sixth, the claim being the Sea Wren's and the work on her own window; the depth, the hour, the length of ground, what was raised; the diver's own figure in the second column with the reason it is in the second column; the tide gang's own slate, in their words, including the words that nothing in the water was glass. The name of the yard at the top of it, and the name of the man who keeps the book, and the date. One sheet. To be kept and not returned, at the holder's asking, in the same terms as a copy on a market wall.*

"You are giving me the page," Tarner said.

"I am copying the page," Calder Marr said, "and the copy has your claim on it and not mine, and the man who keeps the book has written it out in his own right hand and will know in three weeks that he did, and that is the arrangement. A man who hands over the page loses the page. A man who is copied keeps the page and the copy is out of this shed, and if a man in Veyr ever reads the day's work of the *Sea Wren* out loud in a room, he will be reading a thing that is a copy, and there will be a sheet in this yard that says so, and the sheet is on the wall tonight in Calder Marr's own hand."

"And what is it for?"

"It is for the day a man in a boat needs somebody to have written down that he was there," Bevin Rook said from the bench, without looking up. "That is all it is for. I do not know what else it is for, and I have not got a better answer than the one you got in a shed on the fourth of January, and the man who asked me that in a net loft is a better man to ask than I am, and I would like somebody to find him and tell him I said so."

Tarner folded the sheet into his coat.

"There is a thing I will say and then I will go and take my crew out on the way to the twelfth for the rest of this week," he said. "You have written your own bad figures on a wall in this market. I have been in this harbor four days and I have asked six people and every one of them told me about the wall before they told me anything else, and not one of them told me it was a thing to be proud of. They told me it was a thing that was on the wall. In the Free Keel a man does not put his own mistakes up where the fleet can read them, and in the Free Keel a man does not lend his book to a rival crew either, and I have had two arguments with my own crew about my own mistakes this month which did not happen in a shed in front of a boy with a book." He put his hand on the door frame. "I do not know that either of those things is right. I have a great many people telling me for four years that it is, and I have a new boy in a borrowed shed telling me it is not, and I have a pump in my boat, so I will not argue with a man who owns nothing."

"I own a wall," Calder Marr said.

"Aye. And the notice of the thirty-first of October is nailed to it four inches above a thing you wrote yourself, and I have read both, and I am telling you as a man who has a claim window on Thursday and not a round of applause on Sunday: if a man in Veyr ever comes up that road and reads your wall, the first thing he will learn about this harbor is that a yard put its own errors on a public sheet and asked a fleet to check them. And I have never in my life seen a fleet check anything, and I do not think they are going to start with us."

Then he was gone down the dock, and the two and a half went with him, and the yard stood in the shed with the book open and the money column on the page and twenty marks in a bag, and nobody said anything for about a minute, and then the boy said the thing he had been composing since Saturday.

"Entered, the twenty-seventh of January, in my own right hand, at nobody's asking, because I have been asked for the words and I have given them twice today and both times to a man who has a different view of the world from mine and neither of us is a villain, which is worse and I would rather it was not: that the Free Keel yard's book is now the book of another crew's work, that this is the first time in five months that anything this yard has written down has belonged to somebody else, that I do not know whether that is a good thing, and that I have written in this book a thing nobody in this yard asked me to write about and I would like it noticed that it is the second time in ten days and that both of them were about the same subject, which is a record being out of our hands, and that I do not intend to write a third one until somebody in this yard has asked me a question about it."
