# Chapter 54

*What a Boy Is Owed in June*

The message came down on Saturday night by a fish porter and was in the boy's hand, which meant the boy had walked to the top of the fish market and back to write four lines, and it said that she would like to speak to Calder Marr about the fourth week, on Monday, in her front room, at eleven, and that she would not come to the shed because she had been in a shed in a coat in November and had not been warm the whole time.

Bevin Rook read the message twice in the yard on the Sunday and put it on the bench and did not say anything about it for about an hour, and then he said, "She is asking to be told. She said *to be told*."

"You will be there," Calder said.

"I will be there, and I will be under the table, because I am the only one of the three of us who can read the book and I am not going to have my own wages read out of it by a man at a table in front of my mother with the door shut."

"You will sit at the table where she can see you," Nessa Pike said, "and you will put your hand on the page when the figure is read, because a boy who is under a table when his figure is read is a boy who has been told about himself, and a boy who has his hand on the page has been present for himself. And you are going to do that without being asked, because I am asking you now and you will not enjoy it and that is not the reason."

---

The front room of the cottage behind the fish market was eleven feet by nine and had a table in it with a lamp, a chair on each side, and a third against the wall for the boy, and the stall was outside the door and could be heard from the table, and a woman shouting a price outside the door in a hard wind is a thing that gets into a conversation in a way a person does not.

She was fifty-one and her name was not in the book because his name was not in the book, and she had a cloth over her apron pocket and her hand went to it four times in the first ten minutes and did not take anything out of it.

She asked the first question while Calder Marr was still standing.

"Tell me what the four is, and tell me the date it starts, and do not tell me it is being worked on."

"It is four marks a week from the tenth of December last year, and it is not being worked on, and it is not being paid."

"That is what I thought." She did not raise her voice. "The woman in the yard told me in November that a word was going to be taken out of the book and a word put in its place, and I did not know what the words were and I did not ask, because I have found that a question asked in a shed in November is a question that gets answered in a hearing room in a year. I would like to know the two words."

"*Claim* is in it," the boy said. "*Wages* is out of it, and Nessa Pike took it out in her own hand on the fourteenth of December because I asked her to and not because anybody in that yard told her to."

His mother looked at him.

"You asked her to."

"I asked her to."

"Then that is a thing you did and I would like to sit with it for a minute," she said, "and I am not going to ask you why, because a boy of twenty-two who has a hand that will close in June does not owe anybody an account of his own wages."

The second question was the money, and she made him read it out. He read it out. Sixteen marks for the reading of the book from the fourteenth of October, twenty-four for the hand from the twenty-ninth, forty in all, none of it paid, the whole of it standing and not reduced and not asked to be reduced, and she made him read the part about the forty marks twice and made Calder Marr read the part about the yard's own claim back to her after it.

"You have a claim on it," she said. "A claim is a thing you hold. A claim is a piece of paper. Nobody in the world can take a piece of paper off a man who has it, and nobody in the world can spend it."

"That is the second sentence," Calder Marr said. "That is the one I said in a hearing room in November and it is the one I am saying now, and I am not going to say the first one."

"What is the first one."

"That we are extremely sorry," Calder Marr said, "and the first one is worth nothing in this room and I have never once been able to stop myself saying it, and I said it in a hearing room in front of two hundred people, and your son was in the yard at the time and heard me say it, and I have not got a better sentence and I am not going to look for one in your front room."

She took the surgeon's writing out of her apron then, and held it up, and did not read it.

"That has been in this apron since the thirtieth of October," she said. "It has the hour on it and the cause on it and the man who was holding the cleat on it, and the man who was holding the cleat has not been to this house since the day before and I have not asked him and I am not going to ask him, and there is a great deal of that boy's life that I have decided to leave alone this winter because I have a hand of my own that has been on the two of us since October."

She put the writing back in the apron.

The third question came at about twenty past eleven and it was not about money.

"The fifth seam," she said. "My boy is in the book for a plate in nine feet of water on the twelfth of December and a boy of his age was in the water on the twentieth, and a man came into this room in a coat on the last day of October and told me there was a reading he was owed in this house. I have not asked about the plate because it is glass and I do not understand glass and I have decided that a woman who does not understand a thing is not obliged to have an opinion about it. But I want to know one thing about the fifth seam, and it is not whether it is dangerous."

"Which thing is it you want," his mother said.

"Whether it was worth him." She said it without any weight on it at all, which was the worst way. "That is all. I am not asking what is in it. I am asking whether it was worth what it has cost this house, because I have had four months of not knowing and there is a plate in a mole that nobody is allowed to touch, and if the answer is that it was worth it, then I can put it somewhere in me and get on with my winter."

Nobody in the room said anything for about a minute and a half, and outside the door a woman shouted a price and then shouted it again lower.

"It was worth the hand going in," Calder Marr said at last, "and the hand going in was not the plate's fault. The plate is a thing we have not been allowed to measure and the reason we have not been allowed to measure it is a piece of paper about a notice, and the hand went in on a chain that came tight off this harbor's own foundation stone on the twenty-ninth of October, and the chain is not the plate and the stone is not the plate and no office in this harbor or in Veyr has said that the plate sent it. What I can tell you is that the work has been done, that the record of it is on a wall in the fish market where anybody can read it, and that there is one thing in it in eleven feet of water that nobody has touched and that two plans have been written for and both of them have been refused, correctly, by an office that would not sign its own paper."

"And the second plan."

"Was lodged in November and came back the same afternoon on four grounds and every one of the four was correct," Calder said, "and the first of the four was that the office will not countersign an instrument putting a person inside the closure its own notice created, and the yard has a plate in that closure and cannot get to it, and the mechanism for ever getting to it is not a better argument from my shed. It is a change of politics in an office. I cannot make that happen and neither can your son, and I would rather tell you that in January than let you find it out in a year."

"And in the meantime the fleet has one berth short on the first of March," she said, "and I know that, because the woman who holds the cleat said it in this room in November when she came about the sling, and I have not forgotten it and I have not thought about it since, because I have a stall."

"You should think about it," Nessa Pike said. "You should think about it before the end of the month and you should say it in the fish market where the men who moor the boats can hear you, and I will come with you if you want me to, and I am not asking you to want me to."

---

Then the boy said the thing.

He had been sitting with his right hand flat on the table the whole time and he had not said a word since the writing went back in the apron, and he said it now in the flat voice, in the middle of a room, out loud, looking at his mother and not at Calder Marr.

"A claim is a thing you can hold and a wage is a thing you can spend," he said. "That is the difference and I have had four months to work it out and it is the only thing I have worked out. The forty is a piece of paper and it will be a piece of paper in ten years and it will not buy bread and it cannot be divided and I cannot hand it to anybody. And a wage is four a week and it is nothing at all against forty marks and it is the only number in this room that can be spent in a shop on a Saturday."

"Go on," his mother said.

"So I am going to ask for a wage." He moved his right hand off the table and put it back. "Not from the forty. The forty stands and it is not reduced and I do not want it touched. A wage, at four a week, from the first of February, entered in that book as a hand, with the date on it and my name on the line in my own hand and Nessa Pike's under it, and it does not start until the yard raises something and it is not paid on a Friday if there is nothing, and it stops if the yard stops."

"That is a worse bargain than you could have asked for," his mother said. "You could have asked for the whole forty by the first of March."

"I could have asked for the whole forty by the first of March and it would have been a boy's paper that said *by the first of March* on it," the boy said, "and a paper with a date on it is a paper somebody has to be told about when the date comes, and then it is a thing I have been told no to on a Friday, and a man who has been told no on a Friday is a man who asks for something else next Friday, and in two years he is a man nobody hires. I would rather have a wage that has a start and no money behind it than forty marks with a date on it, and I would rather have that at four a week from the first of February, because the first of February is a day this yard can name, and I can tell the *Anna Ray's* master on a quay that the yard is paying a hand four a week from the first of February, and a man who is owed forty marks is a man you send away without a conversation, and a man who is owed nothing until the first of February is a man you send away by accident."

"You are telling your mother you would rather have less," his mother said, "on a Monday, in front of a man who owes you forty."

"I am telling my mother I would rather have something that is a fact on a Monday than something that is a fact in April," the boy said, "and I have been owed forty since the twenty-ninth of October and I have not asked for it once, and I am not going to start now, and the first of February is a Wednesday and I have looked."

He was right about the weekday, which nobody had told him. The first of February in this harbor is a Wednesday, because the first of March is a Wednesday and a February is four weeks and four weeks takes nothing off a week at all, and he had it a second way off the Mondays as well, because the second, the ninth, the sixteenth, the twenty-third and the thirtieth of January are Mondays and the sixth of February is the one after them. The boy had done it himself and had not told anybody, and Calder Marr understood, about four seconds later, why, and did not say it out loud, because the boy had found it out alone and a man who explains a boy's arithmetic to him in his own mother's front room is a man who has decided the boy cannot do it.

"That is a very bad bargain," his mother said, "and I am going to agree to it, and I am going to say why, and it is not because it is fair. It is because you have said the date out loud in front of me, which means that on the first of February there is a person in this house who knows what is supposed to happen, and that is the only protection anybody in this story has ever had, and I have not had it for four months and I have slept on it."

She stood up then, which was the end of it, and put her hand flat on her son's head for about a second, and did not make anything of it.

"You will not work the mole on the twelfth of January," she said. "I have not asked and I am telling you, and the date is the twelfth because I know the slate."

"Mother—"

"You will not work the mole on the twelfth of January," she said, "and on the eleventh of February I will ask you again, and you may tell me no, and I will take the no."

---

They did the entry on the Tuesday the tenth, in the shed, in the cold, with the stove going, and Nessa Pike read the entry out of her own slate before anybody wrote it, because that is the rule, and then the boy wrote it with his right hand and she countersigned it underneath, and Calder Marr put his own initial at the end of the line because the book is a book four men can read and the fourth man is him and has not written anything else in it since the first of December.

*Entered, the tenth of January, read out on the tenth, in the yard. Bevin Rook, of this yard, to be entered as a hand at four marks the week from the first day of February next, in addition to and without reduction of the claim of sixteen marks for the reading of this book from the fourteenth of October and the claim of twenty-four marks for the hand from the twenty-ninth day of October, which stand whole and are not reduced. The wage begins on the day named and is not paid before the yard has raised money to pay it, and does not stop because the yard is slow, and is a wage and not a claim, and is entered at the boy's own asking and not at any other person's, and the boy asked for a worse bargain than he could have asked for and said so out loud in his mother's front room and it is entered here because he asked for it to be entered here.*

Bevin Rook read it back twice and then said the thing that nobody had asked for, which is what he is for.

"I want the first of February written in the wall copy as well," he said, "and I want it written in the same hand on both, and I want the number of the day put against it, and I do not want the word *wages* anywhere near it because the word came out of this book on the fourteenth of December and it is not going back in tonight for a boy to be kind to."

"That is the correct answer," Nessa Pike said, "and it is the third time in four months that a person in this yard has said a thing to this boy that costs the boy something and is right, and the other two are mine, and I am not going to be thanked for any of the three, and if either of you thanks me I will take the book out of your hands."

Nobody thanked her. Alden Marr was at the far bench with his back to the room and had not spoken to anybody since the thirteenth of November, and nobody asked him anything, and Calder Marr did not go, and the book went on the bench, and the pencil went on top of it.
