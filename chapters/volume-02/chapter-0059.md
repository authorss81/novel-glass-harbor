# Chapter 59

*The Day Before*

Amos Pryde, who was a coaster's master of about fifty and had a thumb short of its top joint and had been hiring hands out of the fish market for nineteen years, was standing at his own stall on the market on Wednesday the eighteenth of January at ten in the morning with a mug of something in his hand, and he had come and stood in front of the six things on the wall and read the whole of the bottom one, which is the fifth-seam sheet, and then he read the third one from the top, which is the public record, and he read that one twice.

Then he came up the fish dock to the shed and asked Calder Marr for a copy to keep.

"I am not a Free Keel man," he said, before he was asked anything. "I have never joined anything in my life and I am not going to start in January. I am a coaster and I hire two or three hands a month and I have got a boy on my starboard side this month who cannot read a manifest and will not say so to a man in a coat, and I want a copy of that sheet to keep, and I want to be able to put it in his hand and say read it, and if he gives it back in a week then I know he read it, and if he does not give it back then I know something about him and about the sheet both, and that is worth a great deal more to me than the sheet is."

"The copies are counted," Bevin Rook said, from the bench.

"I know they are counted. I have read the count. Ten printed crew copies, two on that wall and three on other crews' walls and five rolled in oilcloth in your chest, and a naval man wrote the number on a slate of his own and did not ask for one, and that is in the fourth part under a line with six items on it and I have read that too." He put the mug down on the bench. "I am asking to become the eleven."

Nobody in the shed said anything for about half a minute.

"That is the correct request and it is the first one anybody has made in four months," Nessa Pike said, "and it is going to cost us a number, and the number is the one thing on that wall that a vessel holds, and I am not going to decide it in a shed in front of a man with a mug. Calder."

"Give him one," Calder Marr said.

"It is the charter's bond chest," Bevin Rook said. "Five of them are in there against the charter and the harbor's clerk counted them on the twenty-third of November and put the count in the record, and if one of them goes out of the chest then the count in a municipal record is wrong by one on a Tuesday in January and I am the one who signed it out."

"Then you will sign the count out and the count in, today, in the book, in your own right hand, and both of the figures, and the wall copy, and the date and the place it has gone, which is a stall on this dock run by a man called Amos Pryde who is not a Free Keel man and is owed nothing by us and has asked for nothing and has been given something." Calder turned round. "And the eleven goes in the book and on the wall tonight, and the number the vessel has is ten, and it is the right number on the day he took it and it will be the wrong number by the end of the week, and I would rather it be wrong by one in a book with a date on it than be right about a wall that has changed underneath a man who counted it. That is the whole argument and I have been learning it for four months and a man with a mug has just taught me the end of it."

The boy wrote it out. It is in the yard's book in two hands, and the ten printed crew copies became eleven on the eighteenth of January, and the record of the twenty-third of November is not altered and now has a second line under it in the same ink and a different pen, and the six lines of the count are seven, and the seventh reads: *one copy issued on the eighteenth of January to Amos Pryde, master, of no crew of this association, at his own asking, to be kept by him and not returned, and this is entered because a man asked for it in front of three people and was given it, and the number of the copies is therefore eleven and was ten and both numbers are true of their own dates.*

And the man with the mug read that line twice, and put his own hand flat on the page after it, and did not ask to be thanked and was not thanked.

---

They read the plan for the morrow out in the shed in the afternoon, to four men who could reach it, which is the whole of the crew and one more, because Tobias Wren had the boat on the Tuesday and was there on the Wednesday because there was a reason to be.

It was a hand lead and a slate and a bell and a sheet, and the ground was the mole's toe at the third and fourth seams and the bedded seam in the mole's own foundation, and the top of the flood, and the water inside the harbor line, and the permit was a claim window of the seventeenth naming one date and a look only, and there was no diver in it and there was not going to be a diver in it, and the reason that was in the reeve's book would say so in the first sentence.

"No diver," Nessa Pike said. "You have said it four times."

"I am going to say it in a piece of writing that a clerk copies out, and then I am going to say it in my own hand at eight o'clock in the morning in front of that clerk, and then a man who has never met me is going to read it in a ledger in about four years and he is going to be able to say that the Free Keel yard put a hand lead in the water off this mole on a Thursday in January and did not put anybody in it, and that is worth having, and it is worth more than the work, and I want the reason to be strong and short."

---

He wrote it in the shed from two o'clock until nearly five, and it is in the crew's book in his own left hand, and it is two hundred and eleven words, and Bevin Rook read it out twice and then read it a third time slowly while Calder Marr wrote out the figures and the reason in two hands, and it says this, and no more than this.

*The party proposes on the nineteenth instant, at the top of the flood, to take figures with a hand lead and a slate from a boat, on the mole's toe at the third and fourth seams and along the bedded seam in this office's own foundation running north, of which the south end is the position used by the party since the twenty-eighth of October, in the water inside the harbor line, in company with the core hung in eight and a half feet off the south end, and to enter those figures with the hour, the depth, the bearing and the state of the water in this office's own book and in the party's own book, four men to read the same day it is written.*

*No diver is proposed and none will be in the water, and if a diver is proposed on the day the reason will be re-entered before he goes in, and the reason will name him.*

*This office's notice of the thirty-first is not altered, re-served, narrowed or argued by this paper, and the third clause of it is not reached, and the party does not ask for it to be reached, and the party is on the water in a boat and not on the authority's work.*

*And the party states that the run of the bedded material does not stop at the mole, that its own record of it since the twenty-eighth of October is of the south end only, and that it does not know where the run goes and is not asking this office to know on the nineteenth.*

"Two hundred and eleven words," Bevin Rook said, "and it is the best thing you have written since the purpose of the twenty-first, and I am not going to tell you that in a market."

"Read the last paragraph back."

The boy read the last paragraph back, and then read it again, and then stopped reading and looked up, which he had not done in four months of reading things back to this man.

"That is the sentence," he said.

"It is a sentence."

"That is *the* sentence, and you put it in the fourth paragraph, and you put it in there in nine words, and it is the only sentence in two hundred and eleven words that is not an instruction." He put his finger on it. *The party states that the run of the bedded material does not stop at the mole, and that it does not know where the run goes, and is not asking this office to know on the nineteenth.*

"The reeve reads that and he has to do something with it," Bevin said, "and what he can do with it is put it in a ledger, and a ledger is a room he cannot argue in, and then the sentence is in an office in Veyr in about four years, and there will be a man in a room in Veyr who has found a thing under a mole's own foundation that runs out from under a town and a harbor authority with a chart that has never had it on it. And that man will not be able to do one single thing about it, and he will not be able to do one single thing about it because of that sentence, and it is in a book because you wrote it down."

"Then it is a very good sentence," Nessa Pike said, "and it is the last good sentence in this shed before the morrow, and I am going to say the thing nobody wants said and then I am going to go home. What it does not say is why the yard wants to follow it. Read it. Read the whole of it. It names a lead, a slate, a sheet, a bell, a date, a tide, a ground and a depth, and it names four men and a book, and it says *the run does not stop at the mole* and it does not say one word about why a man who has a charter that runs to the last of March and a bell that needs a rope once a year is spending a day of a hired boat on a bedded seam in a mole's foundation."

"It does not say it because I cannot write it," Calder Marr said.

"You cannot write it because of the eighth of December," Nessa Pike said, "and the sixth of January, and the twenty-sixth of December, and I have not asked you about any of those three days tonight and I am not going to, and neither is Rook, and neither is Wren, and it is a fact about this shed that four men have agreed in a month not to ask a question, and the fact is going in the book in the morning as an omission, with a date on it, and the boy is going to enter it in his own right hand because it is the first thing he has entered this month that nobody asked him for, and then we are all going to bed, because it is the morrow and the top of the flood is at a quarter to ten and the pump is not ours."

---

Tobias Wren said one thing on his way out, from the door, to nobody in particular, which is what he does.

"In 1961 there was a man in this yard whose hand went under a chain," he said, "and I have never told anybody that, and I told a woman with a lamp in November, and I have not been able to work out in three months why I did that, and I have not asked you, and I am not going to. But I am going to say this and go. A day with a lead and a slate and a bell is a day that costs eight marks and a day of a boat and it finds out a number, and a number is a number, and a man who has spent four months waiting for a different kind of answer is going to be disappointed in the morning the same as he would be in a month, and disappointed in the morning is cheaper than disappointed in a boat with a southerly on it."

They read the reason one more time. Bevin Rook read it back twice and read it back a third time slowly, because the rule is the rule, and then he entered it whole in the crew's book with the hour against it and read that back too, and then he entered the omission, which is this, in his own right hand, on the eighteenth of January.

*Entered, the eighteenth of January, in my own right hand and at nobody's asking. The reason for the nineteenth contains no statement of the purpose for which the party wants the ground, and I have read the reason four times, and the reason is everything it ought to be except that, and the excepting has been gone at since the sixth of January and nobody in this yard has asked me about it and I have not asked, and I am entering it because a reason with a hole in it that is not written down is a hole, and a man who finds the hole in six months can write anything he likes in it, and I would rather the hole be in this book in my hand on the eighteenth of January than be a thing somebody finds with a reason for it.*

Nobody argued with him about it. Nessa Pike read it and said it was correct, and Calder Marr signed under it, and Tobias Wren read it standing at the bench with his cap in his hands and said one word, which was "Aye," and went out into the dark to get his boat right for a morning.

The market wall was on the west side of the fish dock in the cold with the seven things on it and a crowd of four, and the naval tender lay at a cable's length off the mole with her floats in and out and her oars shipped, and the second cutter's boat was still at the naval pile with her oars up, and the first of March was six weeks off to the day and it was a Wednesday, and there were forty herring boats coming into a harbor with one berth short, and the mole's toe was a hundred yards away in the dark with forty feet of bedded seam under it running north under a town, and the town was built on it, and the harbor authority had no chart of what was under the town, and the yard had a claim and a wage that started on a Tuesday and four marks in a bag, and on the morrow there was a lead and a slate and a bell and no diver, and Calder Marr went up the steps to write the hour down, because that was the job, and the boy put the book in front of him without being asked.
