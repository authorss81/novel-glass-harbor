# Chapter 87

*Instrument or Weapon*

The tenth week of a wage at four marks a week fell due on Tuesday the eleventh of April and the boy entered it in the column with no adjectives in it, and the column was ten lines deep, and on the same afternoon the boy's own arithmetic and the creditor's page did the same thing at the same time, which is the first time in this winter that those two have been in step about anything.

Three marks of bread, oil and candles went onto the note at the sign of the Drowned Bell, in the creditor's own hand, and came out of the bag the same afternoon, and air went out of the same bag on the same afternoon, five marks in all, and the bag was five, and the note was thirty, and the tin had two crowns in it that had been in it since the first of February and had not been touched.

"Read the note back," Nessa Pike said.

"Thirty," the boy said, "and the three counts of the weeks are twenty-two in the creditor's book, thirteen in Calder Marr's head, and eleven as the last figure ever written in that page, and none of them has moved since the fourth of January and none of them is going to be moved tonight by anything I do with a pencil. And the note was twenty-seven on the thirty-first of March, and it has gone up three because the yard bought bread with it, and it is the biggest it has been since the eighth of March and there is nothing in the world to be glad about in that and I am entering it because it is a figure."

"You are a joy," Nessa Pike said, and put the pencil on top of the book.

---

The argument was in the shed at about two in the afternoon and it lasted until about five and it was the longest argument that shed has had since the twelfth of March, and it began because Harl Dace came up the hill at one o'clock to ask for his glass back and Calder Marr said no.

"Give it to me for a fortnight," Calder Marr said. "Fourteen days. Not as ours. As a thing the yard holds on your behalf and hands back to you, with a line in the book, and I will not sell it and I will not show it to anybody but a man of this crew, and if anybody from an office asks for it I will write *it is not ours and it is not here* in whatever words they use, and that is a thing a yard with a wall can do."

"Can you do that," Dace said. "Say it again slowly and tell me which of the three things you have just promised is inside the harbor line and which is outside it."

"None of the three is inside anything. A piece of glass in a shed is not a wreck and it is not a site and it is not water."

"It is in this harbor," Dace said, "and this harbor is where the circuit office of this coast is, and the office that has wanted your record since December is the same office that writes the words *route use* on a piece of glass, and the first thing it will do with a piece of glass that has its own words on it is ask whose it is. And when it asks whose it is, it is not asking you. It is asking you in a way that a man says no to in a room, and the no goes in a file, and the file gets copied, and I have watched three men in this harbor say no in a room and I could not tell you one of them who still has a boat."

"There are three documents in this harbor and every one of them is a form that is competently written," Nessa Pike said, and then stopped, because the sentence had arrived at its own edge and she went and stood at the bench with her back to the room for about a minute.

"Go on," Dace said. "That is the argument, isn't it. You have got a competent false thing on a piece of glass and you cannot tell me what an office would do with it, and neither can I, and neither can the man who put it there."

"Yes," Nessa Pike said. "That is the argument. And I will say the rest of it in Calder's own mouth because he is going to say it and he has not noticed that he is going to say it, and then he can put it in the book himself and have it read back four times. You are about to say that a yard with nothing in its hand has nothing to protect itself with, and that a claim to a real object is the first thing any yard on this dock has been able to say in five months, and that a piece of glass with a Registry seal on it is the only document in this harbor that a man in Veyr cannot simply overrule with a form, and that you are going to hold it for a fortnight."

"I was going to say that," Calder Marr said, "and I am not going to, because of one sentence."

"Say the sentence and not the reason."

"Because you brought it up the hill to get rid of it," Calder Marr said. "You told a fish dock on Thursday that you have been three times to a shop and twice to a house you were told was an office and you could not get anybody to tell you what your own boat was allowed to do with a thing your own boat was supposed to be holding. You did not bring it up here for me to hold it. You brought it up here so that somebody with a trade could tell you, and a conservator told you, and it came out that the seal is false, and now that is done and the only reason left to have it is that it is worth something. And I will not be the man who quietly turns a man's question into a yard's asset while the man who asked it is in the room."

Dace sat down on the bench, which nobody had expected.

"That is a bad answer in a market," he said.

"It is a good answer in a shed," Calder Marr said. "In a market, in about a year, it is the worst thing anybody could have said to a man in front of a crowd, and you know it is, and I know it is, and I would rather be beaten in a market than win in a shed with a man I have just made a present of."

---

"Nessa," Dace said, turning round. "You are the one who says what happens when the plan fails. What happens when the plan fails."

"You give it back," Nessa Pike said, "and you lose the only claim any yard on this dock has had to a real object in five months, and you keep a second column in your book with a conservator's unsigned finding in it, and the finding is true and there is nobody in this coast who may sign it, and the circuit office asks for your book in April and in May and in June, and in July you have a record of a man's coast and a woman's unsigned words about a seal, and the count of the copies of the public sheet is eleven in the office's own type and it is not going down."

"And?"

"And the road is still there whether you claim it or not," she said. "Nobody can take water off a man. They can take a boat's gear off a man, which they did on the fourth of March in this harbor in daylight, and they can take a sheet of glass, and they cannot take a bar of gravel under a neap. If it is worth anything, it is worth something to the men who use it, and it is worth nothing at all on our wall, and we have eleven copies of a piece of paper and no instrument, and if a claim to a thing is worth something to a party then this yard is the poorest party in Morrow Quay and the thing is worth precisely the four marks a day the pump hires for."

Dace made a sound that was not quite a laugh.

"That is the first time anybody has argued against having something by working out what it is worth," he said, "and I have been in this market thirty-one years and I have never heard it at a table with the thing on the table."

"Give him the glass," Mara Venn said.

She had been at the back of the shed with her book shut on her knees, which is a thing she has done perhaps four times in five months, and she said it in about eleven words and then did not say anything else for a while, and what she said was not a trade and was not an opinion and was the plainest thing in the room.

"Because a boat cannot be asked anything," she said, "and if your yard holds the seal and a man in a city asks you who the custodian on it is, then either you say the name of a boat, which is the lie, or you say the name of a man, which is a second lie, and the second lie will be the one that is in the file. A seal that is false in one clause and held by a party becomes false in two clauses the day it is held. That is not a finding about glass. That is a finding about how long a thing takes to be somebody else's problem."

They gave it back at about five o'clock, on the steps, in front of the market, in the same sailcloth, and the boy wrote the hour and the fact and the reason and Dace read it back, and it went in the book as: that the object is in the keeping of Harl Dace, master, of the *Grey Widow*, of no crew of this association, and is not in the keeping of the party, and has not been in the keeping of the party for one hour or one day or one minute, and that the party has declined to hold it and has given a reason in the party's own words which are entered under this line and not shortened, and that nobody was asked to give anything up to make that happen.

"Then there is the check," Dace said, with the bundle under his arm.

"There is the check," Calder Marr said, "on Thursday the thirteenth, six hours of flood after eleven, your boat and our lead and our slate, and there is no permit and no instrument because there is no office in it and no person is going in the water, and I am going to be in your boat on my own account and the boy is going to swing the lead and the shipwright is going to hold the slate."

"And on the fourteenth," Dace said, "or on the fifteenth, or on the Sunday, whatever it is, I want what you find in your own book, in your own hand, with the two columns, and I want the second column to have a reason against it, and I want it to say on the wall in a market that a man of the *Grey Widow* said it and was not paid and was not of this association and was not asked to check it first. And I want it out in your hand, and not in hers, because if it is in a conservator's hand then it is a thing a man in a city will ask her about, and she has no office to be answerable to and she will not be answerable to me either."

"That is the right way round," Mara Venn said, "and I would not have said so and I am glad he did."

The boy had the slate against his knee before Calder Marr had got the door, and he had a sentence in it that nobody had asked him for and that he had been carrying since the Monday on the mole.

"You are going to put a thing on a wall about five miles of coast that this yard has checked one day of and cannot check the rest of," he said, "and the two columns are going to say so, and the wall is going to be a piece of paper with a nail in it and not a seal, and anybody who can read can read it. I have been thinking about it since the Saturday, and a man who wants records to be single is going to have eleven of ours with figures in them and then a ninth thing that is only a man's mouth and two stones in the water. And he will not be able to tell which of ours is the one worth taking, and that is not a thing we have done, because we have never had a page on that wall that we could not stand behind."

"Stand behind what," Dace said.

"Behind the figures," the boy said, "and there are going to be figures on it on Saturday, two of them out of my own lead and one out of a man of fifty-eight in the south lay who has told a market that this is a tale. And if any of those three is wrong then it will be in the second column with a reason against it and not scratched out, and if the whole page is wrong then a man in four years can find out which of the three it was."

"That is the best sentence anybody has said in this shed about a wall," Dace said, "and it is eighteen and it is the eleventh of April, and I would like it in your own right hand and not read back, because I would rather a thing like that were read by one man than by four."

The pump stood on its cart in the shed and had not been out since the Wednesday, and there was a wind outside that had not made up into anything for five days, and the market was shutting, and the eighth thing on the market wall had been standing twenty-four days with a naval paper nailed over one of the sheets beside it, and a working boat was still at the pile under the *Vigil* with her master's logbook in a naval file, and the circuit office had said nothing since the sixteenth of March, and the bag was five.
