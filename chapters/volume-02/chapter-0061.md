# Chapter 61

*The List of Things That Exist*

The pump was on the bench with its head off and its cylinder out and the packing in a cloth, and Nessa Pike had the housing turned over with her thumb in the crack, and she had been at it since half past six on Friday the twentieth of January, and the point of putting it on the bench at all was not to fix it.

"Right," she said. "Say the number."

"What number."

"The number of days a yard in this harbor sells. That is the number you have not asked me for since the fifteenth and it is the only number in this shed that has anything to do with anything, and I have watched you walk past it four times this week." She did not look up. "You went to Kest and asked for a date and a ground and you got a date and a ground and a bill that somebody else is paying. You asked me what we can do and I gave you a smaller thing than you wanted and you took it, and you have been in here since doing arithmetic on a bag with four marks in it as though the bag were a supply of iron. It is not. It is four."

"Then what do you want me to say."

"I want you to say it out loud so that I do not have to," Nessa Pike said. "Say that this yard has three and a half hands and a charter and no gear, and that everything it is going to do this year it is going to do with other people's equipment on other people's ground, and that every one of those two facts has a price attached to it and the prices do not go into the bag until after the bag has decided it can afford them."

She put the housing down.

"And before you say it, I am going to say the part you are going to want to argue with. It is not a pump. A pump is a thing you can put a figure on."

---

The list took until the middle of the afternoon and it is in the crew's book in the boy's own right hand in a column with no figures in it, and Calder Marr wrote the headings and the boy wrote the words, and the reason there are no figures in it is that the boy said so first.

"A list with figures in it is a list of what a man is worth," Bevin Rook said. "I have been at the counter at the sign of the Drowned Bell twice this month and I have seen what a man with a figure on a page gets told about himself, and I am not writing a figure against our own hand in our own book. I will write what it is and what state it is in and the day I looked at it. Somebody else can put the figures on if they want to."

Nessa Pike looked at him for a moment over the housing.

"That is right," she said, "and it is the third time this month you have been right about the shape of a document and nobody in this yard has told you either time."

**The air pot.** A hundred marks a third, and a third of it gone since the fifteenth of October, and the four marks of air bought in January have not put a single mark back into it, because air is bought by the pot and not by the mark. A hundred marks a third is not a figure a poor yard recovers. It is a figure a yard that has already lost it never has.

**The pump.** A web cracked in the housing and a sleeve packed in the reeve's tallow and no new housing to be had on this coast before the spring, and the eighth of March is a Wednesday, and so the pump is done for the winter. It cannot be worked. It cannot be hired out, and hiring the pump out is how a yard in this harbor pays for a bad winter, so the four months of not being able to hire it out is not a fault in a machine. It is a fault in an income.

**The hand-crank.** Hired, out of the net loft, at a mark a tide. The yard's own, not the harbor's. The harbor's is two marks a day and comes out of the authority's repair account on the authority's own work, and the yard's itemisation of the fourteenth of January did not carry it, and the two are two hires and not one.

**The lead and the slate.** The yard's own. The lead is a good lead. The slate is a good slate and has been a good slate since October.

**The boat.** Not the yard's. Tobias Wren's, when he can be had, and he cannot always be had, and when he cannot the yard is at three men and a half in someone else's hull.

**The *Kittiwake*, on the hard.** Her bilge is blocked in with oakum and nothing else, which is a thing you do to a boat you are not going to float, and she has been on the hard since the twenty-eighth of November, and she has no cradle under her at all, and a cradle is four days of a carpenter's time and this harbor's carpenters are on the authority's own account and are not doing private work in the three weeks before forty herring boats come in. So the *Kittiwake* sits where she sits until the fleet is in and the carpenters are free, and after the fleet is in nobody can say when.

**The bell.** Hung on harbor gear in eight and a half feet off the south end with the reeve's own chart notation that has never been taken off, and a rope a year, and a man's day every four or five years for the tongue, and a person in a boat in the weather the rest of the time. Eight marks and a day of a boat was the going out on the eleventh of January and it will be eight marks and a day of a boat again in a year and the year after, and the yard has never once entered it in a plan, and it is entered in the list tonight with the date on it, and Corvin Sarn is not told and does not need to be told, because the reeve put the cost in his own chart in his own hand on the twenty-eighth of November and the yard has been reading it since.

**The clerk's thirteen.** Twenty marks of shoal iron on a stack, unsold, a third of the sale entered to Sella Brigg for February, and the whole of it the bond of the one-season provisional working charter, which means the one thing in this yard that anybody else would call capital is a thing another office can take.

**The book.** The boy's. And the bell, and the slate, and the hand lead, and a wall in the fish market with seven things on it.

Bevin Rook read the whole of it out twice, and then read out the nine heads again without the words under them, and then said:

"That is nine, and eight of them are a shortage of something and the ninth one is a boy, and I have read it twice and I would like it in the book with the date and the hour and no figure against any of it, because in a year somebody is going to ask this yard what it had, and the answer is going to be *that*, and not a number that a man put in when he was feeling strong."

"Put it in," Nessa Pike said. "And put Saturday the twenty-first at the top, because the slate wall gets wiped on Monday the twenty-third and anything we want kept out of a cloth has to be in the book by Sunday."

---

There was one line left and it was Calder Marr's to give, and he gave it about four o'clock, standing at the bench with his hand flat on the housing, and he gave it badly and then gave it again.

"This yard has three and a half hands," he said, "and a charter that runs to the last of March and can be stopped after an inspection for cause by one man with a key and a book, and no gear. Everything it does this year it does with other people's iron on other people's ground. That is the whole of what we are. Say that at the top."

"Calder—"

"Say it."

Bevin Rook wrote it, and read it back, and then said the thing nobody in the yard had said in five months and which he had obviously been composing since the twentieth of November.

"You have got the number wrong and it is your own arithmetic and not mine, and I am going to say it once. It is not three and a half hands. It is three men and a book's worth of hands. A hand is a thing that can be hired and we have not got one of those and we cannot have one, because the notice of the thirty-first of October names you, and names her, and names me, out of the water on the authority's work, and a notice that names the three of us is worth more to a man who wants to hire a crew in this harbor than the price of the hire." He put the pencil down. "And I am not saying that to be clever. I am saying it because you have written a list of things that exist and the list has a mistake in it, and the mistake is that you have counted me as a hand. I have read a book out loud for four months. I cannot take a warp. I cannot hold a lead line and a slate at the same time. In June I will be able to hold a lead line and a slate at the same time, or I will not, and the bonesetter has written *a boat hand in June* in her book and told me it is not a promise and not a lie, and it is the twentieth of January."

The shed was quiet for a bit, and down the dock a gull was picking at a rope.

"Enter that," Nessa Pike said, "in your own right hand, with the date, and do not soften it, and I want it in his own hand and not in Calder's."

"Enter it," Calder Marr said.

---

The reason the boy had got it right, and the reason he said so himself, was that Alden Marr had been at the far bench since half past six with his back to the room and had not spoken to anybody since the thirteenth of November, and had not been asked for anything, and was not asked for anything now, and nobody went near him, and he was not part of any of it, and he was in the shed.

Around five o'clock the boy looked up from the column of nine heads and said:

"Corvin Sarn's going out is on that list at a mark of eight and a day of a boat and I have written it and I have not sent it to him and I would like it sent to him, in my own hand, on Monday, with the date, because he said in this yard on the eleventh that he would read the book when it was written, and he has not asked to see it and he is not going to ask to see it, and a man who said he would read a book should be told it has been written."

"Do it," Nessa said. "And do not take a share of anything to him and do not offer him a meal, because the moment either of those two things happens it stops being his going out and starts being ours."

Then there was a knock on the shed door, twice, and it was not a knock that wanted anything, it was a man with a boat alongside who had come up the steps and had decided to make a noise about it.

He was about thirty-four, wet to the knee, with a pump housing in each hand and a slung toolbag, and he put one housing down on the bench beside Nessa Pike's and looked at her, and then at the cracked one, and then at Calder Marr, and said:

"That is a third go, is it."

"It is a third go," Nessa Pike said.

"Aye. Then you are the crew I've heard of." He put the other housing down and straightened up. "Noll Tarner. *Sea Wren*. I have got a dredged housing in my boat with a new web and I have got a crew of two and a half and one of the two and a half cannot read, and I have got a claim window in this harbor authority's book for the twelfth seam on Thursday, and the twelfth seam is not the toe of that mole and there is no notice on it, and I have been up and down this dock for four days working out who in this harbor can hold a line and write down what comes up it, and there is a man at the net loft who told me the only book on this coast that is read out loud is in this shed."

"You could have asked at the quay," Calder Marr said.

"I asked at the quay," Tarner said. "I asked at the quay and I asked in the net loft and I was told the price of a reader in this harbor in about nine words and I would rather look at the man than be told what he costs by somebody else's boy. So I am here, and there is a housing on your bench that is not mine and mine is in my boat, and if you have got a week in you I have got a day and a rate, and the rate is twenty-six for the day and the eighth and your share come off the top of it because I have read the wall in the fish market and I know how you work, and I am telling you I know, so that you do not have to find out at the end of the day that you have short-paid me."

Nessa Pike looked at the dredged housing on her bench for about three seconds, and then at the cracked one, and then she said the thing Calder Marr had not thought of, which was the second time in six days that somebody in that shed had said the thing he had not thought of.

"Twenty-six is the right rate and you are the right man to ask," she said, "and before either of you two says anything else, here is what has to be true on Thursday morning. On Thursday morning Calder Marr writes out a reason in this harbor authority's own book at eight o'clock in the morning, in his own left hand, with the clerk standing there, and it is not edited, and it says what the work is and it says who is going to do it and it says how long, and if there is no purpose in it then it goes in with no purpose in it and Rook enters the hole afterwards with the date on it, which is what we did on the eighteenth of January and I would like the same thing done again and I would like it done by somebody who has done it once."

Tarner looked at her.

"Who writes it," he said.

"My mate, if you can read it."

"I can read it," Bevin Rook said, from the bench, without turning round, "and I have written two of them and I can write a third, and I would like it in the book in my own hand and not copied, and I would like the count of the copies on the wall to be right by the Sunday, and the wall is a convenience and the book is the record and both of those are correct and neither of them is a reason to be slow."

Calder Marr looked at the cracked housing on the bench and then at the dredged one and then at the man standing in his doorway with the water running off him onto the shed floor.

"Six o'clock on Thursday morning," he said. "Here, and we will be on the water at the top of the flood, and the reason goes into the reeve's book at eight, and if the reeve names a different day we take his day and not mine."

"The top of the flood on the twenty-sixth is a quarter to ten," Tarner said.

"Then we will be on the water at a quarter to ten and not before."

The shed emptied, and the boy wrote the twenty-first of January in and wrote the nine heads under it, and put the pencil on top of the book, and then took it off again and put the note in front of him and read the position out to nobody, because there was nobody there, which he had never done in five months.

"Four marks in the bag," he said. "Nothing in the tin. The note at thirteen and the fortnight's eating on it on the thirty-first. And a man with a dredged housing has just told me the rate out loud before I could cheat him, and the rate is twenty-six, and the note is at thirteen, and I have been at the counter of that shop twice this month and I know what thirteen is."

Then he shut the book.
