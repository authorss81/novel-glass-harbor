# Chapter 51

*Five Marks in a Bag*

The stove had not been lit since Sunday and Calder Marr lit it at half past six on Wednesday the fourth of January, and by the time the water was on it the harbor had a light on it that would be no use to anybody for three hours, and the boy was already at the bench with the book open and the pencil lying flat on the page and his left arm across his chest in a sling Nessa Pike had re-done on Friday out of washed webbing, because the old one had gone soft.

"Read it back," Calder said. "And from the first of December, not from the top."

Bevin Rook read from the first of December.

"Five marks in the bag. Nothing in the tin, and nothing in the tin since the eighteenth of November. Nothing has been put into either of them since the first of December, which is thirty-four days, and there is no day in this yard's book where a mark comes in."

"And the note."

"The note is written up in the chandler's own book as eleven weeks into six, which is the last figure anybody ever wrote in it. The yard's own count of the weeks is thirteen and has been since the eighteenth of November, and thirteen is a figure in Calder's head and it is not in this book and it is not in the chandler's, and I am not going to write a number in this book that nobody can check." He looked up. "That is the first thing I have said this year that is not in the book."

"It goes in the book," Calder said. "Not the number. The fact that the number is in my head."

"That is a worse thing to write down," Bevin said, "and I will write it if you tell me twice."

Once was enough.

---

There were six things on the market wall and the top one was a naval claim, four sheets with a captain's signature line at the foot of the fourth. Under it, slipped four inches so that a man had to lift a corner, were the three sheets of three points with a difference of ten yards taken off them and a fresh one over the top saying the difference was withdrawn by the yard and that the sheet inside said why. Then a notice of four clauses with a circuit's seal over the nail. Then the public record of two objects in a conservator's fair copy with every name that had touched either of them on it, Marr and Rook and Pike and Wren and Mott and Venn, and two crew copies of the same thing printed without her name. And at the bottom, under a rule, the fifth-seam sheet: a thing seen in eleven feet, not measured, not touched, under a bar.

Nine or ten people stood in the cold in front of it, which is a good crowd for the fourth of January, and one of them was reading the fifth-seam sheet out loud in the voice of a man who cannot read.

Down at the quay the harbor authority's slate wall was in a window and blank, and it had been blank since eight on Monday the second, because the clerk takes the cloth off the top left corner at eight and goes across the whole board and does it every Monday whether there is a sitting or not, and there has not been a sitting since the twenty-eighth of November. The next wipe is Monday the ninth, at eight.

"Five days," Bevin said. "A figure on that board is a figure for five days. The ledger is the record and the board is a convenience, and the reeve said so himself in a room, and he is right."

"Or it is worth nothing," Nessa Pike said, coming down the slip with a lamp in her hand, "and we have wanted a figure on it since the seventh of November and we have not got a sitting to put it in, and I want that written down as a thing we wanted and did not get, because the next man who says the yard could have said so in a room is going to cost us a month."

She put the lamp on the bench.

"And the harbor's crane and the harbor's barge are the harbor's this morning, and the harbor is paying their hire out of the authority's own account on the authority's own ground. And the pump is a crack in a housing, and the pump is also a pump we cannot hire out, and I would like somebody to say the second half of that sentence out loud in this yard once this month so that we all hear it."

"You have said it," Bevin said, and wrote it down.

---

Mara Venn came at nine with her book shut under her arm and did not ask for anything, and said it standing in the middle of the floor with her coat on.

"The query of the twenty-first of October came off my file this morning. It is not on a notice. It is a line in a book in an office in the west of this city, and this morning it says the query is closed, and it does not say who closed it and it does not say on what ground, and I have read it twice and there is nothing on the page that is a punishment, because a punishment has a name and a date and a signature and this has none of the three."

She put the book down on the bench and did not open it.

"So here is the arithmetic, and I would like it written down, because you are all going to work it out badly and I have done it properly for eight years. A conservator with something left to refuse is a thing an office will employ, because the refusing is what she is for. A conservator with nothing left to refuse is a conservator an office will find expensive to employ. That is not a complaint. That is a price." She turned her head. "In about four months a shipwright in this harbor is going to want a certificate and she is going to find out what a certificate costs, and it is going to be a great deal more than it was in October, because in October I had a quarter of a standing and now I have none. The three weeks in the queue were always going to be three weeks. Now they are three weeks with nothing at the end of them that can hurry them."

"How long does a certificate take," Bevin said, in the flat voice, and Calder Marr thought, not for the first time, that the boy asked the questions nobody else was going to ask and asked them in a room with four people in it.

"Three weeks. Nobody can hurry it, including me, and I have never hurried one in eight years, and I would rather you found that out in January than in March."

She picked the book up.

"I am going to be expensive," she said, "and I am going to be right, and there is no version of those two where you get both cheap, and you should all stop looking at the door to see whether I am going."

Nobody thanked her. She said they would not, and she was right.

"There is a second thing from the circuit office in my bag," she said, "and I have not opened it and I am not going to open it in this yard. It is addressed in my hand because a clerk in that building cannot address a paper to a salvage yard in Morrow Quay and have it be worth the paper. And the copying room is a day's walk each way, and the walk is free, and the walk is mine, and I would like a morning of it in February and not this week. The second thing will cost us a day and not a mark."

"A day is not a mark," Nessa said.

"A day is three men not on a wall," Nessa said, "which is the only thing this yard has."

---

They went down to the sign of the Drowned Bell at twenty past ten, and the chandler had his door open and a paraffin lamp going, and Calder Marr said the thing before he had said good morning.

"Six. I want you to write a new figure."

The chandler put his hand on the book and did not open it.

"It is a fair thing to ask. I have been expecting a man to say it since the eighteenth of November and I had not expected it from you, and I want you to know it is not a thing against you. I have written that slate the way a man writes a slate. The last figure I put down was six, and every week after that the six went on standing, and nobody came in and paid anything against it, because you did not come in, because you had nothing to come in with, and I am not going to be a man who writes a figure down and calls it a kindness."

He opened the book, and found the page, and read down it with his own finger, and Calder watched a man be accurate at his own expense.

"Against the sixth of November there is the six, and that is what my book says and what your note says and it is true. Since the eighteenth of November you have taken from me a fathom of marline, a stone of tallow, two staves of candle, oil for a stove twice, bread for three men and a half for eight weeks, and a quart of vinegar you have never accounted for and I have never asked you to. The vinegar is on there because I do not put anything off a slate that has not been paid, and if you want to argue with the vinegar, argue with it now."

"Argue with it later," Bevin said.

The chandler wrote. He wrote slowly, which he had never done on that page, and he wrote the day at the top, and the items, and the sum at the bottom, and turned the book round and set it on the counter facing the three of them.

*Nineteen.*

"And a line under it," he said, "which is a line I have never written on this page in nineteen years. It says: entered at the asking, on the fourth of January, by Marr, of the Free Keel yard, with a request against the figure, that it be paid as the first money that comes into the yard and not out of a bag that cannot."

"You have never written a request before."

"I have written *please* before, and *when there is anything*, and I have struck a figure out twice. I have never once written down that a man asked me to, because a man who has asked is a man who can be told, in a shop, in a road, or in a hearing room, that he asked. I have written it tonight because a man who asks has a date on him, and I would rather have the date than have the money for a month."

"There is nothing to pay you with."

"I know exactly what there is to pay you with," the chandler said, "and it is nothing, and you have told me so at my own counter. Sign the line, and then say out loud the thing you came in here to say, and I would rather hear it than be told it in three weeks by a man having a better week."

So Calder Marr said it. On the first of December, at this counter, he had told the chandler he would take the note to him on the second and pay it off the first money that came in. The second of December came and went and he did not come, and it was not because he forgot it, and the chandler had been owed an explanation for thirty-three days and was not going to get one, and would get the money when there was money, which was the only part of the sentence that was a promise.

"That is the first time a creditor has been told that by a debtor who came in to ask for something," the chandler said. "Most of them come in to be pleasant about it and leave the pleasantness where it was."

He signed, and the boy came after him and signed underneath, because a ledger with one name on it is a ledger one man can change. Then the chandler shut the book and put his hand flat on it.

"Twenty-two weeks on my own count, by the way. Not eleven. Eleven is what I last wrote, and there is a difference, and I would rather you had it from me than work it out in a dark shed in March."

"Thirteen," Bevin said. "By the yard's own count, on the fourth of January, it is thirteen weeks, and it has been thirteen since the eighteenth of November, and it is a figure in Calder's head and it is going to stay in his head, because if a man writes thirteen weeks in a book then in three weeks it is a lie, and it cannot be rubbed out without taking the page."

The chandler looked at the boy, who was twenty-two, and had a sling on and a right hand on the counter, and said, "Then it is thirteen," and shut the door, because it was half past eleven and there was a market to open in an hour.

---

They came back up the fish dock with nothing in their hands, which is how a yard walks home from a chandler, and there was work in this harbor and not one hour of it wanted them. The fishery did not hire. The quay had a coaster in on the Tuesday's terms and not the Wednesday's. A man came to the shed at twenty past twelve and offered a day with the *Anna Ray*'s gear and would not say what tide and would not say what was wrong with it, and Calder said he would send a figure, and the man said to send it on a good week, and went away without giving his name.

At two o'clock the boy read the position back and read it twice, and the number came out the same as it had come out every day since the first of December, which is what a boy with a book and a yard of three and a half wants more than anything else in the world, and what he gets.

"Five marks in the bag. Nothing in the tin. Nineteen on the note and nothing paid against it since the eighteenth of November. Forty marks to me, sixteen for the reading and twenty-four for the hand, and not one of them. Forty marks to Mara Venn, first after food, and nothing since the fifteenth of October, and I am not going to stand in a market and say the word food about it. Twenty marks of iron on the stack, a third of the sale entered to Sella Brigg for February, and the whole stack is the charter's bond at a clerk's thirteen. A pump with a housing nobody on this coast can fit before the spring. The *Kittiwake* on the hard with no cradle. The first of March is eight weeks off and it is a Wednesday, and there will be forty herring boats in this harbor on it and one berth, and the ninth seam's mooring is out and the north berth is out. Elias Voke will write the coast office a statement of this authority's capacity to moor the fleet for nothing, and put the north berth and the ninth seam and my name in it, and the coast office has not answered the first letter and this authority has not written the second one it promised in a room on the twenty-eighth of November."

"That is a long one."

"Read it back."

"It is a long one," Bevin said, "and it is correct, and I am going to write it out longhand tonight for the wall, and I am not going to put a number on the wall in chalk, because the board is a convenience and there is no sitting until somebody calls one, and a wall with no ledger behind it is a thing a man can take down with a cloth, and the cloth is at eight on Monday."

Nobody argued. It went into the book, and the book went on the bench, and the pencil went on top of it, and nobody in the shed said anything for a while, which was worth as much as the figure.

---

The naval man was on the quay at half past three with his back to the market wall and his notebook shut under his arm, and he was a man out of a cutter's boat and not the officer, and he had the easy way of standing in a cold street of somebody else's town of being very comfortable in it.

He asked the question in the road, in front of eleven people who had stopped at the wall, pleasantly, the way a man asks a thing he has already written down.

"Does the Free Keel crew mean to be working inside the line this season."

Calder Marr answered it in the road, in front of the eleven people, in one sentence, because a sentence long enough to be argued with is a sentence a man can answer a different way tomorrow.

"We are working inside the line on a charter of the harbor authority, on the harbor authority's own ground, and the harbor authority may stop it after an inspection, and it has not been stopped."

The naval man laughed at the word *charter* in a friendly way.

"A charter is a piece of paper about a ground and I asked a man about a season. There is a cutter at a cable's length off this mole with a claim over a mile of water, and a captain who will be interested in a crew that means to be inside the line all winter, and a man who only works where a piece of paper says he may is not a man we have any business with."

"Put it down the way you like," Calder said. "You have been in this harbor since the eighth of October and you have asked me two things, and the answer to both of them is on the harbor authority's own chart and in a hearing record with a date on it, and both are about paper. The one you want is not paper. So write down that we mean to be inside the line. We do. Then write down the date, because the date is the nineteenth of January and it is a Thursday and the reeve named it, and if a man in a boat wants to know what a Free Keel crew does with a date, he can come and stand on the mole at the top of the flood and watch us use it."

Nobody in the road said anything. Eleven people from the market wall, and a man with a notebook, and a boy of twenty-two with his arm in a sling who was already in his notebook, writing, in the cold, in the flat voice, for anybody within nine feet of him to hear.

"I am entering that," Bevin said. "The vessel's question, the vessel's words, the hour, and the answer as it was given in the road, and the date. And I am entering that he did not ask it a second time after the answer. It is a fact about a man in a road and not about a man in an office, and if a naval file has it, then the file has it right, which is more than I can say for most of what goes into one."

The naval man looked at the boy, and then at Calder, and then at the wall behind him with its six things on it, and did not ask it a second time, and went down the quay.

That was the cost of the day, and it was not five marks. It was that a Free Keel yard's whole position — four months of a hearing record, a charter, a bell, three custody numbers, and two people who would not say a thing aloud — could be read off a wall of its own making in about four minutes by a man with a notebook who had been standing on the quay since two o'clock. The wall was the yard's own doing. The yard had put it up that way on purpose, in public, in three parts in four hands, with the limits written inside the rules, and had been right to.

And the second cost was in a shop at the sign of the Drowned Bell, in a book that had never had a line in it before, and the line said a man had asked, and the figure above the line was nineteen, and the money under it was nothing, and not one mark of that nineteen was available to anybody in this harbor this week, and the first of March was eight weeks off and it was a Wednesday, and a mark a week off a note of nineteen is a wage, and this yard has not got a wage.
