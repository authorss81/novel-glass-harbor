# Chapter 96

*What the Market Would Not Take*

The market on Saturday the twenty-ninth of April had about three hundred people in it, which is a great many for a harbor that is a week into a bad spring, and about a third of them had come in from a boat that had been at sea since Tuesday and had put in at the first light, and the third lay was lying in a row at the top of the boards with her nets stowed and her crew standing about in other men's boots looking for nothing in particular.

The yard did not go down. It sent the boy down at half past eight with a slate and a sheet of the yard's own paper, and the boy was in the market for two hours and came back up the hill at half past ten with twenty-six marks and eleven on a slate, which is a sentence and not a sum, and four men in the market had said the cause out loud before they put anything down, and not one of them would let anybody say a word about it afterward.

---

Edric Pringle was the first up the hill and she came at ten past ten and she had six marks in her hand and she would not sit down.

"Four hundred feet of my own herring warp came off the wharf at the second lay on Friday in a fire and the fire party of this harbor authority took it off with the cart, and I said the cause on Tuesday in a market and it is in your book and it is in the clerk's book and I have not been thanked once and I am not going to be thanked now.

"So that is six marks, and it is for the warp and for a day of my boat and a night of two men with a hose, and the hose is my own fire and I have had that one free, and I have had it free and I am not complaining, and I have not come up this hill to be paid for a fire. **I have come up this hill because a man with a bad hand went into five fathoms of water at the top of a tide on the Friday night and got a man off the ground where an old boat of the third lay had been lying, and that man is not the man I am thinking of and he is not in this harbor's water and I have not seen him since the twenty-eighth of January, and I have not asked anybody anything about it.** That is the whole of it. Take the six or do not take it, and do not tell a market about me, because a market will tell anything if you stand still long enough."

"It is going in the first column with your name against it, and the fire is not in it," Nessa Pike said, "because the fire is free and it is not in anybody's book but ours and the harbor's."

Harl Dace came at half past ten and put eight marks on the bench and would not sit down either, and he had the *Grey Widow*'s wheelhouse door open behind him across the road and two of his men carrying a coil of warp up the hill.

"That is my own money and I want the cause of mine said first, which is a night's work and a boat's loss and a compressor gland of yours that is not a compressor and is not to be paid for. **I was not paid on Friday. I was not paid and I am not asking to be paid and I want that in the book, because a man in four years is going to find my name in your money column and he is going to think a man of the *Grey Widow* got a fee out of a drowning, and there was no fee, and the fire party of a harbor authority is not a salvage crew and the difference is not a word.** There is a hundred and forty years in this harbor of men going out in bad weather for nothing at all, and the whole of what has changed in the last five years is that a man can be given a form for it, and a form is a thing to be got back, and I am too old to want it back."

"That is going in the first column in your own words and I am not going to touch one word of it," the yard said.

Elias Voke of the herring boat *Anna Ray* came last, at about a quarter to eleven, and he had twelve, and he put it on the bench and squared it with his thumb and then did not take his hand off it for a while, the way a man does who has been thinking about a figure for two days.

"Twelve marks, and it is a day of a boat at eleven and a night of three men at eleven and two hours of a man in a pole on the ninth seam's mooring at two in the morning, and that is four marks a man and it is a day's rate, and I know it is a day's rate because I have paid that rate since I was twenty-two. I have written it in my own book on Friday night and read it back, and it is mine and I am not asking this yard for a shilling of it. **And I am not paying for a bell.** My own warp came off the mole in February because this harbor's own moorings gear broke a ring off a stone in its own foundation, and the cause of that is in a book in this harbor and the cause of Friday is in a book in this harbor, and the two are not the same fault and I have not put them in the same place and I would like a clerk to check."

"Entered in the first column with the hour and the man and the rate," said the boy.

---

Coll Ferrow came up at the end with four marks in a hat, which was somebody else's hat, and the somebody was a man of the second lay who would not put his name to it, and Coll Ferrow put it on the bench and stood with his thumb on it and said that he was the carrier and not the giver and the number of men was not his to give and would not be given by him, and that if anybody in that shed asked him how many men put in he would say he did not know and that this was true.

"That is a figure and not a cause," Nessa Pike said. "And I am not going to let a boy put it in the first column, and I am going to have to explain why in front of four people instead of in a book at eleven at night, so listen.

"A man in a hat is not a man I can ask again in four years. He is not on a crew's list, he is not in a lay, he is not on this office's page and he is not on the harbor authority's page, and there is no office in this coast that can be asked about him by name, and the only paper that will ever exist about that four marks is the one the boy is about to write. I cannot put it in the first column because the first column is what a man on a mole can check, and what a man on a mole can check about four marks in a hat is that four marks went onto a note, and that is the whole of it and no more. It goes in the second column and the reason goes against it, and I have written the reason and I have read it to Calder and he has not argued, and I am going to read it out now because the four of you are the only men who will ever be in the room where it was said."

She read it out. It was a sentence long and it was the best thing in the book all that spring and it went in as written, and the boy read it back twice and then said the thing that was waiting at the end of the list.

"Four marks out of a hat, and the name of the man who carried it, and the hour, and the fact that the number of givers was not given and was not asked for, and that the party is not going to ask."

---

Then the money went out, and the order in which it went out is the whole of what happened in that shed on the last Saturday in April, and Nessa Pike said it out loud before a mark of it was touched.

"We have a rule and it is the best rule this yard has got, and it is air, then rope, then food, then the harbor's eighth, then the harbor's share, and it comes off the top of anything raised, in that order, every time, and it has been in that book since the twenty-fifth of October and I would put it on a wall in a town that had walls.

"**This is not a raising.** This is a hat, a woman's warp, a man's boat and a herring skipper's day rate, and they are not the same thing and they are not the same men, and I am not going to take a fifth off a fire party and call it the harbor's share. So the rule is not used today and I am entering that the rule was not used today and why, in the first column, with my name against it, because the rule is ours and I am the one who is not using it and nobody else in this room is entitled to say a word about it.

"What it goes on is three lines. **Eight marks to this harbor's bellwright, being the going out on the second of May at eight marks and a day of a boat, and every mark of it bought today at a market by men who said the cause out loud first.** Sixteen marks to the boy, and Calder will pay that one to the counter himself and he is not going to touch it and neither am I. And six marks onto the note at the sign of the Drowned Bell, and that is the biggest single payment off that page since the eighth of March and it is the only one in six weeks that has not been bread."

The chandler came up at half past three with his cart and took six marks off a page that has been in his own book since the fourth of January at nineteen marks and twenty-two weeks with a line under it and both signatures, and he did not go up the hill again, and he did not offer a fortnight, and Calder Marr did not ask him for one.

---

The boy was paid at the counter in the shed at about half past four on the Saturday the twenty-ninth of April and he worked it out on the slate in front of three people and he worked it out twice and the second time was the same.

"Sixteen marks is four weeks. It is not the first four weeks that were paid in February; those are paid. It is the ninth, the tenth and the eleventh, which fell on the fourth, the eleventh and the eighteenth of April and are three lines in that column, and the twelfth, which fell on Tuesday the twenty-fifth and is the fourth.

"And I have left the fifth, the sixth, the seventh and the eighth, and the fifth is the oldest line in the column and it went in on the seventh of March, and I have left it because I am not going to take a man backwards in his own book, and because when somebody comes along in four years and asks why the oldest line is still in it, the answer is going to be in my hand and the answer is that a boy of twenty-two did not want to be paid out of order." He put the slate down. "That is four lines and sixteen marks and it is the most the column has ever lost at once, and not one mark of the four came out of this yard's own work, and I am not going to say one more word about money in this book and I have already said four."

Nobody in that crew asked him one word about it, then or afterwards, and the forty marks of the claim stand whole on the page above his name, unpaid, unasked for and not asked to be reduced.

---

Corvin Sarn came up the hill at half past four because Nessa Pike sent for him, and he is fifty and has a blacksmith's forearms and he has been this harbor's bellwright since the twentieth of October, and he stood in the doorway and looked at the eight marks on the bench for about four seconds and then said the whole of what he came to say.

"That is two going outs, then."

"That is one going out, the second of May, and the eight marks are for it and for a day of a boat, and you are the harbor's bellwright and this yard is not paying you to be one, and we are paying you for the second of May, and I have known since the eleventh of January that I could not do that and I am doing it today out of other men's money and I am going to write down on the page that it was bought at a market, because in three years a man in a city is going to find my name in a wage column in a yard's book and think I was paid twice."

"I do not want it in a wage column," Sarn said. "I have been in this harbor since the twentieth of October and I have a tin of my own with about four marks in it, and I have had no going out since the eleventh of January and I have not asked one, and there is a bell on this town's own ground in eight and a half feet of water with a tongue half worn through and a waist that has not been in a boat since the twenty-seventh of February, and you are asking me to go out on the second of May, which is a Tuesday, and I will go out on the second of May if the water is there, and if the water is not there I will say so and you will not argue with me about it.

"Now hear the price of it, because you have written me a figure and I am going to give you a figure, and they are not the same figure. It is eight marks and a day of a boat and that is what the going out has cost for two years and it will cost that next year. It is not mine, it is the harbor's, and the harbor has not got it and I am not going to take it out of a hat in a market and pretend I did not know where it came from, and I have said that out loud in a shed doorway and I am not going to be asked to say it a second time.

"That is the whole of me. **A bell only speaks while somebody keeps the weather working on it, and the somebody is a person in a boat, and the price of a person in a boat is eight marks and a day, and there is no instrument in this harbor that will tell you when it stops.** And you will all get that boat on Monday whatever the water is doing, and if it is a hard water you will sit in it and complain and you will come home and the bell will be no better, and if it is a flat calm you will get nothing and come home, and both of those are the going out and there is not a third kind."

He took the eight marks, counted them, put them in the inside of his coat, and said, on the way out of the door: "And the light on that mole head is a light that this office is paying for with oil out of an account that has been empty since the middle of February, and the third lay went dark on the nineteenth of March because a light costs oil, and if there is going to be a writ about on Monday then somebody in a room ought to be told that a bell in the dark is a bell that is not there, and I am not the man to say it in a room, and I have said it here and that is the end of it."

---

The clerk of the harbor authority came up the hill at half past four on the Saturday and read the whole of the money column and the three causes and the boy's sentence and the second column's sentence about a hat, and he had a page of his own and he wrote it all out in his own hand and read it back and did not shorten it, and he said what he says.

"**This office cannot enter any of it.** It can enter that four sums were paid out of a market in this harbor on the twenty-ninth of April, at the figures, and by the names. It can enter the claim window of the twenty-eighth and the two days' work and the raising of nothing. It can enter the man that was found and the two men that were in the water and the tender's cutoff. It cannot enter what a woman thinks a warp is worth in April, and it cannot enter what a herring skipper's night is worth, and it will not try, and the record is the ledger and I will keep a copy of this page whether the wall is up or not."

"You have said that four times this year," said the boy.

"I have said it four times this year and I will say it in May," the clerk said, "and the wall is wiped on Monday at eight and the ledger is not, and if a person in a city ever gets hold of a figure off that wall and not out of that book, then this office has failed at the only thing it is for, and that will be on my page and not on anybody else's."

The four who read the position back were the boy, the yard, the shipwright, and Harl Dace, who read it standing at the back of the shed with his hand on the door frame; and Pringle's six, Voke's twelve, Dace's eight and a hat with no name in it were entered and were not shortened, and **nothing in that book said whose fault the warp on the north wall was on the twenty-fourth of April, because no man had said the cause, and the entry says so, and the clerk has it in his own hand as well.**

The position at the end of Saturday the twenty-ninth of April is **thirteen marks in a bag, twenty-seven on the chandler's note after six marks off it in the creditor's own hand and a fortnight of bread in the same six weeks, nothing in a tin, four lines and sixteen marks in the wage column, forty marks of claim standing whole, forty marks to a conservator with no office standing, eight shillings and tenpence to Sella Brigg standing, twenty marks of unsold iron on the yard's own ground with a saw kerf in a crown shackle, no instrument and no claim, and a piece of worked glass in a master's keeping that is not a seal and is not a chart and is not this yard's.**
