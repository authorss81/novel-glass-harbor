# Chapter 74

*The Second Seam on a Moon*

The iron came up the coast in a handcart on Wednesday the eighth of March in the middle of the morning and went onto the note in the creditor's own hand, and a mile and a half of carting is a shilling and threepence, and the cart was the creditor's own and he would not put the carting on that page, and said why, which is that a man who is owed nineteen marks by a yard with a mark in the bag does not add a shilling and threepence to a page for the sake of being paid it in a year. The housing was fitted before the tide turned.

Nineteen marks of iron went on that page and five marks came out of the bag, and the bag had six in it on the Monday night and had one at two o'clock on the Wednesday afternoon, and Nessa Pike made the boy write all four figures in one line and read them back before she let anybody touch the pump.

"Five marks out and one left," she said, standing over it, "and I am not going to have a man be pleased about a pump today without somebody saying the other half. A pump is not money. A pump is a thing that makes money on a tide when somebody with money wants it, and there is exactly one tide in this harbor in any month where a fouled ring of a herring boat at the second seam is worth doing, and that is the moon, and the moon is on Friday."

"Friday," Bevin Rook said, and wrote it, and added under it, with no adjective anywhere in it, that the fifth week of a wage at four marks a week fell due on Tuesday the seventh and is not paid and the party has not asked and the holder has not been told a date by anybody.

The line went in and nobody in that shed said one word about it, and it was read back twice, and that was the whole of the treatment the fifth week got, and the rule that has held since the first of February is that a week of a wage is entered as a fact and is not a subject, and the first of March is not a precedent for anything except the last of February.

---

At about a quarter past ten on Friday the tenth, with the moon two days off full and the spring flood running hard up the mole's face, Calder Marr asked Nessa Pike to say the last sentence of the reason again, and she said it again, and he wrote it after her, and the boy wrote it under his, and Calder Marr read his own back and it came out right, and it was the sixth time since the fourteenth of October and he had asked her to do it and had not pretended he had not.

"Say the state of the water again," he said, and she said it, and this time he caught it the first time and did not have to ask twice, and neither of them said anything about that either.

Then they went down to the mole at half past ten and stopped at the crown, above the marker at the eleventh, because that is where a man stands now, and the marker cut with its ring of paint that has not been repainted since the thirtieth of October was four feet above them and the wind was in the west and the mole's face was running with the flood.

Sella Brigg was on the second seam's ground with two of her own men and her own ring and about four feet of her own warp gone, and she had been up the hill twice since the fourth of March and had not once said the word *held* to anybody in that shed, and she said it now.

"I am not going to pretend I have not noticed it," she said. "I came down here on Tuesday with twenty-two marks and two men of my own and this yard was shut and you were in a city, and I am not bitter about that and I am telling you what it is: I have a man asking me every day whether I can hire a pump in a blow, and there is one pump left in this harbor that is not the harbor's and it is standing on a cart being bolted together, and I am told by four people this week that it is on my account on Friday for four marks a day. Fine. Twenty marks for the day, and the pump at four of it, and a diver out of the *Grey Widow* at two because I have had two of mine taken out on a fall since October and I am not putting a man of mine on a fouled ring at the second seam in a spring flood. And you do not get the eighth and the share off me twice, because the last time I came down here in January I had somebody else's figure on a wall and I read it on the way, so read it out."

"Twenty gross," Bevin Rook said, from the boards, "the eighth three, the share three, air two, rope a mark, the diver two, the pump hire four, and the yard's is six. And the pump is the yard's and is not the harbor's and is hired and not lent, and that line is in the first column because you can check it by asking the man who wheeled it down the mole."

---

The reason went into the yard's own book at eight that morning, in Calder Marr's own left hand, in six lines, and the boy read it back, and it named the second seam of the mole's own face and said that the ground is landward of the third seam and is not within the first clause of the notice of the thirty-first, and that nothing is raised, and that the work is a herring skipper's own ground tackle on her own account, and that the party in the water is proposed to be Tam Slee, hired out of the *Grey Widow* and not of the party, and that the limit is fixed by the diver at the boat, and that the tender is Nessa Pike and is not the diver, and that the cutoff is hers to pull without asking anybody.

It went into the yard's book and not into the harbor authority's book, because no permit was needed and no instrument of that office was used and no person was put inside any closure, and Nessa Pike said so out loud in the reason itself, in the third line, and made the boy read that line back twice on its own.

Slee set his own limit at the boat, which was thirty minutes, and would not take forty off anybody. Neale was back on the mole that morning with the tide gang whole for the first time since the twenty-fourth of February, and he put three men on the second seam and one of them was Wil Slee, whose brother went over the side at twenty past eleven and who has said nothing about that and put the guide line himself.

The pump ran for two and a half hours in a spring flood on a wheel that turned true for the first time since the fifteenth of January, and a thing the yard had been carrying since November turned out to be a thing the yard owned.

At eighteen minutes the diver said the ring was under a shelf of the mole's own rubble and clear, and at twenty-two he said he could get a chain on it, and at twenty-six he came up with nine feet of warp bent through it and the splice good, and Nessa Pike pulled the cutoff at no particular moment at all, which is what a cutoff is for.

Above them, at the crown, Calder Marr read the first clause of the notice of the thirty-first out loud to nobody, the way he has read it at that marker four times since the twelfth of January, and the words are the words, and they close the toe and the inner face from the third seam to the eleventh to all work and to all approach by all persons, which includes this office, this yard, and any other crew in this harbor, and the second seam is the second seam and is a hundred and forty feet inside the boundary and belongs to a woman with a cart.

---

The entry went in two columns and it went in the way it has gone in since the twenty-sixth of January, and it took forty minutes to write out, and it is now in another crew's logbook as well as this one.

*First column, checkable from the mole: the diver, Tam Slee, hired out of the *Grey Widow* and paid at the figure named in the reason; the tender, Nessa Pike, and the cutoff hers; the air read at the boat by the diver and by nobody else; in the water at twenty past eleven and up at forty-six minutes past, that being twenty-six minutes of a limit he set himself at the boat and would not take forty off anybody; the moon, two days off full; the flood; the state of the water, a west wind and a hard run on the face; the pump, the yard's own, hired to the party for the day at four marks and worked for two hours and twenty minutes; the men of the harbor's tide gang on the face, being Neale at the ring bolt, Wil Slee on the guide, and two others.*

*Second column, not checkable from a mole: what was under the shelf of rubble before the chain went onto it, which is the only figure in this entry that no man on this mole could have told anybody; the state of the ring's inner face; what the water was doing under the shelf in a spring flood at twenty-two minutes, which is in the second column because a man in a boat can see it and cannot stop it; and the diver's own figure for the depth of the shelf, which is three and a half, and which is his, and which goes in as his.*

The four who read it back were the boy, Slee, Pike, and Sella Brigg, who had come down for it and who said the same two words Edric Pringle said standing on a wall in February, which were that the first column was right and the second column was honest, and who then gave a third thing that nobody had asked her for.

"Put in that I was here and that I read it," she said, "and do not put in that I was pleased, because I was not pleased. I am fifty-one years old and my ring came up on the same morning I heard that a working pump went into a lighter in this harbor in daylight, and one of those two things paid for the other and I would rather that was not in a book. I want the ring in a book."

The ring went in, and so did the third of the shoal iron, undiminished, still eight shillings and tenpence short of a third of twenty marks, still in her name, still not paid, and nobody in that shed said one word about it then and nobody has said one word about it since the sixth of February.

---

The money was read out on the Friday night and it was the shortest column of the winter and the boy read it twice and then read the position.

"Gross twenty, and it is Sella Brigg's and out of her own account and not a word of it is the harbor's. The eighth three and the share three. Air two, and the pot is not a third gone this week and the reason it is not a third gone this week is Wednesday. Rope a mark. Tam Slee two. The pump four, hired, and the pump is the only gear in this yard that anybody can hire and I want that entered in that column and not in an inventory.

"That is fifteen out of twenty and the yard's is five, and the bag had one mark in it at two o'clock on Wednesday and it has six, and the note was at one mark at the counter on Wednesday and it is at twenty marks, and on Tuesday a fortnight of bread, oil and candles goes onto that page in this creditor's own hand and comes out of this bag, and I am entering both tonight so that nobody has to do it in the morning."

The shed was quiet for a moment with the stove going.

"Read the last of it back," Nessa Pike said.

"The last of it is not money," the boy said. "The last of it is that on the fourth of March a working pump belonging to a crew of this association was put into a lighter on this dock in daylight with a form and a signature line, and that from that day until this Friday the only pump in this harbor that a man could hire was the harbor authority's, at three a tide, out of the harbor authority's own account, and that the only reason there is a line in the column above for four marks a day is that the housing came up the coast on Wednesday. I am not writing an opinion under it. I am writing that two dates are in the same book and the yard knows which one made the four."

Nobody read that back, because it was not a thing to be read back, and Nessa Pike did not tell him to read it back, and she took the pencil out of his hand and put it on the bench and said: "That is the best thing anybody has written in this book in five months and you are not going to read it back, because a thing that is read back four times becomes a rule, and that is not a rule, that is a Tuesday."

And on the dock outside, in the dark, a boat went up the hill that was not a herring boat, and somebody shouted for a hand, and the shed was shut, and the air pot was two marks fuller than it had been in January, and the fifth week of a wage was three days old and unpaid and in a column, and Sella Brigg's eight shillings and tenpence was on the same slate where it had been since the sixth of February, undiminished, and the moon was coming up over the mole and there were two men on a mole in a spring flood who could read a book and neither of them was one of them.
