# Chapter 53

*The Warp on the North Wall*

Elias Voke came down the north wall himself at half past seven on Friday the sixth of January, in a coat with nineteen winters of dues in it, and he had come because the north berth has been out of service since the first of December and a herring skipper whose berth is out of service does not sit down about it, he comes and finds out.

"I want my ring and my warp moved one berth along," he said. "That is the whole of the request. South berth to the second lay, forty feet of wall, and the warp is a boat's length and a half of the best tarred stuff on this coast and it has to come up out of the water and go along and be laid again where the other boats can get round it. Your boy is in the book and I have read the book, because a man told me to read it in a net loft in November and I read it and I have not stopped thinking about a line on it."

"Which line."

"The one that says no other crew's paper is in that book." He put his thumb on the wall. "I have forty boats in this harbor on the first of March and a berth short, and I have a piece of paper on a wall that a boat's master can read, and I want to know whether that paper is a true thing about my fleet or whether it is a thing that says what somebody needed it to say. That is all. Now. My ring is fouled and it has been fouled since October and it is going to be fouled again by March, and I want it moved, and I will pay a day's rate and I will pay a bad one because I have got a fleet coming in and no berth for it."

"We will do it Saturday as well," Calder said, "and we will do it for twenty-four for the two days, and the eighth and the share come off the top of it because the charter says so and I am not going to argue that in a shed to a man who was in the room when it was read."

"You are not going to argue it."

"I am not going to argue it and I am not going to pretend it is not there," Calder said, "and I would rather tell you now than have you find it out in a market and think we did it behind you."

Voke looked at him for a second.

"Twenty-four for two days is a bad rate and I know it is a bad rate," he said, "and I am paying it because I have nowhere else to put her, and the harbor authority promised me an answer about the coast office in a room on the twenty-eighth of November and has not written it, and the coast office has written me one line with the word *capacity* in it and I do not know what capacity means in a word."

"It means they want a statement of what this harbor can moor," Bevin Rook said, from the wall, with the book under his arm, "and the reeve has not sent one, and Mr Voke has said he will write one himself and not be paid for it, and it is in a hearing record with a date on it, and I have read the date out in this yard twice."

Voke turned round.

"You are the boy with the hand."

"I am the boy with the hand," Bevin said, "and I read the book, and the date is the tenth of November, and the promise was a fortnight, and the fortnight was the fifteenth of December, and it is the sixth of January."

"Yes," Elias Voke said. "That is the whole of the fleet's complaint against this authority and it fits on a boy's hand."

---

They did it on the Friday and the Saturday with no diver and no boat and no pump, off the wall, with the capstan the harbor's own men keep on the north end and a hired hand-crank out of the net loft at a mark a tide, which came out of the twenty-four and is the first time a number went out to a person outside this yard since the *Low Lantern*'s stove-in boat, and it is entered in the book as a hire and not as a gift.

The tide was wrong for it. It came at the wrong end of the slack and by ten o'clock on the Saturday there was half a foot of slop coming over the wall run and the ring was under four inches of moving water and Bevin Rook could not see it, which is the condition a harbor puts a boy in when it wants him to be useful and not to be in the water.

He took the capstan bar in his right hand and a coil of the guide in the same hand and could not get the bight on his left, and he did it anyway, and it took him four minutes that it should have taken forty seconds, and he did not ask anybody and he was not asked, and after a while he worked it out of his own account by putting the line over his right shoulder and hauling with the right and the hip, which is a thing a man does on a lighter and which costs him his back by the fourth hour.

"That is not safe," Nessa Pike said, in the end, at half past two, not to him, to Calder.

"No," Calder said.

"Then say it to him."

Calder said it to him. The boy put the bar down, and shook his right hand out, and went up the steps, and did not speak for about twenty minutes, and then came back and finished the last hour on the guide and not the bar, which is not what he had been doing and which is the whole of what a yard can do with a hand like his in January.

---

The money went in the book on the Saturday afternoon and the boy read it out and read it back twice.

"Gross on the board, twenty-four, from Elias Voke of the *Anna Ray*, and it is a private work on his own ring and his own warp and his own account, at the north wall, landward of the third seam, with no permit, and that is not a favor of this office and it is not a breach of anything, because the notice of the thirty-first closes the mole's toe and inner face from the third seam to the eleventh and does not reach a herring skipper's own ring on the north wall, and no notice has ever closed water."

"The eighth and the share," Nessa said.

"The harbor's eighth, three. The harbor's share, three. Rope and the two days' victuals, three. That is nine out of twenty-four and the yard's share is fifteen."

Fifteen marks on a Saturday afternoon in a shed on the fish dock, on the sixth and seventh of January, was more money than had come into that shed since the twenty-seventh of November, and for about a minute nobody said anything, and the boy wrote it in and wrote the outgoings under it in his own right hand, which he had been taught to do on the sixth of December and had not had cause to do since.

Then Calder Marr took a mark out of it and put his coat on.

"Where are you going," Nessa said.

"To the sign of the Drowned Bell," he said, "and I am going to put one mark on that note and I am going to do it on the day it was earned, which is the seventh, and not on the Monday, and I know what a Monday would have said and I am not going to say it."

---

The chandler took the mark and turned the book to the page and wrote the figure himself, which is a thing he has not done since the eighteenth of November, and did not comment, which is worse than comment, and then said, because he was a man who had put a line in his own book on Tuesday and had not stopped thinking about it:

"Eighteen," he said. "And there is nothing else to say about that, and you have come in on the day and not on the Monday, and I have written the day."

"That is all I came to do."

"Then you can wait while I put the page away," the chandler said, "because a man who waits is a man who has not got somewhere else to be, and there is not much of that in this harbor this week, and I would rather you waited in a warm shop than stood on a quay in it."

Calder Marr waited in the warm shop for about four minutes, and while he waited he thought about the fact that on the first of December he had told the man behind that counter that he would come on the second, and had not come, and had come today instead, six days after the note in that book had been nineteen marks with a line under it saying a man had asked, and that a promise is a thing with a date on it, and that he had made one and broken it, and that the only honest account of the two facts was the nineteen in the book on Tuesday and the eighteen in it tonight, and that neither of those figures had been in it on the second of December.

---

Neale caught him on the mole steps at four o'clock with the tide making and his hands in his pockets, and he had not come down to say anything and had come down because a foreman who reads a market wall knows what is on it.

"Three things," Neale said, "and I am not the man who put any of them there, and two of them are the reeve's and one of them is nobody's, and I would like it understood which is which before I say them."

"Go on."

"The ninth seam is still out. We have three men standing by on the Friday, the Saturday and the Monday, waiting on a fouled shackle under a stone that is in this authority's own foundation, and the shackle came off the ring last winter and the ring came off the shackle and the ring has been in the water since October with a stone over it, and I have wanted that mooring worked since the first of December and I have asked the office for it twice and I have been told twice that a crew that wanted that mooring worked would want it worked by men in the water on the authority's work, and the third clause of the notice of the thirty-first stops every man in this harbor who has ever handled transparent material, and there are three of them, and one of them has a hand that cannot close." He said it in the flat voice of a man reading his own list. "I have got the gang on three days' notice and nowhere to put the three days, and the fleet is coming in on the first of March and the warp I have got on my shelves is nine years old because the money has gone on the fenders and the moorings and not on the warp."

"That is one."

"That is one, and it is the reeve's price and he told a room what it cost before he paid it, and I was in the room, and I want it said that the reeve has never once pretended it was somebody else." Neale shifted his feet. "The second is that the coast office wrote four lines back on the twenty-eighth of November and one of the words in them is *capacity*, and the authority has still not written the answer it said in a room it would write, and it is a month gone on that, and I am a man who works under the answer and not a man who wrote it, and the fleet does not distinguish between those two men and it should."

"And the third."

"The third is nobody's," Neale said, "and it is mine to say because I am the man it is going to happen to. The first of March is a Wednesday. It is seven weeks and five days off. There are forty boats coming in and one berth short, and the berth that is short is the north one, and the north one is the one that takes a northeast blow, and on the last three northeast blows that came into this harbor in my time, the fleet on the other side of the basin lost eleven boats' worth of ground tackle and one boat, and the eleven belonged to men who could not pay for the eleven and the one belonged to a man of sixty-two who has just paid nine winters and cannot pay the tenth."

He took his hands out of his pockets and looked at the north wall.

"That is what your notice costs, and you did not write it, and I am not asking you to write it, and I have known since the thirty-first of October what it costs and so have you, and the thing I have come down the steps to say to you this afternoon is that a man who works under a leash is allowed to say what the leash is for, and this one is for a thing at the fifth seam that nobody in this town has been allowed to touch, and I have thirty men who cannot pay for their own winter."
