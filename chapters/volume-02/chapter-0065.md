# Chapter 65

*Four Marks on a Wednesday*

The hearing room's slate wall was wiped on Monday the thirtieth of January at eight and Calder Marr stood in the road and watched it again, and this time he said the useful thing out loud, which was that a man who wanted a figure kept in public should not put it on a board, and the boy wrote that down too.

The thirty-first was a Tuesday and the chandler took five marks off the note and Calder Marr carried the coin up from the shop himself and put it on the counter in front of the man, because in November a figure written on a note had been sitting on that page as though it were paid, and nobody in this harbor was going to make that mistake twice in one winter if Calder Marr could help it.

The note went to eight. The bag went to twelve. Both figures were read out in the shed at four o'clock by the boy, and the two of them are the same five marks and not two of them, and the boy said that out loud, twice, because that was the mistake this yard made in November and corrected, and nobody in a shed was going to make it again in one winter.

---

The clerk came up the fish dock at ten on Wednesday the first of February with a canvas bag and a receipt and the weather behind him, and he put the bag on the bench and read out of the receipt rather than out of the bag, because a clerk is a man who reads the box.

"Harbor authority, west basin. Day work, the nineteenth of January instant. The holder's own time on this office's ground, at the rate entered in this office's book on the seventeenth, at eighteen marks, with the eighth and the share off the top, the sum of fourteen marks, and the bills of this office in this quarter going out on the first of the month."

He put the receipt down on top of the bag and did not untie it.

"Fourteen," Bevin Rook said. "And it is not a raise and there was nothing to raise, and the eighth and the share are the two and two that came off the eighteen before it ever came to a bag, and it is one of the two and two and not a second of the same two and two."

"Read the receipt back," Nessa Pike said, and the boy read it back, and then Calder Marr untied the bag and counted it on the top of the bench in front of five people, and it was fourteen, in coin and in a note of ten and two crowns, and the crowns went into the tin because the tin had been empty since the eighteenth of November and a tin with crowns in it is worth more than a tin that is merely open.

Twelve and fourteen is twenty-six, and out of that came the boy's first four marks, and it was the first of February and it was a Wednesday, and the boy had worked the date out in his mother's front room in January and had not mentioned it to anybody since, and he had come into the shed that morning with his right hand washed, which he had never done.

---

He did not take the four marks off the bench. He put his hand flat on the page instead, the way he had been made to do in his mother's front room, and he waited until the twenty-six had been read back, and then he said:

"Enter where the four came from."

"It came from the harbor authority for the nineteenth of January," Calder Marr said. "You are owed a wage and I am paying a wage and the wage is being paid out of a day the yard worked on the mole's toe with a lead. Those are the facts and they are in the book in that order."

"That is not what I asked." The boy had the pencil out and had not written. "I asked you to enter that the four marks of my first week came out of the harbor authority's account and not out of this yard's own, and that they came out of an account the harbor authority has been drawing on since the seventeenth of January for four sets of moorings and a season of warp and a day of Neale's gang standing at a wall watching three men it cannot use."

"That is a long sentence to put against a wage."

"It is one sentence," Bevin Rook said, "and it is in the yard's own book, and in four years the only person who is going to know that a boy of this harbor was paid a wage out of a mooring is whoever reads that line, and if the line is not there then in four years a man will find a pay sheet and think the harbor is generous." He looked up. "And I have been owed forty marks since the twenty-ninth of October and I have not asked for one of them, and a wage I do not name the source of is a wage a man can be quietly proud of, and I would rather it was a wage I could say out loud in a market."

"Enter it," Nessa Pike said. "In his own right hand, the source, the date, the account, and that he asked for it and nobody in this yard suggested it. And Calder, do not put your hand on the boy's arm, because he is twenty-two and he has just asked for a thing that costs him nothing and it will cost him something later and he is doing it now while it is free."

The four marks went into his own hand and he read the entry back twice and put the money in his coat, and Calder Marr did not say a word about it, and that was the whole of the morning.

---

Sella Brigg came down on Monday the sixth of February at about eleven, and she had come herself, and she had a cart with a boy in it who was not hers.

"I am not here about the cover," she said, before she was through the door. "The cover went off the slate on the twenty-ninth of October on my own statement and it is in your book with the date on it and I am not going to take it up again today, because if I take it up again it will be a negotiation and I did not come here to negotiate with a yard that owes me a third of twenty marks."

"A third of twenty marks is six marks, eight shillings and tenpence," Bevin Rook said, from the bench, without looking round, "and there is no coin in this harbor for the two thirds of a penny that is missing off it, and I have got that on the slate, and I would rather write the true figure than write a round one and be found out in March."

"You have been writing down my arithmetic as well as your own," Sella Brigg said. "That is a thing I did not expect from that book and I am going to have to think about it and I would rather not think about it in your shed."

The money was on the counter in front of her, six marks, and she counted it twice and did not put it away.

"The third is paid," Calder Marr said. "It is a third of the sale of the stack and the stack is unsold and the whole of it is the bond of your charter."

"It is a round six," Sella Brigg said. "Your boy has the true figure on the slate and the true figure is eight shillings and tenpence more than what is on this counter, and I have been owed since November, and I am not going to be handed a tidy sum and be told the rest of it is understood." She put the coin in her apron. "The stack is the bond of *our* charter, since the twenty-eighth of November, and the yard is the party and I am the crew that keeps the party solvent, and I have not come here to argue the difference. I have come to say one thing and then I am going up the hill with the cart. There is a soft blow coming and it is coming from the wrong quarter for the fleet, and I have been up the hill to every man in this harbor who takes work off the wall and there are two of them, and one of them is a man off a boat called the *Sea Wren* who has been off the slate since November and who I have never worked with and who I would work with tomorrow."

"What about Harl Dace," Nessa Pike said.

"Harl Dace has the only cover in this harbor inside the line and not past the marks, and he has not been asked and has not offered, and this yard does not know whether it stands after the twelfth of January, and that is in your book as a question and it is a real one." She got hold of the cart handle. "So that is my business. I wanted a yard to know that there were two men to hire off the wall in a blow and one of them had a falling out with me and I am telling you about it anyway, and I am not being thanked and I do not want to be, because if I am thanked then next month I will come and not come."

She went up the hill. Calder Marr put the eight shillings and tenpence on the slate under the six marks in the boy's own right hand, with his name against it and the date, and it is entered there and it is not paid, and he said that he would rather owe a woman eight shillings and tenpence than have a round number in a book that a clerk could not check. The bag went from twenty-two to sixteen, and the tin had two crowns in it, and the boy wrote the figure and read it back.

---

And on the next day, the seventh, after the wage had gone out, Mara Venn came up the fish dock with a book under her arm and asked for six marks, and said it in the first four seconds, before she sat down, because she had decided in the road that she was going to do it that way or not at all.

"Six marks for a certificate of method and three weeks in a queue at the end of it, and I am not going to tell you what it is for because I do not have a thing for it yet and if I invent one it will be a lie by Thursday."

"We have one mark."

"I know exactly what you have." She put the book down on the bench and did not open it. "I have been in this yard eleven weeks with no standing and I have been expensive in it and I have not been useful in it, and this is the first thing I have come here to ask for, and I am not going to pretend that I have not noticed the shape of it. Calder, look at me. You are not going to pay me and I am not going to let you say that you cannot. You can. You have one mark and a note at eight and a boy's wage on the first of March and a mooring on the ninth seam that is bought and not worked. The sentence *we cannot* is the sentence of a man asking me to do the arithmetic for him and I have done it for him since the fourth of January and I am not going to do it again in the same week twice."

"Then say the rest of it."

"The rest of it is that there is a plate in a mole in this harbor that a conservator's certificate of method is the only document in the world that could put a hand on, and there are two plans for it and both have been refused correctly, and I am the only person in this coast who could write a third one that any office would countersign, and I cannot, because a certificate is an act of an office and I have no office." She picked the book up again. "So in about two years there is going to be a hearing in Veyr about a mole, and somebody is going to say that a conservator stood on a wall in Morrow Quay for four months and knew the ground, and she will be right, and it will be worth nothing, and the reason it will be worth nothing is in a line in a file in the west of this city dated the fourth of January, and there is nothing you can do about it and there is nothing I want you to do about it, and I have come here to tell you about it once in daylight, in your own shed, and then I am going to be expensive at somebody else for the rest of the winter."

"Then what do you want," Nessa Pike said.

"Nothing," Mara Venn said. "I have a stall-holder's stall four rows back who has a hull he wants a man to look at before he pays four marks of freight, and I am going to do it for nothing because I have nothing to charge, and if it goes wrong it will be a thing a fisher said about a conservator, and I will take that, and I would like the book to say that I asked you for six marks and you had one and I did not ask twice."

"Enter it," Nessa Pike said. "All three parts, and that she asked, and that she did not ask twice, and that the forty marks stand, and Calder, put the one mark back in the bag and do not put it on that note, because that mark is the only mark in this yard that is not spoken for, and I have decided this morning that it is the air for the seventeenth of February, and I have written it down, and you may argue with me on the seventeenth about it and not before."

She went down the dock, and the book went on the bench, and the pencil went on top of it, and nobody in that shed said anything for about a minute.

"I have been in this yard five months," Bevin Rook said, to the top of the book, "and I have entered a great many things in it at other people's asking, and in the last week three people have asked me for nothing at all, and the one that frightened me is the man who put his hand on my head and said a figure was correct, and I am not going to write that down anywhere."


The second week's four marks went out of the same bag on the Tuesday the seventh of February, and the boy entered it in the same form and read it back twice, and the bag went to twelve. And on the Tuesday the fourteenth the third week went out and the fortnight's bread, oil and candles went onto the note at the sign of the Drowned Bell and came out of the same bag, and the note went to four, and the bag went to four, and on the Friday the seventeenth the yard bought two marks of air and a mark of rope and the bag went to one, and the whole of that fortnight is in the book in the boy's own hand in a single column with no adjectives in it anywhere, because he had promised Nessa Pike on the twenty-eighth of January that no adjective would go in a column and he had not broken it once in twenty days.

And the fourth week fell due on Tuesday the twenty-first of February and the bag had one mark in it and the boy wrote the entry himself before Calder Marr said anything, in his own right hand, with the date on it, and it said that four marks were due to him on that day for the week then ended, and that one mark was in the bag, and that the other three were not, and that he was not asking for them in this book and that the book was the wrong place to ask.

"You are not going to ask me at all," he said.

"No," Calder Marr said.

"That is correct and it is not kindness, and I want it written down that it is not kindness." He squared the book against the bench. "Four marks is not a thing a man asks for twice. When I was fourteen I crewed for three weeks in the summer for nothing because my uncle said he would remember it, and he did remember it for eleven years, and the eleven years were worth more than the three weeks would have been. A wage is a thing a man works and does not ask after. If I ask you on the twenty-eighth of February then I have asked, and if I have asked then the four marks on the first of March is the fourth time and the fifth is never. So I am going to work the twenty-eighth and I am going to leave the four marks where they are, and I would like you to leave them there too, and I would like it entered in this book that I did not ask and that you did not pay and that neither of those two things is a thing either of us has to explain to anybody."

"I am not going to put that in the book," Nessa Pike said.

"You are going to put it in the book in one line," Bevin Rook said, "because the rule is that four men read the same day it is written, and you are not going to have the twenty-eighth come round with nobody in this yard knowing that I had decided in advance not to ask, and if I had a bad day and I did ask, then the twenty-first is a document and the twenty-eighth is not a mood."

"Enter it in one line," Nessa Pike said. "And the forty marks stand and are not reduced and are not to be mentioned in the same breath as the four, because the forty is a claim and the four is a wage and I will not have a man of twenty-two taught that the difference is a matter of emphasis."

And the first of February was a Wednesday, and it had been a Wednesday when the yard entered a wage of four marks a week into a book in a shed with a boy's hand on the page, and the fourteen marks had come out of the harbor authority's own account on the same morning, and the first four had been paid on the same afternoon, and three of the four weeks had been paid, and the fourth had not, and Sella Brigg's third of the iron had been paid at the counter out of a bag that had a mark in it by the end of that week, and Mara Venn's forty marks stood whole and were not paid and were not going to be paid out of any of it, and the note was at four, and the air pot was a third gone and a quarter of a mark worse than it had been in January, and the pump was not going to be fitted before the eighth of March, and the mooring on the ninth seam had been bought with the harbor authority's money on the seventeenth of January and was worked on the seventeenth of February and is in service, and a mooring is a place to tie a boat and a berth is a place to lie in a wind, and the first of March was a Wednesday and it was in eight days and there were forty herring boats coming into a harbor with one berth short, and Bevin Rook wrote all of that on the twenty-first of February in a single column with no adjectives in it, because he had promised Nessa Pike on the twenty-eighth of January that no adjective would go in a column, and he had not broken it once in twenty-four days.
