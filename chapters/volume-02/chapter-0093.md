# Chapter 93

*The Green Water*

It came on the Wednesday, the twenty-sixth, out of the north-north-east with the glass still falling at four marks an hour, and it came on the town the way it came on the sea, which is to say that a man in Morrow Quay at twenty to five in the morning could not tell whether the water had come up the harbour or whether the harbour had come up him.

Bevin Rook got up at four because he was twenty-two and has been getting up in the dark since he was nine, and he went out to the head of the dock in his boots and stood there, and came back in about ten minutes with his coat streaming and said one sentence in the doorway and it was this: "There is a green sea inside the harbour and the second lay's store roof is being salted."

He said it in the doorway because he was not going to stand in a warm shed and say a thing like that, and the yard and Nessa Pike were both in the shed and both were awake, and the sentence took about a quarter of an hour to do its work on the three of them.

"Green," Calder Marr said.

"Green and white and it is coming over the top of the second lay's wharf in a sheet and there is no white on it, and there is white on the rest of it going out past the mole, and I have never seen the inside of a harbor do that, and I have been in this harbor since I was in a basket on this dock and I am twenty-two years old and I am telling you that I do not know what that is."

"Then it goes in the book with the hour and not with an adjective," Nessa Pike said. "Go and get your coat."

---

It was not a word for it in Morrow Quay. The boy found one, or made one, on the Wednesday morning, and put it in the second column with a reason against it, and the reason was that no man on a mole in this harbor could have been inside the harbor on the Wednesday morning of the twenty-sixth of April and come back with the same figure twice.

He called it a glass storm. There was no ground to call it that. It did not come off the water, it came through it, and Calder Marr said so at about nine, standing in a doorway with his hands wet.

"It is coming through," he said. "That is the whole of what I can tell anybody and it is not nothing, because the only glass anybody in this harbor has heard about for six months is one sheet the size of a folded chart in a sailcloth under a counter at the back of a shop, and that is a different kind of thing and it is in Dace's hands and it is not ours, and I am not going to hold two things together in one morning. This is weather. Whatever is in it, it is in all of it, and it is in the water and it is in the air and it is in the stone of this mole, and there is no instrument in this harbor that will tell you one thing about it."

"Nine fathoms of a ship's stem on a sandspit, eleven feet on a cairn of ballast, and a bar of gravel," the boy said, from the bench. "That is a road. That is three figures and a line of them on a hand lead and they were right twice, and I have carried the second cast of the stem for three weeks and I know what it weighs and it is not nothing, and the man who swung it was a man of forty-eight and he was right about a bar of gravel to the quarter of a foot on a flood off a spring. And that road is five miles north of this headland in this wind, and I want to know what the road is doing now, and I cannot know, and I am not going to stand in this shed and guess."

"Then go and look at the rocks at the Rooks' end of the point and see whether it is making a hole anywhere, and if it is, then it is a hole in the town and everybody in this harbor will know about it by Thursday, and it will not be a mystery. And if it is not making a hole, then it is weather, and it is the worst weather anybody in this town has had in a year, and it will be called a storm in June." He turned round. "That is my opinion and I am not entering it, because I cannot check it and I have not had it out in a boat. Write what you can check and put the rest in the second column, and I will say the rest out loud, and it will be a man in a shed guessing and not the yard."

---

The boy read the day back on the Thursday morning, which is a day late and was going to be a day late every day for the next ten days, and Nessa Pike did not argue about it because the man who reads the position and the man who reads the day are not the same man and both of them are necessary. It is the longest entry that book has outside the seventeenth of April.

*First column, checkable by any man who can get to a mole in this harbor: the wind at north-north-east and rising through the morning and at its worst between two and four in the afternoon, and the glass falling at four marks an hour at six in the morning and still falling at nine at night; the mole steps awash twice and the third lay's outer boat taking green water on her quarter; the spray going over the roof of the coal store at the second lay, which is a store that is out and empty and is being salted at four o'clock in the afternoon; the ninth seam's mooring in service with a pole against it and the pole not moved, and the fouling on the ground line between the third and the ninth seams untouched, which is the fourth time that page has said so and the fourth time it has been true; the two boats of the third lay not in; the third lay's boat still against the second lay's wharf with forty of her men in it; forty men of the third lay stood in the harbour on the second lay's wharf from half past four in the afternoon until dark in six inches of water with buckets, and one man of them sick at about six and put on the second lay's boards; the harbor's own hose cart standing on the second lay with a wet hose laid out in eleven lengths on the boards, which is the hose that was on it on Friday and which nobody can roll and nobody can lift and which is a fire party's hose for the rest of the season; the *Anna Beth* hauled up on the second lay's slip at ten in the morning with her pump stopped and her bilge making, and the yard's own hand at her pump being a man of no crew of this association who came up the hill at three o'clock for nothing and would not take a shilling.*

*Second column, not checkable from a mole: the thing that was done to sound, which is that at about half past two in the afternoon in the worst of it, on the roof of this shed, with the rain going sideways, the boy heard his own voice behind him and about a yard off, and he turned round and there was nobody on the roof and there was nobody in this shed who had said anything, and he has had it before and has not written it before, and it is written now because a boy who cannot read well is still the only person in this yard who is going to be on a roof in a thing like this, and the reason against it is that no man in this harbor could check it except by asking him and he is not going to be asked about it; what a man of fifty-one with a bad wheel heard at the same time, which was a voice telling the wheel to come round, twice, in a flat voice, and he had the wheel in both hands at the time; what two men heard at the second lay which is the same thing with different words in it; and that the boy is not saying the two are the same, and is not saying they are not.*

Then the boy put the pencil down and said the thing about the bell, which was the sentence that made it a page and not a complaint.

"There is a bell on this town's own ground in eight and a half feet of water off the south end of that mole, and it has not been looked at since the twenty-seventh of February, and it is three months and a half now, and there is a man in this harbor who would come out in a boat about eight marks and a day of that boat to look at it. And I went out on the point this morning at first light and I stood where I could see the mole head and I listened for the tide bell, which goes at the top of the flood and comes back out of the water off the south end a hand's width of a second later. And I could not take the delay."

"Why not," said Nessa Pike.

"Because there was a sea on that mole the whole time I was standing there, and a sea that looks like that puts four things into your head at once, and I have got a figure in this book that says a hand's width of a second, and I have had it since the sixth of December, and I am not going to write a new one this morning because the water was ugly. If I write it down in this weather I will be writing down what I heard and not what came back, and those are two different things and one of them is in the first column."

"That is right," said Calder Marr, "and I am going to say the other half of it. **I could not place the sound either.** I asked Nessa to say a thing to me twice and I had it back twice and I would not be able to tell anybody which of the two times was the one I got. That is a fact about me and not about the weather and it goes in the second column, and the reason against it is that no man in this yard is going to be asked about it and none of them has been."

"It is in the book," Nessa Pike said. "In your words, with the hour, and the reason is in your words, and nobody is going to ask you."

"Nobody has," said the boy.

---

At four o'clock in the afternoon Ewan Tristram came up the hill on foot with his hat in his hand and the water standing in the brim of it, because the telegraph was down on the second lay's pole from about two o'clock and the wind took the insulators off it, and the mole steps were over the top at one point and he had gone round by the north wall where the warp is laid.

"I have got a man sick on my own wharf and a doctor in the south lay who will not come over a mole head in a sea like this, and I have got a boat at the end of the first lay with nobody in her, and the man on the mole head will not go down the steps to me for money. So I am going to walk round that mole to the north wall, where the water is not going over, and find somebody to carry my man in a chair from the second lay to the first, and I want to know how many of you are in and what you have got, and I am not asking for it as a favor and I have got no money and I am not going to pretend that a hat on a slate is a fare."

"We have nobody in," Nessa Pike said, "and the pump is at the second lay on the harbor's own account, and this is a shed with a boy in it and a man of fifty-one with a bad wheel and two men with a bad shoulder between them, and the far bench is under the leak and there is a man of sixty-one sitting on it and he is not going anywhere today and neither is anybody else in here."

She said it in about nine sentences and she said it in the order of the things, and the wharfinger listened to the end of it, and then he said the sentence that made the rest of the day.

"Then I am going to stand in your doorway," he said, "and I am going to be in the way, and there are two men in the third lay at this moment who cannot stand up, and they are at the second lay, and the third lay's boat is lying against that wharf, and I am going to be in your doorway until somebody in this house tells me that there is no point in it."

So that is what happened on the twenty-sixth of April, and it is a thing nobody in this harbor would believe if it were told at a table: a wharfinger of the second lay stood in the doorway of a yard that owes a boy four marks a week from a shed with a leaking roof for six hours in a north-north-east gale, and the reason he was there was that he had a man who could not stand up and four hundred yards of mole that no man would cross, and the reason the yard did not do anything about it was that it had nothing to do anything with.

The man at the far bench moved off his bench at about six o'clock when the drip found it, and moved it himself, and moved it into the same shed, and the two of them were in one room for nine hours and did not speak, and Calder Marr did not go over to him, and that is the whole of it.

---

The boy read the position at four the next afternoon, the twenty-seventh of April, and the outgoings for the Wednesday had been a lamp and oil and a hand of coal for a stove, a mark out of a bag of eight, because a candle in a boat is a candle in a man's hand for four hours and the yard has three lamps and two of them are the harbor's.

"The bag is seven," he said. "The note is at thirty-three and has been since the twentieth. The tin has two crowns in it and they have not been touched since the first of February. The wage column is eight lines and thirty-two marks. The iron is on the yard's own ground and unsold and there is a saw kerf in a crown shackle. And there is one mark on this page for a lamp and it is the only mark of it that has been in this book since the fifth of April, and there is a lamp in this shed tonight because of it."

"Read the owing back," said Nessa Pike.

"Forty marks to the boy for the reading and the hand, standing whole, not reduced, not asked to be reduced, and not paid. Forty to a conservator with no office, first after food, unpaid since the fifteenth of October, and she was refused four for an opinion on the twelfth and the forty is not one mark smaller for it and she said so out loud on a mole. Eight marks and a day of a boat to this harbor's bellwright, owed since the twenty-seventh of February, and I am not going to write the same sentence about October again in this book. Eight shillings and tenpence to Sella Brigg in her own name, undiminished, unpaid since the sixth of February, and about four hundred feet of her own warp came off the second lay's wharf on Friday in a fire and most of it is in the harbour and is worth nothing in the spring and everything now, and the yard has not got it and has not sent for it and is not going to."

He put the pencil on top of the book, and the wind was still in the same quarter at ten at night and had been for nineteen hours, and the position in the book at the end of Wednesday the twenty-sixth of April is **no instrument, no claim, and no paper of any kind, one lamp, a leaking roof, nineteen hours of north-north-east, and a bell on the town's own ground that nobody in this harbor can hear and one boy who can and will not write down a figure he did not take.**
