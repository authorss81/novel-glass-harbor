# Chapter 78

*The Third Lay*

Harl Dace hired the pump and a half day on Tuesday the twenty-first of March for a shackle of his own at the first seam, and he came down the fish dock at half past seven to say so in person, which he had never done.

"I have read your wall," he said. "I have read the eighth thing on it this morning and I want to say the rest of it before we go down, because you are not going to get another morning like this one. I have had your copy of the public sheet in my net loft since November and I asked you in January who it was for and you said you did not know. I am hiring on it. That is the answer to my own question and I did not arrive at it, you did, in November, by putting a sheet of paper on a wall in a place where a man who hires hands has to walk past it four times a day, and I have thought about that all week and I am not going to pretend I have not."

"Fourteen for the half day," Bevin Rook said, from the boards, reading off the slate, "the eighth two, the share two, air two, and a hand two, and the yard's is six, and the pump is the yard's and is hired and not lent and the figure goes in the first column because you were here when it went down the mole."

"That is what I came to tell you," Dace said. "Read the figure out again and put it in the book in that form, because I have been in this market thirty-one years and I have never once had a man read my figure back to me before I have paid it."

---

They were on the first seam at nine and the shackle came up at twenty past ten with a foot of the mole's own rubble still in the eye of it, and the pump ran for an hour and ten minutes, and a boy on a mole with a book called the depth of the water at the wall's head every four minutes without being asked and was not told he was doing it wrong, and there was nothing whatever in the day's work that was remarkable and it is the only day of that month that was not.

It is in the book in two columns with the first column checkable from the mole and the second column not, and it is four men reading it back, and the fifth was Harl Dace, who read it standing on the mole in his own oilskins and said the two words that Edric Pringle and Sella Brigg have both said, and then said a third one of his own.

"Put in that I read it before I paid and that I am paying on the strength of it, and put in the date, because I have been a man in this market for thirty-one years who has had two men drowned on ground somebody else called his own, and the second one was on a day nobody wrote down. There was a day nobody wrote down. That is the whole of what I have to say and I have said it in a net loft in January and I am saying it on a mole because there are eleven of these sheets in this harbor and a man who reads one of them knows that this yard writes down the day, and I am not a fool and I did not hire you for a shackle."

The shackle went in, and the rubble went in the skip, and Harl Dace paid fourteen marks in coin on the head of the fish dock at about one in the afternoon in front of two people, and the boy read the figure back twice.

---

The boy off the *Sea Wren* came up the hill at about four and found all five of them in the shed and asked whether anybody was going.

"Going where," Nessa Pike said.

"Out. There is a man called Neave Tarrant of the third lay with nine winters of his own and he went out on the Saturday in the blow and he has not been back and two boats have been out on the Saturday and the Sunday and one came back on the Sunday with a man in her who was not looking for him." Piet Marrow had his hands in his sleeves and he was fifteen and he was not asking to be sent. "My master says a boat with a pump and two hands is what the third lay has not got and that the fleet's own boats are on their own moorings and that the men who would go are the men who went to the north wall in a blow, and that a man in the third lay has been in a fouled hull twice since the first of March because there are four boats in a lay that takes one."

"Read it back and put the hour on it," Nessa Pike said, and then: "No."

"You did not ask me yet."

"You are fifteen and there is no tide gang in this harbor that will put a man of fifteen in a boat with a search in it, and I have hired forty men in my life and I have not lost one and it is because I have never once done a thing like this, and I am not going to start in the last fortnight of the herring season with a boy who cannot read a chart." She shut the book. "That is me and not the law and you may write that I said it and not that the law said it."

"Then who."

Nobody in that shed answered for a while, and outside the fly went along the water with the tide.

"I will say what I can and what I cannot and I will say it in the order I say it," Calder Marr said. "What I can is this. A man of the third lay went out in a northeast on the twenty-eighth of February at nine winters of his own and has not come back, and I have not been asked to go and I am not going to be the man who says yes in about four seconds, because four seconds is not long enough and I have been in this harbor five months watching a man say yes to a thing in four seconds and then price it for a year.

"What I cannot is the second half. I cannot go. It is not modesty and it is not the weather. The notice of the thirty-first names me in its third clause and stops me from the water on the authority's work, and a search over the third lay is not the authority's work, so the clause does not stop me, and I have checked that, and I checked it this afternoon before you were up the hill, which I would like entered in the book with the date on it and not with any reason at all, because a man who says no and does not look is a man who is hiding behind a clause and I looked.

"So it is the other three things. I have sixteen days of a charter and it runs out on Friday the last of this month, and a search in a northeast is a day I cannot sell, and a day I cannot sell in the last fortnight of the herring season is a day that is not coming back. There is a boy in this yard whose wage is four marks a week and there are three weeks of it in March that are not paid, and I am not going to be the reason there are four. And there is a man in a slip at the pile whose boat was taken on Saturday the fourth in daylight over an entry, and if this yard is the first thing anybody in this harbor looks at when a man does not come back, then this yard has been the reason once already this month and it can be the reason once."

Nessa Pike entered all of it, and the checking, and the four seconds, in her own hand, and read it back, and Calder Marr read it back after her.

"Read the last of it back," she said.

He read it back, and the last of it was *and it can be the reason once*, and the shed was quiet, and Bevin Rook squared the book against the edge of the bench and then did the thing he did on the twenty-eighth of February and the nineteenth of January and the first of March, which was to say the thing nobody had asked for.

"Tarrant is not in our book," he said. "Not one line. He was never in it and he never hired us and we never hired him and there is no reason in any of our columns and there is no depth and there is no hour and there is nothing whatever about a man called Neave Tarrant in the book of this yard, and I have looked, and I have looked twice, and I am entering that I looked, and I am entering it because in about a year somebody is going to ask this yard what it knows about a man who went out in the blow on the twenty-eighth of February, and the true answer is nothing, and the true answer being nothing is a fact and it goes in with a date on it, and if it does not go in now then in a year this yard will not be able to say it, because a year is long enough for a man to convince himself that a book has a hole in it that somebody filled."

The entry went in.

What the harbor had actually done about it was on the wall by the second sheet, and the yard had read it that morning without a word. Nine boats of the herring fleet had their own crews in their own boats, two of them out on the Saturday and the Sunday and on the Monday after that, and four of the third lay's boats had doubled their hands out of the second and the south lay, and the tide gang had put a light on the mole head at dusk on the Saturday and taken it in on the Monday because a light on the mole head costs oil and the harbor authority's repair account had bought four sets of moorings and a season of warp and was empty, and there was no money in it for a light. That last fact was in a notice nailed to the tide-gang store door in Neale's own hand on Saturday night and it was the reason the third lay had gone dark on the Sunday night, and it was on that wall because a notice about a man who is out is a thing a fleet reads before it decides to look.

"And then the boy read the position of the day out, because that is the hour for it, and the bag had eight marks in it and the note stood at twenty-four marks and nineteen of that was the pump's iron and the tin had two crowns in it that had been in it since the first of February.

"And on the fourth of March," the boy said, after he had read the position, "a working pump belonging to a crew of this association went into a lighter on this dock at ten in the morning with a form and a signature line, and it was the only pump in this harbor a man could hire that was not the harbor authority's, and the men in it would have been on the water in a blow in the last fortnight of the herring season. I am not writing an opinion. I am writing that the fourth of March and the twenty-eighth of February are ten days apart and both of them are on this page and I would like a man reading this in a year to have to look at both of them at once, because I do not think either of them means anything on its own and I do think together they are the price of a piece of paper on the fish dock."

He shut the book.

That is the last time anybody in Morrow Quay could say anything at all about Neave Tarrant, and a man of the third lay with nine winters of his own has not been back.

---

And the last thing on that hill was not about the third lay, and Piet Marrow had heard it two days before in the market and had been carrying it up the hill since Wednesday and had only now found the moment for it.

"There is a man asking about the conservator," he said. "In the market, on Saturday, in the fish market, at the end of the wall. He had a slate and he was not a naval man and he was not from the court, and he asked two men whether the woman who wrote the fair copy of the sheet had lost her office in January and what month, and one of them told him, because it is on a wall that any man may read, and he wrote it down, and then he asked one of them whether she still came to the fish dock. I did not want to say it and I have been carrying it since Saturday, and I am saying it because you said my name is on a piece of paper and I do not think that is a thing you should hear about from a market."
