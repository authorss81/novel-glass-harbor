# Chapter 765

*Wednesday the Twenty-Fourth Day of August, the Fifth Day, the Four Parts of One Page Laid Out on the Flat of That Bench Stone From About Ten in the Morning and Every One of Them True and the Total at the Foot of It False, Line One Going onto That Fitting-Out Sheet With the Twenty-Sixth on Its Face for the Day It Settles and Not Settled Today, a Rota Day Tomorrow and No Charge on That Cistern Today, a Second Mark Not Going onto Any Page Today, the Bag at Twenty-Eight Pence, the First Column at Four Hundred and Ninety-Eight and the Second at Two Thousand Three Hundred and Sixty-Four*

Wednesday the twenty-fourth day of August, with that market open from about nine and the same dead air and the bar flat as the day before and warmer, a yard below that market with a shed standing in it, and about nine people in that shed at half past five that afternoon.

**The four parts of that leaf were laid out on the flat of that bench stone from about ten in the morning and every one of them is true, and the total at the foot of that leaf is not. Line one went onto that fitting-out sheet at about two, carrying two days on its face, being the twenty-fourth for the work and the twenty-sixth for the day the yard settles it, being one thousand one hundred and fifty-eight pence, being seven marks, three shillings and twopence, in five parts. Not a rota day and not a wage Tuesday and not a wipe, and no charge is put on that cistern, and a charge is put on it tomorrow and the day after. A hundred and sixty-eight pence came into that yard at about ten and a hundred and sixty-eight went out of it at about four, and under that slate is twenty-eight pence, being two shillings and fourpence, and no tin in that shed has been opened since the twenty-ninth of April past.**

**Nine counters were carried through this Wednesday. That notice of the seventeenth of May past is on its four hundred and sixty-third day. That bellwright of this harbor has now gone four hundred and sixty-five days with nobody asking him anything and his chest is shut and stays shut. Those two objects belonging to nobody on that crown are into the eight hundred and third day of themselves. That bench under that pump has now stood bare for the five hundred and sixty-ninth night running. Two lights about a mile off that point have gone three hundred and eighty-seventh days and no figure is set against them. That six-line sheet at the back of that end stall has reached the three hundred and forty-second day of its standing. That paper of four lines in Fish Street is on the three hundred and fifty-seventh day. That nine-line sheet with a mark on it in that front street has stood three hundred and seventy-fourth day. And a man of about fifty-one has kept four hundred and five days of saying nothing to anybody about a page of six names. Nobody stands over the third of those columns and a boy's hand of twenty-two is at the head of it, that boy's surname is on no page, and he is off the water. The fourth of them stands at one hundred and twenty-five, has taken no line, and is neither shut nor struck. About two sea miles off that mole head there are nine hulls this afternoon and nine lay there on the twenty-first of January, and this harbor has not said a word about any of them easing. A man of about twenty-nine hears out of the left side of his head and it is two hundred and sixty-three days today, one more than the day before, no figure is set against it, and he is not on the water.**

---

The four parts on that leaf are written in one hand and they are as follows, and they were read out loud in about nine people at about ten in the morning at the flat of that stone, and about five of those nine could not read them and heard them.

The first part is one day of a boat hired at the figure in chalk, being one hundred and sixty-eight pence, being one mark and eightpence. The second part is four men at two days at sixteen pence a man-day, being four times two times sixteen for one hundred and twenty-eight pence. The third part is coal for one day at twenty pence. The fourth part is thirty yards of line at three pence the yard, being ninety pence.

---

Every one of those four sentences is true and no person said a false thing on that leaf and no person has been asked to say one.

The boat was hired for one day at the start. Four men turned up for two days and were paid for two days. The coal was for one day. The line was bought in June at three pence the yard and thirty yards of it came out of a hundred and twenty.

---

The line is the part that will not sit still, and it is the smallest of the four, being ninety pence of three hundred and eighty-six, and a man of about thirty-one of the second lay said out loud that thirty yards bought in June for three crossings is ten yards a crossing and that a line is bought by the yard and not by the crossing.

And the man of about thirty-two said out loud that he would not go into that, because a line is bought by the yard and that is not what the page was asking.

---

The page was asking one thing and it asks it on its own face at the top, in three words, being what that crossing cost, and a person who is asked what a crossing cost is not asked what a yard bought in June.

The four parts are the cost of a crossing and the two days of men are in it because the men were on the crossing for two days, and the coal is in it because the coal was burnt on the crossing, and the line is in it because the line went out on the crossing, and the day of hire is in it because the boat was hired for the crossing, and the boat was hired on the first day and started again on the second morning and came in that afternoon.

---

At about half past eleven the man of about forty of the Ashtide Run said out loud the thing that the shed had not said, and he said it in front of about nine, and the words he used were that the page does not add up and does not say it should, and that the parts are right and the sum is a man deciding what a crossing is.

And then he said the other thing, which is that he has had a copy of that page since April and that the copy he has carries one day of hire and that he has been carrying one day of hire in his own head ever since, and that he did not know until the Monday that anybody else was carrying two.

---

The woman of thirty of no office did not answer that at about half past eleven and was not in that yard again until about two.

When she came she said one sentence out loud and went up the steps again, and the sentence was that if a page is a sum of things that are each true and the sum is not, then the page is not wrong, and the question on it is wrong, and that is a different fault and it has a different repair and nobody in that shed knows what the repair is yet.

---

At about two line one went onto that fitting-out sheet and the keeper read the two days against it out loud in about nine people before he wrote a single figure, and the two days are the twenty-fourth for the work and the twenty-sixth for the day the yard settles it, and the five parts are five hundred and four pence of three days of a boat hired at the figure in chalk, sixty pence of coal and oil for three days, two hundred and seventy pence of ninety yards of line at three pence the yard, thirty-six pence of two bell seats cut and faced at eighteen pence, and two hundred and eighty-eight pence of six men at three days at sixteen pence a man-day.

Five hundred and four plus sixty plus two hundred and seventy plus thirty-six plus two hundred and eighty-eight is one thousand one hundred and fifty-eight pence, being seven marks, three shillings and twopence.

---

Nobody put a second mark on any page on that Wednesday, and a man of about twenty-six of that market said out loud that he was not going to put his hand to a leaf until somebody knew what the leaf was for, and about four of that nine agreed with him.

The page of days carries no figure against this day and a day with no figure is not a zero and is not written as a zero.

---

About half past five on that Wednesday the day's whole was said out loud at that stone in the yard's own mouth, in about nine hundred and seventy-four words, to about nine people, and the four parts were read with their multiplications because about four of those nine can add a column in their heads and about five cannot.

"**The four parts on that leaf are written in one hand and were read out loud in about nine people at about ten in the morning at the flat of that stone. The first part is one day of a boat hired at the figure in chalk, one hundred and sixty-eight pence, one mark and eightpence. The second is four men at two days at sixteen pence a man-day, four times two times sixteen for one hundred and twenty-eight pence. The third is coal for one day at twenty pence. The fourth is thirty yards of line at three pence the yard, ninety pence. Every one of those four sentences is true and no person said a false thing on that leaf.**"

"**At about two line one went onto that fitting-out sheet and the keeper read the days against it out loud before he wrote a single figure. Three days of a boat hired at the figure in chalk is five hundred and four pence, coal and oil for three days is sixty pence, ninety yards of line at three pence the yard is two hundred and seventy pence, two bell seats cut and faced at eighteen pence is thirty-six pence, and six men at three days at sixteen pence a man-day is two hundred and eighty-eight pence, and five hundred and four plus sixty plus two hundred and seventy plus thirty-six plus two hundred and eighty-eight is one thousand one hundred and fifty-eight pence, being seven marks, three shillings and twopence, and the twenty-fourth and the twenty-sixth are both on the face of that line and the twenty-sixth is the day it settles.**"

"**And the rest of it. Nobody put a second mark on any page on this day and a man of about twenty-six of that market said out loud that he was not going to put his hand to a leaf until somebody knew what the leaf was for, and about four of that nine agreed with him, and the page of days carries no figure against this day and a day with no figure is not a zero. A hundred and sixty-eight pence came into this yard at about ten and a hundred and sixty-eight went out of it at about four, and under that slate is twenty-eight pence, being two shillings and fourpence. This yard is one hundred and seventy-eight days without a tin in it. Nothing has ever been entered in the third of those four columns and nobody has been appointed to it and a boy's hand of twenty-two is at the head of it, that boy's surname is on no page, and he is off the water, and the fourth of those four stands at one hundred and twenty-five and has taken no line. About two sea miles off that mole head there are nine hulls this evening and this harbor has not said a word about any of them easing. A man of about twenty-nine hears out of the left side of his head and it is two hundred and sixty-three days today, and the hearing he got back on the fourth of December is a different hearing from that one and is not set against it, and the hearing in the left side of the man of about thirty-two has not come back and is not priced.**"

Nobody was thanked for the four parts or for the line or for the reading, and that shed stood empty by about six with its door standing open on a slip nobody was on, and that page of that week had twenty-nine lines on it.

*Second column, not checkable from a mole, entered on the said Wednesday at about six in the evening in that shed, and no name is against it: that at about half past eleven the reason the man of about forty said the thing he said out loud in front of about nine is that a man holding a wrong figure for four months cannot put it down in a room with four people in it, and a yard is the cheapest place on this coast to put a figure down; that at about two the reason the woman of thirty of no office went up the steps again instead of arguing the four parts is that four parts can be argued for a day and a question cannot be argued at all, and a woman who keeps the record has to be the one who decides which of the two is being repaired; that at about two the reason the keeper read the days against the line out loud before he wrote a figure is that a line with one day on it is a figure a stranger cannot check and a line with two is a figure a stranger can, and this yard has a rule about that and does not make exceptions for its own sheet; and that at about four the reason a man of about twenty-six would not put a second mark on that leaf is that a mark is a promise that somebody else has looked at a thing, and a promise made before anybody knows what the thing is for is a promise about the man and not about the page.
