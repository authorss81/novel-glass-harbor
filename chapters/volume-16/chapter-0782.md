# Chapter 782

*Saturday the Tenth Day of September, the Twenty-Second Day, Line Five Settling into the Foot of That Fitting-Out Sheet at About Four With the Five Parts Read With Their Multiplications Before the Sum Went Down and the Foot Read After It at One Hundred and Twenty-Seven Thousand and Eighty-Two Pence Out and One Hundred and Sixteen Thousand Four Hundred and Forty-Two Pence Short, a Man of About Forty-Two of the Harbor Authority Asking Out Loud at About Half Past Four What That Figure Is For and the Keeper Answering Him Out Loud in About Nine People, the Bell of the Cinder Bell Out of Service on Its Third Day of Fourteen, the Bag at Twenty-Eight Pence, the First Column at Five Hundred and Fifteen and the Second at Two Thousand Four Hundred and Forty-Nine*

Saturday the tenth day of September, with that market shut until about six and a flat grey morning with no wind at all and the water inside that mole not moving, a yard below that market with a shed standing in it, and about nine people in that shed at half past five that afternoon.

**A settlement day and nothing else, and no charge goes on that cistern until the morning of Monday, no wipe comes before Sunday, and the next wage Tuesday is the thirteenth of September. Line five settled into the foot of that fitting-out sheet at about four and the five parts were read with their multiplications before the sum went down, and the foot of that sheet was read after that settlement and stands at one hundred and twenty-seven thousand and eighty-two pence out, being seven hundred and ninety-four marks, three shillings and sixpence, and one hundred and sixteen thousand four hundred and forty-two pence short, being seven hundred and twenty-seven marks, ten shillings and twopence, against ten thousand six hundred and forty pence in. A hundred and sixty-eight pence came into that yard at about ten and a hundred and sixty-eight went out of it at about four, and under that slate is twenty-eight pence, being two shillings and fourpence, and that shed is one hundred and ninety-fifth days without a tin opened in it, the last having been the twenty-ninth of April past.**

**Nine counters stand at the close of this twenty-second day. The notice of the seventeenth of May past has now reached its four hundred and eightieth day. The bellwright of this harbor is on the four hundred and eighty-second day of a chest nobody has opened, and it stays shut. Those two objects nobody owns on that crown are into the eight hundred and twentieth day of themselves. The bench under that pump has stood bare five hundred and eighty-six nights running. Two lights about a mile off that point are on their four hundred and fourth day and no figure of money is set against either. The six-line sheet at the back of that end stall is on the three hundred and fifty-ninth day. The paper of four lines in Fish Street is on the three hundred and seventy-fourth day. The nine-line sheet with a mark on it in that front street is on the three hundred and ninety-first day. And a man of about fifty-one is on the four hundred and twenty-second day of saying nothing whatever to anybody about a page of six names. Nothing has ever stood under the head of the middle of those four columns, which is a boy's hand of twenty-two, and nobody has ever been set over it, and that boy's surname is on no page of this harbor and he is off the water. The fourth of those four is at one hundred and twenty-five and has taken no line into it and has been neither shut nor struck. Nine hulls lie about two sea miles off that mole head where nine lay on the twenty-first of January and this harbor has said nothing about any of them easing. A man of about twenty-nine hears nothing out of the left side of his head and it is the two hundred and eightieth day of that, one more than yesterday, and no figure is set against it and he is not on the water.**

---

The yard did its ordinary work and the only thing in it that was not ordinary was the hour, because the hour had been named on a line since Thursday and the Saturday had been named with it twice, and about four people in that shed had told two other people outside it.

The bell of the Cinder Bell is on the third day of being out of service and out of service is the whole of it, and nobody in this harbor has rung it and nobody in this harbor has asked anybody else to do without it.

---

A man of about forty-nine of the west end came in at about eleven with the three sheets of his own still squared up in a bundle under his arm, and he put them on the flat of that bench stone and did not open them.

He said out loud that he had come back to see whether the figure at the foot of that money sheet had moved since yesterday, and that he had heard in the market that it does move, and that a figure which moves is a figure a yard can get behind.

The keeper read him the foot of that sheet as it stood before that day's settlement, being one hundred and twenty-six thousand three hundred and ninety-four pence out and one hundred and fifteen thousand seven hundred and fifty-four pence short, and the man of about forty-nine wrote both of them on the back of his own hand with a stub of pencil and put the sheet away again.

---

At about four line five went into the foot of that sheet and the five parts were read with their multiplications before the sum went down, and he read them into about nine people and read the last of the five twice, once into the shed and once out towards the step for the men that had come in off the market late.

Two days of a boat hired at the figure in chalk is two times one hundred and sixty-eight for three hundred and thirty-six pence. Coal and oil for two days is forty pence. Thirty yards of line at three pence the yard is thirty times three for ninety pence. A case and four nails at thirty pence is thirty pence. Six men at two days at sixteen pence a man-day is six times two times sixteen for one hundred and ninety-two pence.

And three hundred and thirty-six and forty and ninety and thirty and one hundred and ninety-two is six hundred and eighty-eight pence, being four marks and four shillings.

---

One hundred and twenty-six thousand three hundred and ninety-four and six hundred and eighty-eight is one hundred and twenty-seven thousand and eighty-two, and one hundred and twenty-seven thousand and eighty-two less ten thousand six hundred and forty is one hundred and sixteen thousand four hundred and forty-two, and the foot of that sheet was read after that settlement and not before it.

Seven hundred and ninety-four marks, three shillings and sixpence is one hundred and twenty-seven thousand and eighty-two pence, and seven hundred and twenty-seven marks, ten shillings and twopence is one hundred and sixteen thousand four hundred and forty-two pence, and both of those were read out loud and neither of them was read twice.

---

A man of about forty-two of the harbor authority stood at the back of that shed with his hat in his hand and asked out loud, at about half past four, what that figure was for.

He said that he was not asking about the five parts and was asking about the hundred and sixteen thousand four hundred and forty-two, and that a figure of that size standing at the foot of a sheet in a working harbor ought to be good for something, and that nobody had ever told him what.

The keeper put his pencil down on the ledge of that bench stone before he answered, and he answered in about nine people and not to the man of about forty-two alone.

---

He said out loud that a total is for deciding whether to do a thing again, and that a total which cannot be spent cannot decide anything, and that the figure at the foot of that sheet is a figure of what this yard has spent and has not had back, and that it is not in the slate and not in anybody's hand and cannot be laid on a coal merchant's counter, and that a figure a yard cannot spend is not a smaller figure than one it can but is the same figure with nothing on the far side of it.

Then he said out loud that he had read that foot after that settlement because a total read before the sum goes down is a number this yard can be asked about all afternoon and has not yet paid for anything, and that this coast has lost three arguments to that order of reading and has won none of them.

---

Nobody put a mark on the face of anything that day.

The man of about thirty-one of the second lay was at the end stall at about four and said out loud that a mark is a way of counting how many people have looked at a line, and that there was nothing in that shed worth looking at twice, and that the column of the second hand would carry nothing against this day and a day with no figure on it is not a zero and is nowhere on it.

About four of that nine said out loud that the money had moved that day by six hundred and eighty-eight pence and ought to have been marked somewhere, and the man of about thirty-one said out loud that a sheet carries its own sum and does not need a hand on it to say that it moved.

---

Nobody was thanked for the settlement or for the answer, and that shed stood empty by about six with its door standing open on a slip nobody was on, and that page of that week had thirty lines on it.

*First column, entered on Saturday the tenth day of September at about six in the evening in that shed: that the said day was a settlement day and was not a rota day and was not a wage Tuesday and was not a wipe; that no charge is put on that cistern until the morning of Monday and that no wipe comes before Sunday and that the next wage Tuesday is the thirteenth of September; that line five settled into the foot of that fitting-out sheet at about four, that the five parts were read with their multiplications before the sum went down, and that those five parts are two days of a boat hired at the figure in chalk at two times one hundred and sixty-eight for three hundred and thirty-six pence, coal and oil for two days at forty pence, thirty yards of line at three pence the yard at thirty times three for ninety pence, a case and four nails at thirty pence, and six men at two days at sixteen pence a man-day at six times two times sixteen for one hundred and ninety-two pence, the sum being six hundred and eighty-eight pence, being four marks and four shillings; that one hundred and twenty-six thousand three hundred and ninety-four and six hundred and eighty-eight is one hundred and twenty-seven thousand and eighty-two, that one hundred and twenty-seven thousand and eighty-two less ten thousand six hundred and forty is one hundred and sixteen thousand four hundred and forty-two, and that the foot of that sheet was read after that settlement and not before it; that the said foot is one hundred and twenty-seven thousand and eighty-two pence out, being seven hundred and ninety-four marks, three shillings and sixpence, and one hundred and sixteen thousand four hundred and forty-two pence short, being seven hundred and twenty-seven marks, ten shillings and twopence; that the bell of the Cinder Bell is on the third day of being out of service and is not rung; that a man of about forty-nine of the west end wrote the said foot as it stood before that settlement on the back of his own hand; and that no mark went onto the face of any page on the said day.*

*Second column, not checkable from a mole, entered on the said Saturday at about six in the evening in that shed, and no name is against it: that the reason that question was asked on the day the sheet moved and not on any of the nine days before is that on the other nine days the foot of that sheet was a number that had not changed, and a number nobody has looked at for a week is a number a man asks about when it moves and not before; that the reason the keeper put his pencil down before he answered is that a man of about forty-two of the harbor authority has the standing to take a sentence as an instruction and does not have the standing to take one as an opinion, and the difference between those two is the width of a pencil on a ledge; that the reason the last of those five parts was read twice and not the other four is that the other four are this yard's own figures and this yard knows them, and the six hundred and eighty-eight was not this yard's figure until Thursday and is now going to be asked about at every settle from this month on; that the reason no mark went onto the face of anything is that the column of the second hand counts marks, and a mark put on a page to say that the page's own sum moved would be a mark saying that a sheet is a person.*
