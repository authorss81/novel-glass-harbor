# Chapter 781

*Friday the Ninth Day of September, the Twenty-First Day, an Ordinary Working Day and Not a Rota Day and Not a Wage Tuesday and Not a Wipe, Line Five Standing on That Fitting-Out Sheet Since About Two on Thursday With the Tenth Day of September on Its Face for the Day It Settles and Not Yet in the Foot of It, Three Sheets of Three Seasons of What One Yard Has Bought Laid Flat on That Bench Stone at About Ten in the Morning and One Mark Going onto the Face of One of Them at About Two, the Bag at Twenty-Eight Pence, the First Column at Five Hundred and Fourteen and the Second at Two Thousand Four Hundred and Forty-Four*

Friday the ninth day of September, with that market open from about nine and a fine clear morning with the wind gone into the south-west and a flat sea inside that mole and no spray on anything, a yard below that market with a shed standing in it, and about nine people in that shed at half past five that afternoon.

**An ordinary working day, and no charge goes on that cistern until the morning of Monday, no wipe comes before Sunday, and the next wage Tuesday is the thirteenth of September. Line five has stood on that fitting-out sheet since about two on Thursday carrying the tenth day of September on its face as the day it settles, and it is not in the foot of that sheet tonight and is not to be in it until that Saturday. The foot of that sheet therefore stands where it stood on Thursday at one hundred and twenty-six thousand three hundred and ninety-four pence out, being seven hundred and eighty-nine marks, twelve shillings and tenpence, and one hundred and fifteen thousand seven hundred and fifty-four pence short, being seven hundred and twenty-three marks, six shillings and twopence, against ten thousand six hundred and forty pence in. A hundred and sixty-eight pence came into that yard at about ten and a hundred and sixty-eight went out of it at about four, and under that slate is twenty-eight pence, being two shillings and fourpence, and that shed is one hundred and ninety-fourth days without a tin opened in it, the last having been the twenty-ninth of April past.**

**Nine counters were carried to the close of this twenty-first day. That notice of the seventeenth of May past is on its four hundred and seventy-ninth day and nobody has taken it down. That bellwright of this harbor is on the four hundred and eighty-first day of not being asked anything at all, and the chest against his wall is shut and stays shut. Those two objects belonging to nobody on that crown are into the eight hundred and nineteenth day of themselves. That bench under that pump is on the five hundred and eighty-fifth night of standing bare. Two lights about a mile off that point have gone four hundred and third days and no figure of money stands against either of them. That six-line sheet at the back of that end stall has reached the three hundred and fifty-eighth day of its standing. That paper of four lines in Fish Street is on the three hundred and seventy-third day. That nine-line sheet with a mark on it in that front street has stood three hundred and ninetieth day. And a man of about fifty-one has now gone four hundred and twenty-first days without saying one word to anybody about a page of six names. Under the head of the middle of those four columns, which is a boy's hand of twenty-two, there has never been anything at all and nobody has ever been set over it, that boy's surname is on no page of this harbor, and he is off the water. The fourth of those four stands at one hundred and twenty-five and has taken no line into it and is neither shut nor struck. Nine hulls lie about two sea miles off that mole head where nine lay on the twenty-first of January, and this harbor has not said a word about any of them easing. A man of about twenty-nine hears nothing out of the left side of his head and that is the two hundred and seventy-ninth day of it, one more than the day before, and no figure is set against that figure and he is not on the water.**

---

The yard did its ordinary work with the door open on a morning that was good for it, and the work was line against a gauge and oil into a can and the cutting of a length of coil down to size, and no line went onto that fitting-out sheet because a line with an hour against it and no boat against it is not a line.

The Saturday on the face of that fifth line was named out loud twice before anything was written against it, once by the keeper at about nine and once by a man of about forty-nine of the west end at about half past nine, and the second naming was because the first one had been said to about four people and the second was said to about nine.

---

At about ten a man of about forty-nine of the west end put three sheets on the flat of that bench stone and squared them up against the edge of it with the heel of his hand.

They were his own sheets and not this yard's, and they were three seasons of what one yard had bought for its own use, and each of the three carried the same three headings at the top of it in his own hand, being coal, oil and line, and each of the three carried a figure against every one of those three headings, and the figures were his figures and had come off his own book.

He said out loud, to about nine, that he wanted to know whether to buy the winter's coal at that figure again, and that he had brought the three sheets to that stone because it was the only flat thing in this harbor with a square edge to it, and that he had not come to be told anything and had come to look at a page.

---

He had a page in his own coat and he took it out and put it beside the three sheets, and it was a copy of the page of the crossing with four lines on it and four figures under them and three words at the top of it.

He said out loud that the four figures on that page were true, and that he had had a copy of that page since April and had never once tried to make them into anything, and that they would not make themselves into anything this morning either, and that a yard which buys coal three seasons running cannot tell from that page whether it was charged for one crossing or for two, because that page is not asking the question a yard asks.

A man of about thirty-one of the second lay said out loud that the page was copied off a copy and that a copy of a copy is a page that has had its middle taken out of it.

---

The man of about forty-nine said out loud that he had come three seasons wanting that page to be a number he could spend and that it had never once been a number he could spend, and that on the twenty-ninth day of August there went up at that end stall a sheet saying that the number on it was wrong, and that since that day he had been carrying that sheet in his coat as well and had got no further with it than he had been in June.

He said out loud that a figure of money is worth what it can be spent against, and that this harbor has three seasons of coal on a stone and one page that cannot be spent against any of them, and that a total nobody can spend is not a smaller thing than a total somebody can. It is the same thing with nothing on the far side of it.

---

The keeper read the four lines of that copy out loud at the flat of that stone, the four figures first and then the three words at the top of it, and he read the three words last and slowly, and about four of that nine could not read them and heard them.

The four figures are one day of a boat hired at the figure in chalk, being one hundred and sixty-eight pence, being one mark and eightpence; four men at two days at sixteen pence a man-day, being four times two times sixteen for one hundred and twenty-eight pence, being ten shillings and eightpence; coal for one day at twenty pence, being one shilling and eightpence; and thirty yards of line at three pence the yard, being ninety pence, being seven shillings and sixpence.

The three words at the top of that leaf ask what that crossing cost.

---

A man of about thirty-four of the second lay was in that yard from about ten and stood against the wall by the door all morning and was not asked anything by anybody, before the reading of those four figures or after it.

A man of about thirty-eight of that landing was not in that yard today and had gone up that coast road two days before, and nobody said anything about where he was.

---

At about two a man of about thirty-one of the second lay put one mark on the face of the middle sheet of those three, against the line heading and not against the coal and not against the oil, and he gave his reason out loud.

He said that a line is bought by the yard and has been bought by the yard on every coast on this inlet for two hundred years, and that a figure for line against which no yard can be said to have bought anything is a figure that will be argued about in a month and settled in none, and that he would rather it had one mark on it now with a reason under it than nine marks on it in six weeks with none.

One mark is one hand and not the hand that wrote it, and it is not an approval and it is not a signature and it is not a person, and the page of days carries one against this day.

---

About half past three the keeper went up to the room over that chandler's store with about six people and stood in front of the wall of it for the length of a cigarette.

The wall is bare and has been bare since the fourteenth of August, and the four nails in it have gone in and out of their holes a little in the draught off that visitor's door, and the board that hangs beside that wall has been standing empty from the same hour since the same day.

Nothing went up on either of them and nothing was said about either of them, and the wall and that board are on no page of this harbor.

---

A woman of about thirty-four of the Saltmarch sent word from that place on the Friday morning that the bell is going to be seated on the Wednesday whatever is on that leaf, and that the days the men are off the water are coming out of the same page as before, and that her community is not asking this yard for anything.

The keeper read that word out and did not answer it, and about four of that nine said out loud that a place which is not asking for anything can still be waiting for an answer, and about four said nothing at all.

---

*Second column, not checkable from a mole, entered on the said Friday at about six in the evening in that shed, and no name is against it: that the reason those three sheets were laid on that stone and not carried to that record room is that a man who wants to know what to buy next winter does not need a record room and needs a square edge, and this yard has one square edge in it and it is the flat of a bench stone, and every figure that has ever been settled in that harbor began on it; that the reason the Saturday was named out loud a second time at about half past nine is that the first naming at about nine was said to about four people and a thing said to four people in a yard of nine is a thing said to nobody and remembered by four; that the reason the three words at the top of that leaf were read last is that if they are read first the four figures under them stop being figures and start being arguments, and a man of about forty-nine has had three seasons of not being able to tell the difference between the two; that the reason nobody in that yard answered the word from the Saltmarch is that an answer given this week would be an answer about a figure nobody in that shed has, and this harbor does not put a guess on a place's page because the place has been patient.*
