# Chapter 801

*Thursday the Twenty-Ninth Day of September, the Forty-First Day, a Rota Day and the Hundred and Eighty-Eighth Time, That Cistern Charged at About Nine in Fourteen Trips by Hand and Walked on by None of It, the Figure Written Against That Slate at About One and Not Read Out, a Rule About What a Correction Costs Drafted in Chalk on the Flat of That Bench Stone at About Three and Made Nowhere and Going Nowhere, the Next Wipe on the Morning of Sunday and the Next Wage Tuesday on the Fourth of October, the Bag at Twenty-Eight Pence, the First Column at Five Hundred and Thirty-Four and the Second at Two Thousand Five Hundred and Forty-Four*

Thursday the twenty-ninth day of September, with that market open from six and the wind gone out of the north in the night and a flat sea at eight with the light coming off the water in the east, a yard below that market with a shed standing in it, and about nine people standing in that shed at half past five that afternoon.

**A rota day and the hundred and eighty-eighth time. That cistern was charged at about nine and it took fourteen of them and fourteen trips by hand, and not one of the fourteen put a foot on it. The figure went against that rota slate at about one and was not read out, and there is no charge on that cistern tomorrow. This is not a wipe and not a wage Tuesday. Nothing settled into the foot of that fitting-out sheet today, and no line was worked on it and none is owed to it, that foot standing at one hundred and twenty-seven thousand and eighty-two pence out, being seven hundred and ninety-four marks, three shillings and sixpence, and one hundred and sixteen thousand four hundred and forty-two pence short, being seven hundred and twenty-seven marks, ten shillings and twopence, against ten thousand six hundred and forty pence in and nothing else in it in fifty days. A hundred and sixty-eight pence came into that yard at about ten on a Thursday as on any other day and a hundred and sixty-eight went out of it at about four, and under that slate is twenty-eight pence, being two shillings and fourpence, and that shed is two hundred and fourteenth days without a tin opened in it, the last having been the twenty-ninth of April past.**

**Nine counters were carried to the close of this forty-first day. The notice of the seventeenth of May past is on its four hundred and ninety-ninth day. That bellwright of this harbor is on the five hundred and first day of nobody asking him anything, and the chest against his wall stays shut. Those two objects belonging to nobody on that crown are into the eight hundred and thirty-ninth day of themselves. That bench under that pump has stood bare six hundred and fifth night running. Two lights about a mile off that point have gone four hundred and twenty-third days, and no figure of money stands against either of them. The six-line sheet at the back of that end stall is on the three hundred and seventy-eighth day of its standing. The paper of four lines in Fish Street is on the three hundred and ninety-third day. The nine-line sheet with a mark on it in that front street has stood four hundred and tenth day. A man of about fifty-one of that market is on the four hundred and forty-first day of saying nothing to anybody about a page of six names, and that page is still where it is. A boy's hand of twenty-two heads the middle of those four columns and there is nothing under it, and that boy's surname is on no page and he is off the water for the seventh time. The fourth of those four stands at one hundred and twenty-five and has taken no line since this run of days began, and it is neither shut nor struck. Nine hulls lie about two sea miles off that mole head where nine lay on the twenty-first of January, and this harbor has said nothing about any of them and says nothing today. It is the two hundred and ninety-ninth day, one more than the day before, that a man of about twenty-nine has heard nothing out of the left side of his head, no figure is set against that figure, and he is not on the water.**

---

The butt at the back of that market was charged from the standpipe at nine and it took fourteen men and fourteen trips to fill it, and not one of the fourteen stood on it. By about one there was a skin on the water in it and the butt was short, and nobody drank out of it and nobody said it was bad. Two men chained the cover over before the light went and neither of those two had to be there, and they have never said why they were, and the water stood up the sides of the butt and stayed in it.

The ordinary work of that Thursday was four boats off that mole before noon and a bell seat cut and faced against a board for a crossing that goes on in the morning, and no figure of money stands against either half of that seat, because a line wants an hour and a boat and that seat has neither of them yet. The seat and the board are lying on the bench stone under an oiled cloth and they go up at six in the morning in a cart.

---

At about three the keeper took a piece of chalk and wrote on the flat of that bench stone, and about four people stood round it and looked at it upside down, and one of them said the words out loud as he wrote them so that the four of them would know what the words were before they dried. The draft is five lines and not six, and it says what a correction costs, who may refuse one, and what has to be written down when one is refused, and the last of the five lines is the one that says it was made under pressure by people with no stake in it and can be repaired by anybody who has to work under it.

That draft was in chalk on the flat of that stone and it came off that stone before that yard was locked, and it is not a page and it is not on one. The keeper said out loud to about four that a rule is not made by writing it out twice, that what he had done at three o'clock was a thing he could wipe off with his sleeve, and that he would say it again in front of about nine on the Friday if about nine of them wanted to hear it and if about nine of them wanted a word on it.

He said out loud why it was being made at all, and nobody added to what he said. A correction put up in public costs the two places that spent on the figure being corrected, the method has a rule for refusing a record and no rule at all for refusing a correction, and on the twenty-eighth of August a woman kept a page of her own place and put a refusal on that end stall and had the standing to make it, and there was nothing in this harbor to write such a thing on. That is what he said, and a form is not a rule, and this yard is not the office that makes rules.

The man of about thirty-two was in that shed from about ten until about one with the page of routes in his hand and the tally slate in front of him, and the woman of thirty of no office was not in that yard and was two days up that coast road. The four new crews were on their eleventh day and did the morning's work in about two hours under the man of about twenty-seven of the second lay, and the man of about thirty-two was handed the book of hours with them at about ten and wrote nothing at all in it for that hour and a half.

A man of about thirty-four of the second lay was on that bench from about ten until about two and was not asked anything by anybody, and it is the fifty-first day of this run of days on which that has happened, that run having begun on Wednesday the tenth day of August, ten days before this run of fifty days began.

---

The correction and the refusal were standing at that end stall on their thirty-second day at the same size and a hand's breadth apart with the wrong figure left showing underneath the correction and not struck out, and the leaf from the Cinder Bell was on its twenty-seventh day a step to the left of that refusal, and the sheet with the fifteen gaps stood at that end stall at fifteen with nothing on it moved. The wall of the room over that chandler's store is bare and has stood bare since the fourteenth of August, and nothing went up on it today and nothing came down off it, and the board beside that wall, which a person would have to be told about, is on no page of this harbor.

About half past five on that Thursday the day's whole was said out loud at that stone standing up, in about one thousand and thirty-four words, and the butt came first in it and the chalk came in the middle of it, and nothing that is a page came in it at all.

"**The two sheets were standing at that end stall on their thirty-second day and nothing went up on any wall in that harbor and nothing came down off one, and that foot was not written into today and stands at one hundred and twenty-seven thousand and eighty-two pence out and one hundred and sixteen thousand four hundred and forty-two pence short, against ten thousand six hundred and forty pence in. A hundred and sixty-eight pence came into this yard at about ten and a hundred and sixty-eight went out of it at about four, and twenty-eight plus eight thousand four hundred less eight thousand four hundred is twenty-eight, and the twenty-eight is still under that slate, being two shillings and fourpence, which is six hundred and twelve pence short of the wage of four marks, being three marks eleven shillings. This yard has gone two hundred and fourteenth days without a tin in it. A boy's hand of twenty-two heads the middle of those four columns and there is nothing under it, that boy's surname is on no page, he is off the water for the seventh time, and the fourth of those four is at one hundred and twenty-five with no line entered into it and is neither shut nor struck. About two sea miles off that mole head there are nine hulls lying where nine lay on the twenty-first of January, and nobody on this coast has ever said a word about any of them easing. It is the two hundred and ninety-ninth day, one more than the day before, that a man of about twenty-nine has heard nothing out of the left side of his head, no figure is set against that figure, he is not on the water, and the hearing in the left side of the man of about thirty-two has not come back and is not priced.**"

Nobody was thanked for the cistern or for the four lines of chalk, and that shed stood empty by about six with its door standing open on a slip nobody was on, and that page of that week had twenty-nine lines on it.

**A ROTA DAY AND THE HUNDRED AND EIGHTY-EIGHTH CHARGE WENT ONTO THAT CISTERN THIS MORNING AND WAS WALKED ON BY NONE OF IT. A RULE ABOUT WHAT A CORRECTION COSTS WAS DRAFTED IN CHALK ON THAT BENCH STONE AT ABOUT THREE AND WAS NOT MADE IN THIS SHED AND WENT NOWHERE. NO WALL IN THAT HARBOR TOOK ANYTHING AND GAVE ANYTHING BACK. THE BAG HOLDS TWENTY-EIGHT PENCE, BEING TWO SHILLINGS AND FOURPENCE, AND THIS YARD IS TWO HUNDRED AND FOURTEENTH DAYS WITHOUT A TIN IN IT.**

*First column, entered on Thursday the twenty-ninth day of September at about six in the evening in that shed: that the said day was a rota day, being the hundred and eighty-eighth charge on that cistern, charged at about nine in fourteen trips by hand, walked on by none of them, the figure written against that rota slate at about one and not read out, and that there is no charge on that cistern on the Friday; that the said day was not a wipe and was not a wage Tuesday, and that the next wipe is on the morning of Sunday the second of October and the next wage Tuesday is on the fourth of October; that nothing settled into the foot of that fitting-out sheet on the said day and that no line was worked on it, that foot standing at one hundred and twenty-seven thousand and eighty-two pence out, being seven hundred and ninety-four marks, three shillings and sixpence, and one hundred and sixteen thousand four hundred and forty-two pence short, being seven hundred and twenty-seven marks, ten shillings and twopence, against ten thousand six hundred and forty pence in and nothing else in it in fifty days; that the ordinary work of the said day was four boats off that mole before noon and a bell seat cut and faced against a board for a crossing that goes on in the morning, and that no figure of money stands against either half of that seat, a line wanting an hour and a boat and that seat having neither yet; that at about three the keeper took a piece of chalk and wrote on the flat of that bench stone with about four people standing round it and one of them saying the words out loud as he wrote them, that the draft is five lines, that it says what a correction costs and who may refuse one and what has to be written down when one is refused, and that the last of the five lines says the rule was made under pressure by people with no stake in it and can be repaired by anybody who has to work under it; *Second column, not checkable from a mole, entered on the said Thursday at about six in the evening in that shed, and no name is against it: that the reason the rule was put in chalk on a stone rather than written out fair is that a draft a person can wipe off with his sleeve is a draft, and a draft made in front of four people is worth exactly as much as the four of them will say in front of nine tomorrow; that the reason the fifth line of it says it was made under pressure by people with no stake in it is that every person who stood round that stone on that Thursday is paid out of a page that did not spend on the figure being corrected, and that a rule written by people who spend nothing on it has to carry that on its own face or it is a rule with a hidden hand in it; that the reason the keeper did not read the chalk out again after he finished writing it is that a thing said once by the man who wrote it is a thing about nine people can argue with, and a thing said twice is a thing about nine people have to listen to; that the reason the bell seat cut and faced on that Thursday carries no figure of money against it is that a line wants an hour and a boat, and that seat had a Thursday and no boat in it yet, and putting a figure on it would be a figure for a piece of work whose cost nobody in this yard has paid.*
