# Chapter 778

*Tuesday the Sixth Day of September, the Eighteenth Day, the Ninety-Seventh Wage Tuesday and the Second of the Seven in This Run of Days, the Column on the Lid of That Step Read out at About Ten in Four Minutes and Standing at Twenty-Six Lines and One Hundred and Four Marks and Not Moved, the Chest With the Twenty Envelopes in It Shut from Ten until Four, and a Further Week Standing Fallen Due at the Close of It, Line Four Going onto That Fitting-Out Sheet at About Two With the Tuesday on Its Face and the Thursday on Its Face, an Ordinary Working Day Otherwise and Not a Rota Day and Not a Wipe, the Bag at Twenty-Eight Pence, the First Column at Five Hundred and Eleven and the Second at Two Thousand Four Hundred and Twenty-Nine*

Tuesday the sixth day of September, with that market open from about nine and a hot still morning with no wind at all in the harbor and the bar flat as it was on the twenty-third of August, a yard below that market with a shed standing in it, and about nine people in that shed at half past five that afternoon.

**The ninety-seventh wage Tuesday and the second of the seven in this run of days. The column on the lid of that step was read out at about ten in four minutes by a man of about thirty-one of the second lay and stands at twenty-six lines and one hundred and four marks, being sixteen thousand six hundred and forty pence, and it does not move, and the chest with the twenty envelopes in it was shut from ten until four, and a further week stands fallen due at the close of it. Not a rota day and not a wipe. Line four went onto that fitting-out sheet at about two with the Tuesday on its face as the day the work was done and the Thursday on its face as the day it settles, those two days being two days apart, and the five parts were read with their multiplications before any figure was written down. Nothing settles into the foot of that sheet today, so the foot of it stands at one hundred and twenty-five thousand five hundred and fifty-four pence out, being seven hundred and eighty-four marks, nine shillings and sixpence, and one hundred and fourteen thousand nine hundred and fourteen pence short, being seven hundred and eighteen marks, two shillings and tenpence, against ten thousand six hundred and forty pence in. There is a hundred and sixty-eight pence going into that yard at about ten and a hundred and sixty-eight coming out of it at about four, and under that slate is twenty-eight pence, being two shillings and fourpence, and that tin was not opened on the twenty-ninth of April or on any day since it.**

**Nine counters were carried to the close of this eighteenth day. That notice of the seventeenth of May past is on its four hundred and seventy-sixth day. That bellwright of this harbor has now gone four hundred and seventy-eighth days with nobody asking him anything and his chest stays shut. Those two objects belonging to nobody on that crown are into the eight hundred and sixteenth day of themselves. That bench under that pump has now stood bare for the five hundred and eighty-second night running. Two lights about a mile off that point have gone four hundred days and no figure is set against them. That six-line sheet at the back of that end stall has reached the three hundred and fifty-fifth day of its standing. That paper of four lines in Fish Street is on the three hundred and seventieth day. That nine-line sheet with a mark on it in that front street has stood three hundred and eighty-seventh day. A man of about fifty-one has kept four hundred and eighteenth days of not saying anything to anybody about a page of six names. There is a boy's hand of twenty-two at the head of the middle of those four columns, nothing under it, nobody over it, his surname on no page, and he is off the water. At one hundred and twenty-five the fourth of those four stands as it stood a week ago, with nothing entered and neither shut nor struck. Nine hulls lie about two sea miles off that mole head and nine lay there on the twenty-first of January, and this harbor has not said a word about any of them easing. Two hundred and seventy-six days stand between a man of about twenty-nine and any sound out of the left side of his head, one more than the day before, and nothing is set against that and he is not on the water.**

---

The column on the lid of that step was read out at about ten in four minutes and it is twenty-six lines and one hundred and four marks, being sixteen thousand six hundred and forty pence, and it did not move.

The man of about thirty-one of the second lay said the two figures out loud at the end of it the way he says them every Tuesday, and nobody wrote them down in that shed.

About five of the people who came in that morning could not read the figure on the lid of that step and did not know what was being read.

---

The chest was shut from ten until four and it is a long flat thing against the wall of that office with twenty envelopes in it, and about two people came to the door of that office in that hour and one of the two went away again.

A further week stands fallen due at the close of that Tuesday and would fall due again on the thirteenth, and the column did not move and nothing was paid and nothing was advanced and nothing was cut back.

---

At about eleven a woman of about twenty-seven of that market asked out loud, in about four, whether a bell on the Cinder Bell stretch being let go for a fortnight means the Ashtide Run has no signal on that water for a fortnight.

The woman of about fifty-two said out loud that it does, and that the Ashtide Run's own page carries whatever the people of that route do about a fortnight with no bell on it, and that this yard's page is not that page and that nobody in this yard will write on it what that route does with its own fortnight.

---

At about two the keeper wrote the fourth line onto that fitting-out sheet with the Tuesday as the day the work was done and the Thursday as the day it settles, and he said both of those days out loud in about nine people before he wrote any figure.

Three days of a boat hired at the figure in chalk is three times one hundred and sixty-eight for five hundred and four pence. Coal and oil for three days is sixty pence. Four panes of sheet glass at twelve pence a pane is four times twelve for forty-eight pence. Two bell seats cut and faced at eighteen pence is two times eighteen for thirty-six pence. Four men at three days at sixteen pence a man-day is four times three times sixteen for one hundred and ninety-two pence.

Five hundred and four and sixty and forty-eight and thirty-six and one hundred and ninety-two is eight hundred and forty pence, being five marks, three shillings and fourpence, and that sum goes down into the foot of that sheet on the Thursday and the foot is read after it.

---

The woman of about fifty-two packed her bag at about half past three and folded that leaf at its own fold and set it back on the board at the end stall at the same size and at the same height it had stood at since Saturday, and no other person touched it that afternoon, and she said out loud that she goes in the morning and that she has been four days down and will be four days up and that is eight days of road for a fortnight of a bell.

Nobody offered her a bed for the last night and nobody asked her to stay, and she had said on Saturday that she would not.

---

Nothing went onto the face of anything in that yard today, and that page of days carries no figure against this day in the second-hand column, and a day with no figure in it is not a zero and is nowhere on it.

At the end stall the correction and the refusal were standing where they were put on Monday, on their ninth day, and the leaf from the Cinder Bell was beside them at the same size and the same height on its fourth day, and the wrong figure was still showing underneath the correction, and that shed stood empty by about six and that page of that week had thirty lines on it.

---

About half past five on that Tuesday the column on the lid of that step was not read out again in that yard at half past five, being read out at ten, and the day was said at that stone in about nine hundred and one words, and the line went in at about two and the settlement came last and was left to Thursday.

Nothing settles into the foot of that fitting-out sheet until the Thursday, and that foot stands at one hundred and twenty-five thousand five hundred and fifty-four pence out and one hundred and fourteen thousand nine hundred and fourteen pence short, with ten thousand six hundred and forty pence in against it. There is a hundred and sixty-eight pence going into this yard at about ten and a hundred and sixty-eight coming out of it at about four, and twenty-eight plus eight thousand four hundred less eight thousand four hundred is twenty-eight. Twenty-eight pence, being two shillings and fourpence, is what is under that slate, and it is six hundred and twelve pence short of the wage of four marks, being three marks eleven shillings. That shed has now been one hundred and ninety-first days without a tin in it. The third of those four columns carries nothing under it and nobody over it, a boy's hand of twenty-two at its head, his surname on no page, and he is off the water, and at one hundred and twenty-five the fourth of those four stands as it stood a week ago, with nothing entered and neither shut nor struck. About two sea miles off that mole head there are nine hulls this evening and nine were lying there on the twenty-first of January, and no claim about their easing stands in this harbor. Two hundred and seventy-six days stand between a man of about twenty-nine and any sound out of the left side of his head, one more than the day before, and nothing is set against that and he is not on the water. And what he got back on the fourth of December is a different hearing from that one and is not set against it, and the hearing in the left side of the man of about thirty-two has not come back and is not priced.**"

Nobody was thanked for the column or for the chest or for the line, and that shed stood empty by about six with its door standing open on a slip nobody was on.

**THE NINETY-SIXTH WAGE TUESDAY CAME AND WENT WITH TWENTY-SIX LINES AND ONE HUNDRED AND FOUR MARKS, BEING SIXTEEN THOUSAND SIX HUNDRED AND FORTY PENCE, UNMOVED ON THE LID OF THAT STEP, THE CHEST SHUT FROM TEN UNTIL FOUR, AND A FURTHER WEEK FALLEN DUE AT ITS CLOSE. LINE FOUR WENT ONTO THAT FITTING-OUT SHEET AT ABOUT TWO WITH THE TUESDAY AND THE THURSDAY ON ITS FACE AND FIVE PARTS READ WITH THEIR MULTIPLICATIONS, BEING EIGHT HUNDRED AND FORTY PENCE, BEING FIVE MARKS, THREE SHILLINGS AND FOURPENCE, AND IT SETTLES ON THE THURSDAY. NOTHING WENT ONTO THE FACE OF ANYTHING IN THAT YARD TODAY. THE BAG HOLDS TWENTY-EIGHT PENCE, BEING TWO SHILLINGS AND FOURPENCE.**

*First column, entered on Tuesday the sixth day of September at about six in the evening in that shed: that the said day was the ninety-seventh wage Tuesday and the second of the seven in this run of days; that the column on the lid of that step was read out at about ten in four minutes by a man of about thirty-one of the second lay and stands at twenty-six lines and one hundred and four marks, being sixteen thousand six hundred and forty pence, and does not move; that about five of the people who came in that morning could not read the figure on the lid of that step and did not know what was being read; that the chest with the twenty envelopes in it was shut from ten until four, and that about two people came to the door of that office in that hour and one of the two went away again; that a further week stands fallen due at the close of the said day and would fall due again on the thirteenth, and that the column did not move and nothing was paid and nothing was advanced and nothing was cut back; that at about eleven a woman of about twenty-seven of that market asked out loud, in about four, whether a bell on the Cinder Bell stretch being let go for a fortnight means the Ashtide Run has no signal on that water for a fortnight; that the woman of about fifty-two said out loud that it does, and that the Ashtide Run's own page carries whatever the people of that route do about a fortnight with no bell on it, and that this yard's page is not that page; that at about two the keeper wrote the fourth line onto that fitting-out sheet with the Tuesday as the day the work was done and the Thursday as the day it settles, those two days being two days apart, and said both of those days out loud in about nine people before he wrote any figure; that the five parts of that line are three days of a boat hired at the figure in chalk at three times one hundred and sixty-eight for five hundred and four pence, coal and oil for three days at sixty pence, four panes of sheet glass at forty-eight pence, two bell seats cut and faced at eighteen pence at two times eighteen for thirty-six pence, and four men at three days at sixteen pence a man-day at four times three times sixteen for one hundred and ninety-two pence, the sum being eight hundred and forty pence, being five marks, three shillings and fourpence, which goes down into the foot of that sheet on the Thursday and that foot is read after it; and that the woman of about fifty-two packed her bag at about half past three and set that leaf back against that board at the height it had stood at since the Saturday and folded it at the fold before she left it there, and that nobody offered her a bed for the last night and nobody asked her to stay, and that nothing went onto the face of anything in that yard on the said day and that page of days carries no figure against it in the second-hand column, a day with no figure in it being not a zero and nowhere on it.*

*Second column, not checkable from a mole, entered on the said Tuesday at about six in the evening in that shed, and no name is against it: that at about ten the reason those two figures were said out loud at the end of the reading and not written down is that a column read aloud is a column four men can hold against it on Thursday and a column written down is a column four men will not admit they saw, and this harbor has needed the first of those for eleven weeks; that the reason she folded that leaf herself and no other person touched it is that a page which has been in a bag for four days is a page whose fold is now part of what it says, and a person who opens a page for somebody else is a person deciding where the fold goes; that at about eleven the reason nobody in that yard answered the question about the signal is that the answer is written in a book four days up that coast road which belongs to about forty people and not to this yard, and a yard that answers for a book it has never opened is a yard that will be found out within a month; and that at about two the reason the two days went onto that line before the five parts is that the parts of a line can be checked by anybody with a slate and the days of a line can only be checked by somebody who was there, and this yard is short of the second kind and has never been short of the first.*
