# Chapter 772

*Wednesday the Thirty-First Day of August, the Twelfth Day, the Fourth Sheet of That Copy of a Schedule Read Out Once at About Three in the Afternoon in the Yard's Own Mouth to About Twenty People of Whom About Eleven Cannot Read It and Not Read Again Anywhere Afterwards, Two Hundred and Eleven Entries in Two Columns and No Name Against Any of Them and One of Those Lines Saying That a Bell the People of the Cinder Bell Gave to the Ashtide Run for Its Use in April Is Unclaimed, an Ordinary Working Day and Not a Rota Day and Not a Wage Tuesday and Not a Wipe, the Bag at Twenty-Eight Pence, the First Column at Five Hundred and Five and the Second at Two Thousand Three Hundred and Ninety-Nine*

Wednesday the thirty-first day of August, with that market open from about nine and a clear morning with the haze gone off the water by about seven and the wind still in the south-west and no cloud on the point, a yard below that market with a shed standing in it, and about nine people in that shed at half past five that afternoon.

**The fourth sheet of that copy was read out once this afternoon at about three and is not read out again anywhere afterwards, and it carries two hundred and eleven entries in two columns and there is no name against any one of the two hundred and eleven. Nothing is put on that cistern before Thursday, no wipe comes before Sunday, and the next wage Tuesday is the sixth of September. No line went onto that fitting-out sheet today and none settles before Friday, so the foot of that sheet stands at one hundred and twenty-four thousand eight hundred and twelve pence out, being seven hundred and eighty marks and one shilling, and one hundred and fourteen thousand one hundred and seventy-two pence short, being seven hundred and thirteen marks, seven shillings and eightpence, against ten thousand six hundred and forty pence in. There is a hundred and sixty-eight pence coming into that yard at about ten and a hundred and sixty-eight going out of it at about four, and under that slate is twenty-eight pence, being two shillings and fourpence, and that tin has not been opened since the twenty-ninth of April past.**

**Nine counters were carried to the close of this twelfth day. That notice of the seventeenth of May past is on its four hundred and seventieth day. That bellwright of this harbor has now gone four hundred and seventy-second days with nobody asking him anything and his chest stays shut. Those two objects belonging to nobody on that crown are into the eight hundred and tenth day of themselves. That bench under that pump has now stood bare for the five hundred and seventy-sixth night running. Two lights about a mile off that point have gone three hundred and ninety-fourth days and no figure is set against them. That six-line sheet at the back of that end stall has reached the three hundred and forty-ninth day of its standing. That paper of four lines in Fish Street is on the three hundred and sixty-fourth day. That nine-line sheet with a mark on it in that front street has stood three hundred and eighty-first day. A man of about fifty-one has now gone four hundred and twelfth days without saying a word to anybody about a page carrying six names. No person has ever been named over the middle of those four columns. At the head of that one is a boy's hand of twenty-two whose surname appears on no page, and he is off the water. The fourth of those four has held at one hundred and twenty-five since the spring, has taken no line into it, and is neither shut nor struck. Nine hulls lie about two sea miles off that mole head and nine lay there on the twenty-first of January, and this harbor has not said a word about any of them easing. The left side of the head of a man of about twenty-nine has been silent two hundred and seventy days this evening, a day further than yesterday, and no figure is set against that figure and he is not on the water.**

---
The reading was at about three in the yard and the keeper did it standing at the flat of that bench stone with the fourth sheet in his own hand and about twenty people standing in that yard and on that step, and it took about eleven minutes, and it was not read out a second time anywhere afterwards. He read it down the first column and not across the two of them, and he did not hurry the names of the months, and twice he stopped and began the same line again because a man at the back had not got the first one.

About eleven of that twenty could not read the sheet. They heard it, and four of those eleven wrote nothing and seven of them marked the time on a cuff.

The man of about thirty-four of the second lay was in that yard at about ten and was standing at the back of it at three and was not asked anything by anybody, before the reading or after it, and nobody said out loud that he was not to be asked anything. He stood where he could hear the voice and not the paper, and he kept his hands still at his sides the whole of the eleven minutes.

---

The list is in two columns. The first column gives a place and the second gives a thing, and against each of the two hundred and eleven there is a month and a year of the giving, and there is no name, no hand, no seal and no person of any kind against any of them.

The head of the sheet says what the two columns are. The first is what is unclaimed and the second is what is sealed, and the count of the first is two hundred and nine and the count of the second is two, and the two add up to the two hundred and eleven and the sheet prints the addition.

---

The woman of thirty of no office took that fourth sheet at about half past three and read down the first column with her finger going down and about four of the twenty standing close enough to see which line she was on, and at about four she put her finger on one line and read that one out loud herself, in the same words and the same order the keeper had used. She read it in the flat voice of a woman reading a price list, and she gave no name to it and no face, and she stopped her finger on the line and left it there.

The line is the Cinder Bell, and a bell, and the words given by the people of that place to the Ashtide Run for its use, and the month of April, and unclaimed.

Against that line on the sheet there is nothing at all. There is no hand, no seal, no person and no note of any kind, and the sheet says in three words at the foot of the column that an entry stands unclaimed where no claim has been entered against it.

---

Nobody in that yard asked why nobody had entered a claim against it, because the sheet says at the foot of the column that a claim is entered by the person who makes it, and a person who has never been asked for a claim does not make one.

About four of that twenty said out loud, to one another and not to anybody, that a man who gives a bell away for nothing is not a person who has been asked for a claim. About four said nothing, and the four who said nothing were the four who could read, and one of the four who could read put a thumb on the edge of the step and left it there.

"Read me the one about the bell," said a man of about thirty-four of that market to the woman of thirty of no office, in about four, at the side of that yard and not in front of it.

She read him that line twice and the second time she read it slower. "That is all of it that is printed," she said. "There is nothing under it and nothing beside it, and a man who has never been asked for a claim does not enter one. That is not this harbor's line and it is not the other place's either. It is only true."

"Then somebody four days up that road has to want it back."

"Or not want it. Those two are the same size of thing and neither of them is ours." She put her finger back on the same line and left it there. "If somebody wants a bell back they will write and they will sign it, and the day they do that this yard will hear about it inside a week." 

---

The keeper said out loud to that whole yard, before anybody left, that those eleven sheets have now been read once in a working place and will not be read again this week, and that anybody wanting a line off that list after today is to come to this yard and take the line off the sheet in this yard and not to ask for it to be read out a second time to a bigger crowd.

The man of about thirty-eight of that landing was in that yard at about four and said out loud that he is going back up that coast road on the Friday and that anybody with something to say to the Cinder Bell can say it to him and he will carry it, and that he is a man with a boat and not a body with a room.

Nobody asked him anything, and he said out loud that he had expected that and had come anyway, and he put the sheet of his own back under his arm and went up the lane.

---

At the end stall the correction and the refusal were standing where they were put on Monday, at the same size and a hand's breadth apart, with the wrong figure left showing underneath the correction and not struck out. The sun was off the wrong figure by four and onto the refusal, so that one could be read and the other could not.

A man of about forty-five of that market stood at about four and looked at the two of them and said out loud to about four that they have been up three days and that a man who puts two sheets of paper on a board expects to be wrong about one of them inside a month and not to be told which.

---

About half past five on that Wednesday the whole of the day went out at that stone in the yard's own mouth, in about nine hundred and thirty-three words, and the list was not read again in it, being read once already at three, and that was said out loud before anything else was said.

"**The reading was at about three in this yard and the keeper stood at the flat of that bench stone with the fourth sheet in his own hand and about twenty people in that yard and on that step, and it took about eleven minutes, and it was not read out a second time anywhere afterwards. About eleven of that twenty could not read that sheet and heard it. A man of about thirty-four of the second lay stood at the back of that yard at three and was not asked anything by anybody, before the reading or after it, and nobody said out loud that he was not to be asked anything.**"

"**That list is in two columns. The first column gives a place and the second gives a thing, and against each of the two hundred and eleven there is a month and a year of the giving, and there is no name and no hand and no seal and no person of any kind against any of them. The head of that sheet says what the two columns are: the first is what is unclaimed and the second is what is sealed, and the count of the first is two hundred and nine and of the second is two, and the two make the two hundred and eleven.**"

"**The woman of thirty of no office took that sheet at about half past three and read down the first column with her finger going down and about four of that twenty standing close enough to see which line she was on, and at about four she put her finger on one line and read it out loud herself, in the same words and the same order the keeper had used. The line is the Cinder Bell, and a bell, and the words given by the people of that place to the Ashtide Run for its use, and the month of April, and unclaimed. Against that line there is nothing at all. There is no hand, no seal, no person and no note, and the sheet says in three words at the foot of that column that an entry stands unclaimed where no claim has been entered against it.**"

"**Nobody in this yard asked why nobody had entered a claim against it, because that sheet says at the foot of that column that a claim is entered by the person who makes it, and a person who has never been asked for a claim does not make one. About four of that twenty said out loud to one another and not to anybody that a man who gives a bell away for nothing is not a person who has been asked for a claim, and about four said nothing.**"

"**The keeper said out loud to that whole yard before anybody left that those eleven sheets have been read once in a working place and will not be read again this week, and that anybody wanting a line off that list after today is to come to this yard and take the line off the sheet in this yard and not ask for it to be read out a second time to a bigger crowd. The man of about thirty-eight of that landing said out loud that he is going back up that coast road on the Friday and that anybody with a thing to say to the Cinder Bell can say it to him and he will carry it, and that he is a man with a boat and not a body with a room, and nobody asked him anything.**"

"**And the rest of it. No sum went into the foot of that fitting-out sheet on this day, and the foot of that sheet is at one hundred and twenty-four thousand eight hundred and twelve pence out and one hundred and fourteen thousand one hundred and seventy-two pence short, with ten thousand six hundred and forty pence in standing against it. There is a hundred and sixty-eight pence coming into this yard at about ten and a hundred and sixty-eight going out of it at about four, and twenty-eight plus eight thousand four hundred less eight thousand four hundred is twenty-eight. That slate holds twenty-eight pence, being two shillings and fourpence, and stands six hundred and twelve pence short of the wage of four marks, being three marks eleven shillings. This yard has gone one hundred and eighty-five days without a tin in it. The third of those four columns carries nothing, has nobody over it, and has at the head of it a boy's hand of twenty-two whose surname appears on no page, and he is off the water, and the fourth of those four has held at one hundred and twenty-five since the spring, has taken no line into it, and is neither shut nor struck. There are nine hulls about two sea miles off that mole head this evening, where nine lay on the twenty-first of January, and this harbor has made no claim that any of them has come up easier. The left side of the head of a man of about twenty-nine has been silent two hundred and seventy days this evening, a day further than yesterday, and no figure is set against that figure and he is not on the water. And a hearing he got back on the fourth of December is a different hearing from that one and is never set against it, and the hearing in the left side of the man of about thirty-two has not come back and is not priced.**"

Nobody was thanked for the reading or for the line that was read twice by two mouths, and that shed stood empty by about six with its door standing open on a slip nobody was on, and that page of that week had thirty-two lines on it.

---

**THE FOURTH SHEET OF THAT COPY WAS READ OUT ONCE AT ABOUT THREE IN ABOUT TWENTY PEOPLE OF WHOM ABOUT ELEVEN CANNOT READ IT, AND IT CARRIES TWO HUNDRED AND NINE ENTRIES OF WHAT IS UNCLAIMED AND TWO OF WHAT IS SEALED AND NO NAME AGAINST ANY ONE OF THEM. ONE OF THOSE LINES SAYS THAT A BELL THE PEOPLE OF THE CINDER BELL GAVE TO THE ASHTIDE RUN FOR ITS USE IN APRIL IS UNCLAIMED, AND NOBODY IN THAT YARD ASKED WHY NO CLAIM WAS ENTERED AGAINST IT. A MAN OF ABOUT THIRTY-FOUR OF THE SECOND LAY STOOD AT THE BACK AND WAS NOT ASKED ANYTHING. NOTHING WENT ONTO THAT FITTING-OUT SHEET AND NONE SETTLES BEFORE FRIDAY. THE BAG HOLDS TWENTY-EIGHT PENCE, BEING TWO SHILLINGS AND FOURPENCE.**
