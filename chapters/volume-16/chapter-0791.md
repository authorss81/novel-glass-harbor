# Chapter 791

*Monday the Nineteenth Day of September, the Thirty-First Day, a Rota Day and the Hundred and Eighty-Fifth Time, That Cistern Charged at About Nine in Fourteen Trips by Hand and Walked on by None of It, Four New Crews Standing at the Back of That Shed From About Ten, the Bell of the Cinder Bell Out of Service on Its Twelfth Day of Fourteen, the Bag at Twenty-Eight Pence, the First Column at Five Hundred and Twenty-Four and the Second at Two Thousand Four Hundred and Ninety-Four*

Monday the nineteenth day of September, with that market open from six and a low water about four in the afternoon and an east wind that had come about in the night and had not gone again, a yard below that market with a shed standing in it, and about nine people standing in that shed at half past five that afternoon.

**A rota day and the hundred and eighty-fifth time, that cistern charged at about nine and taken in fourteen trips by hand and walked on by none of them, the figure written against the rota slate at about one and not read out, and there is no charge on that cistern tomorrow or the day after, the next being on the morning of Thursday. Not a wage Tuesday and not a wipe, and nothing settled into the foot of that fitting-out sheet today, that foot standing at one hundred and twenty-seven thousand and eighty-two pence out, being seven hundred and ninety-four marks, three shillings and sixpence, and one hundred and sixteen thousand four hundred and forty-two pence short, being seven hundred and twenty-seven marks, ten shillings and twopence, against ten thousand six hundred and forty pence in. A hundred and sixty-eight pence came into that yard at about ten on a Monday as on any other day and a hundred and sixty-eight went out of it at about four, and under that slate is twenty-eight pence, being two shillings and fourpence, and that shed is two hundred and fourth days without a tin opened in it, the last having been the twenty-ninth of April past.**

**Nine counters were carried to the close of this thirty-first day. The notice of the seventeenth of May past is on its four hundred and eighty-ninth day and the corners of it have gone soft where it is nailed. The bellwright of this harbor is into the four hundred and ninety-first day of a chest against his wall that nobody has opened, and no figure has been asked of him. Those two objects belonging to nobody on that crown are into the eight hundred and twenty-ninth day of themselves. That bench under that pump has stood bare five hundred and ninety-fifth night running. Two lights about a mile off that point are on their four hundred and thirteenth day and no figure of money stands against either of them. The six-line sheet at the back of that end stall is on the three hundred and sixty-eighth day of its standing. That paper of four lines in Fish Street is on the three hundred and eighty-third day. That nine-line sheet with a mark on it in that front street has stood four hundred days. Four hundred and thirty-one days have gone by with a man of about fifty-one saying nothing at all to anybody about a page of six names. A boy's hand of twenty-two heads the middle of those four columns and there is nothing under it, nobody has ever been named over it, that boy's surname being on no page of this harbor and his being off the water for the seventh time. The fourth of those four is still at one hundred and twenty-five, still holds no line at all, and is neither shut nor struck. Nine hulls lie about two sea miles off that mole head as nine lay there on the twenty-first of January, and this harbor has not said a word about any of them easing. A man of about twenty-nine hears nothing out of the left side of his head and that is the two hundred and eighty-ninth day of it, one more than the day before, no figure is set against it, and he is not on the water.**

---

That cistern went at about nine and took fourteen men and fourteen trips and was walked on by none of them, and the butt stood short at the end of it and had a skin on it by about one, and nobody drank it and nobody said that it was bad. The fourteen came off it in the order they went on, and the last of them wiped his soles on that step and stood a moment looking at the water standing in it, and then the cover went on and was chained, and the chain went through two links and had to be turned before it would take.

The figure went against that rota slate at about one in the hand of the man about twenty-seven of the second lay and was not read out, and about four of that nine saw it go on and nobody said the number out loud. The chalk went on and the man who put it there wiped his thumb along the edge of it and then put the thumb down his own leg, which is what a man does when he has written a thing he does not mean to say. The keeper said, to about four and not to about nine, "That number is read by the man who wrote it and by nobody else, and a number that has to be carried from a mouth will be carried wrong."

---

Four new crews came in on that Monday at about ten, being eight men and two of about twenty years, and they stood at the back of that shed because nobody had told them where to stand and they did not ask.

The man of about thirty-two had them from about ten. He told them the thing he had been told himself in the spring and he told it three times over and got a different part of it different each time, and at about eleven a man of about twenty-seven of the second lay did the same job on the same bench in about a minute and a half without saying one word, and about four people in that shed watched both of them do it and about four of those four said nothing whatever about the difference. The keeper stood a pace back from that bench with his slate under his arm and did not put himself between a man and his work, and he had come into that yard that morning with the answer to a question nobody in it had asked, and he held on to it about an hour longer than he needed to.

---

The ordinary work of that Monday was the third coat coming off that pump and a length of coil measured against a gauge, and no line went onto that fitting-out sheet because a line with an hour against it and no boat against it is not a line. The third coat came off in flakes about the size of a thumbnail and went into a bucket that had been standing under that pump since the spring.

A man of about thirty-four of the second lay was in that shed from about ten until about two and was not asked anything by anybody, and it is the forty-first day of this run of days on which that has happened. He had the end bench to himself and he worked at it the way a man works who has decided in advance that he is not going to be spoken to.

---

At about half past two a boat of the Ashtide Run came in over the bar with nothing in her and put a coil of line on that stone, and the man of about forty of that route was not in her and had not sent anybody.

That is the ordinary of a Monday in this harbor and nothing was written about it, and the keeper wrote nothing in any column about it because a boat that crosses with nothing in her is a boat and not a page. She lay alongside without a line out, and one of those four new crews looked at her a while and then looked away, which is what a man does with a thing he has been told nothing about.

---

The bell of the Cinder Bell is on the twelfth day of being out of service and the fourteen days of it run to the twenty-second of September, and nobody in this harbor has rung it and nobody in this harbor has asked anybody else to do without it. It is four hundred yards and three fields off, and on the days when it is out there is no hour on this coast that a man in this yard can hear and count, and there has not been a complaint about that either.

The correction and the refusal were standing at that end stall on their twenty-second day at the same size and a hand's breadth apart with the wrong figure left showing underneath the correction and not struck out, and the leaf from the Cinder Bell was on its seventeenth day a step to the left of the refusal, and the sheet with the fourteen gaps stood at that end stall at fourteen with nothing on it moved.

---

A hundred and sixty-eight pence came into this yard at about ten and a hundred and sixty-eight went out of it at about four, and twenty-eight plus eight thousand four hundred less eight thousand four hundred is twenty-eight, and the twenty-eight is still under that slate, being two shillings and fourpence, which is six hundred and twelve pence short of the wage of four marks, being three marks eleven shillings. This yard has gone two hundred and fourth days without a tin in it. Nobody was thanked for the charge or for the ten people standing at the back of that shed, and that shed stood empty by about six with its door standing open on a slip nobody was on, and that page of that week had thirty-one lines on it.

**A ROTA DAY AND THE HUNDRED AND EIGHTY-FIFTH CHARGE WENT ONTO THAT CISTERN THIS MORNING AND WAS WALKED ON BY NONE OF IT. FOUR NEW CREWS STOOD AT THE BACK OF THAT SHED FROM ABOUT TEN AND WERE GIVEN NOTHING TO DO AND ASKED FOR NOTHING. THE BAG HOLDS TWENTY-EIGHT PENCE, BEING TWO SHILLINGS AND FOURPENCE, AND THIS YARD IS TWO HUNDRED AND FOURTH DAYS WITHOUT A TIN IN IT.**

*Second column, not checkable from a mole, entered on the said Monday at about six in the evening in that shed, and no name is against it: that the reason the figure of that charge was put against a slate and not read out is that a number said aloud in a yard of nine people is a number four of them will have by the stair and the fifth will have differently, and a rota is a thing a man checks with his own eyes and not with his memory of a voice; that the reason he was kept on those four crews and not taken off them is that a man learns a thing by being wrong at it in front of people who are better at it than he is, and that this yard has never had the money to put a man beside him and is not going to pretend otherwise; that the reason the boat that came in over the bar with nothing in her went into no column is that this harbor keeps columns of pages and not of water, and a column of boats would be a list of what has not happened and this yard has been told for years what it is like to keep one of those; that the reason the gap at that bench was not remarked on by anybody is that saying it out loud in about nine would make one of them something he is not, being a man somebody has spoken about, and there is no office in this harbor a man can be sent back from.*
