# Chapter 585

*Friday the Twenty-Fifth Day of February, Not a Rota Day and Not a Wage Tuesday and Not a Wipe and No Charge Placed on That Cistern on It, a Figure Brought Up That Quay at About Eight in the Morning by a Man of About Forty-One of the First Lay in a Boat on the Night of the Twenty-Fourth and Not Asked For, Ninety-Four Seconds Again by a Half-Minute Glass in a Boat in the Dark and the Three Figures on That Slate Not Added To, a Fifth Line Put on the Back of an Old Slate at About Ten in the Morning in That Shed With the Door Standing Open and Nobody on That Slip, Eleven Vessels of That Port Inside That Line of Boats When the Answer Came on the Night of the Twenty-Fourth and Eleven on the Night of the Twenty-Fifth, Being Two Times Eleven and Twenty-Two Lines of Crossing on a Page of Days and Off the Crossings and Not Off an Estimate, and Never Said in a Room, a Man of About Thirty-Three Who Hires Hands Asked and Not Answered and Not Given the Fourth Thing, a Woman of About Thirty-One Saying in About One Hundred and Forty-Six Words That a Man Who Is the First One to Go Down Is the Man Every Man in That Harbor Will Send the Second One to, Nobody Hurt and Nobody Made a Hero and Nobody Thanked, the Shortfall Said Out Loud at About Half Past Five in About Three Hundred and Fifty-Three Words Unmoved at Ninety-Six Thousand Six Hundred and Twenty-Three Pence, Being Six Hundred and Three Marks, Eleven Shillings and Elevenpence, the Bag at Twenty-Eight Pence, Being Two Shillings and Fourpence*

Friday the twenty-fifth day of February, in that harbor, in about four hundred yards of water and along a stone quay, and in a shed with a roof on it, a bench stone, and that door that stands open on a slip whether anybody is on that slip or not.

**The said Friday was not a rota day and was not a wage Tuesday and was not a wipe, and no charge was placed on that cistern on it, being a Monday and a Thursday thing, and the covers were on it. Nothing went into that sheet in any hand in any line on that day and nothing came off it in any hand, and the figure that was made on the back of an old slate at about ten in the morning is a figure in a column and is not on that sheet and is not in any minute. One hundred and sixty-eight pence came into that yard at about ten in the morning and one hundred and sixty-eight pence went out of it at about four in the afternoon, and one hundred and sixty-eight pence is one mark and eightpence, and there are twenty-eight pence in the bag at the close of the said day, being two shillings and fourpence, and the figure that bag is short of the wage of four marks is six hundred and twelve pence, being three marks eleven shillings. That sheet stands at one hundred and seven thousand two hundred and sixty-three pence out, being six hundred and seventy marks, five shillings and threepence, and ten thousand six hundred and forty pence in, being sixty-six marks, six shillings and eightpence, and it is ninety-six thousand six hundred and twenty-three pence short, being six hundred and three marks, eleven shillings and elevenpence, and that figure is against that yard's own book and was said out loud in that yard's own mouth and was not answered by anybody. Nothing has been in a tin in that shed since the twenty-ninth day of April past, and no penny of the whole of that day came out of that bag, out of the owner of any boat, out of the man with two papers, or out of the Crown.**

**The nine that a stranger in that harbor could have read off any wall are the nine below, and not one of them was found by looking: the notice of the seventeenth of May past, two hundred and eighty-three days; the bellwright of that harbor, not asked for two hundred and eighty-five; the two objects of nobody's on that crown, six hundred and twenty-three days; the bench under that pump, bare for the three hundred and eighty-ninth night; the two lights at about a mile off that point, two hundred and seven days; the six-line sheet at the back of that end stall, its hundred and sixty-second day; the paper of four lines in Fish Street, its hundred and seventy-seventh; the nine-line sheet with a seal on it in that front street, its hundred and ninety-fourth; and a man of about fifty-one who has said nothing to anybody about a page of six names for two hundred and twenty-five days. The board at the head of that mole carries five papers and two of them have never been opened, and the demand for the returns went down that coast in a basket yesterday morning in the same basket it came back in on the thirteenth, and it is a second asking and it has a day and a night of a boat on it. There are nine hulls about two sea miles off the head of that mole this afternoon. A man of about twenty-nine who came home on that boat can hear out of the left side of his head and it has been eighty-three days and no figure has been set against it and he is not on the water and nobody has sent for him.**

---

The man of about forty-one of the first lay came up that quay at about eight in the morning with his own figure in his own mouth and nobody had asked him for it and nobody was in the room when he gave it.

He had it off the half-minute glass in his own boat in the dark, from the hour they stood off on Thursday afternoon to the hour he came alongside, and he counted from one answer to the next answer as it came, and he got ninety-four seconds, and the three figures already on that slate are ninety-four seconds, and the four of them are the same figure. He did not add his to the other three, and the sum on that slate is still two hundred and eighty-two seconds, being four minutes and forty-two seconds, and the sum of three and the figure of four are two different things on one piece of slate and a stranger with a leaf and a pencil can see that they are.

---

At about ten in the morning the man who keeps the leaf made a fifth line on the back of an old slate, and he made it in that shed with the door standing open and nobody on that slip at that hour, and he did not read it out to anybody afterward and has not read it out to anybody since.

The fifth line is a count and not a time. It is the number of vessels of that port inside that line of boats when the answer came, on the night of Thursday the twenty-fourth and on the night of Friday the twenty-fifth, taken out of the crossing column of that port as it stands on a page of days and taken out of nothing else. It is eleven on the twenty-fourth and eleven on the twenty-fifth, and two times eleven is twenty-two, and those are twenty-two lines of crossing on a page of days in a harbor four days down that coast, and a man in that harbor can go and read them.

The fourth line of that slate, which is the count of nine on each of the two nights of the twenty-first and twenty-second of January, being eighteen lines, is where it was, and the fifth line is under it, and the two of them are six weeks apart on one piece of slate and neither one is the answer to the other one. A stranger with a leaf and a pencil can take both off and can see for himself that the number went up by two between January and this month, and that nobody in that harbor did that.

Then a man of about thirty-three who hires hands asked, once, out loud, in that shed with the door open, whether that figure was going to be read out at the end stall, and the man who keeps the leaf said out loud, in about one hundred and ninety-nine words, what a figure in a column is for.

"**No. It is not going to be read out at the end stall and it is not going to be read out in that shed either, and the reason is not modesty. A figure in a column is a thing anybody can go and be wrong at, and a figure said out loud in a room is a thing four people will be right about in four kitchens by supper and that is worse.** I have a slate on that bench stone with five lines on it and four of them are a figure anybody can check with a watch and a stop, and the fifth one is a figure that anybody in that harbor can check with a page of days and a pencil, and I would sooner have eleven lines that can be argued with than a sentence in a room. It is in a column. It is not in a room. It is not going in a room while I am the man who holds it, and there is a man in this harbor who is not going to hold it at all and that is the correct number of holders for a figure like that."

Nobody argued with him about it, and the man of about thirty-three did not ask a second question about it, and nobody was given a fourth thing to do that day.

At the end stall at about eleven in the morning, in the open, with about nine people on the boards, a woman of about thirty-one said out loud, in about one hundred and forty-six words, what this harbor would do on Monday with the man who went down on Thursday, and she was not asked and nobody had asked her for anything.

"**On Monday there will be eleven men on this quay who want to be the man who goes next, and in three weeks there will be one, and in three months the one will be the man who went on Thursday and nobody will remember that he went on a Tuesday and came up on the same Tuesday.** I watched this harbor do that in the winter of the twelfth and it cost that harbor a boat and a winter. A man who is the first one to go down is the man every man in this harbor will send the second one to, and that is not a thing I am going to let the word go round about. I hold a particular and I am not giving it, and that is a different thing, and I am not answering anything about it this morning."

Nobody took her up, and nobody thanked her, and the boards emptied at about the half hour and the fish went on being landed.

---

And at about half past five on that Friday the man who keeps the leaf said the shortfall out loud at that bench stone and used about three hundred and fifty-three words on a fifth line on the back of a slate, and nobody in that slip was asked to read that slate aloud.

"Ninety-six thousand six hundred and twenty-three pence short, being six hundred and three marks, eleven shillings and elevenpence. Unmoved, and nothing went onto that sheet and nothing came off it, and the bag is twenty-eight pence, and a basket went down that coast at about four yesterday afternoon with a paper in it and a day and a night of a boat on it.

**There is a fifth line on that slate with a figure under it that is a count and not a time, and it is off the crossings of two nights of this month and not off anybody's estimate, and it is bigger than the figure on the fourth line by two, and I am not going to say the number in this slip because a number said in a slip is a number four people carry home, and it is on the slate and a stranger with a leaf and a pencil can take it off in a minute and be wrong in front of me.** The four of us who counted the interval on Saturday and the fifth man who counted it in a boat last night have the same figure, and I have not added his to the three because three of them is the sum I printed and one of them is a fourth man in a dark boat, and those are two different things and I will not put them on one line.

A pin came out of a clapper arm on Thursday and the number on the fifth line is what happened next, and a man of about thirty-two is the only person in this harbor who has been in that water and looked at that bell, and there is no figure anywhere on any board for what he saw, because what he saw was not a number. The third column of that leaf took nothing today and nobody was appointed to it, and the fourth took nothing and stands at one hundred and twenty-five, and the reason that fourth has taken no line in eleven weeks is not entered again by me tonight."

Nobody was thanked for the count and nobody was thanked for the arithmetic, and the slate stayed face up on that bench stone with five lines on it and nobody read it aloud.

**A FIGURE CAME UP THAT QUAY AT ABOUT EIGHT IN THE MORNING FROM A MAN OF ABOUT FORTY-ONE OF THE FIRST LAY WHO COUNTED THE INTERVAL IN HIS OWN BOAT IN THE DARK WITH A HALF-MINUTE GLASS AND GOT NINETY-FOUR SECONDS, WHICH IS THE SAME FIGURE AS THE THREE ALREADY ON THAT SLATE, AND HIS WAS NOT ADDED TO THE THREE AND THE SUM ON THAT SLATE IS STILL TWO HUNDRED AND EIGHTY-TWO SECONDS, BEING FOUR MINUTES AND FORTY-TWO SECONDS. A FIFTH LINE WAS PUT ON THE BACK OF AN OLD SLATE AT ABOUT TEN IN THE MORNING IN THAT SHED WITH THE DOOR STANDING OPEN AND NOBODY ON THAT SLIP, BEING A COUNT AND NOT A TIME, AND THE COUNT IS ELEVEN VESSELS OF THAT PORT INSIDE THAT LINE OF BOATS WHEN THE ANSWER CAME ON THE NIGHT OF THE TWENTY-FOURTH AND ELEVEN ON THE NIGHT OF THE TWENTY-FIFTH, BEING TWO TIMES ELEVEN AND TWENTY-TWO LINES OF CROSSING ON A PAGE OF DAYS FOUR DAYS DOWN THAT COAST, OFF THE CROSSINGS AND NOT OFF AN ESTIMATE, AND IT IS SIX WEEKS APART ON ONE SLATE FROM THE NINE AND THE EIGHTEEN OF THE TWENTY-FIRST AND TWENTY-SECOND OF JANUARY AND NEITHER FIGURE IS THE ANSWER TO THE OTHER. A MAN OF ABOUT THIRTY-THREE ASKED ONCE WHETHER IT WAS GOING TO BE READ OUT AT THE END STALL AND WAS ANSWERED IN ABOUT ONE HUNDRED AND NINETY-NINE WORDS THAT A FIGURE IN A COLUMN IS A THING ANYBODY CAN GO AND BE WRONG AT AND A FIGURE SAID IN A ROOM IS A THING FOUR PEOPLE WILL BE RIGHT ABOUT IN FOUR KITCHENS BY SUPPER. A WOMAN OF ABOUT THIRTY-ONE SAID IN ABOUT ONE HUNDRED AND FORTY-SIX WORDS THAT A MAN WHO IS THE FIRST ONE TO GO DOWN IS THE MAN EVERY MAN IN THAT HARBOR WILL SEND THE SECOND ONE TO. NOBODY WAS HURT AND NOBODY WAS MADE A HERO AND NOBODY WAS THANKED AND NOT ONE PERSON WAS ASKED FOR A THING HE HAD NOT ALREADY PUT ON THAT BENCH STONE. THE SHEET THAT CARRIES THE MONEY IS NINETY-SIX THOUSAND SIX HUNDRED AND TWENTY-THREE PENCE SHORT AGAINST THAT YARD'S OWN BOOK, BEING SIX HUNDRED AND THREE MARKS, ELEVEN SHILLINGS AND ELEVENPENCE, AND THERE ARE TWENTY-EIGHT PENCE IN THE BAG UNDER THE SLATE.**

*Second column, not checkable from a mole, entered on the said Friday at about six in the evening in that shed, and no name is against it: that at about eight in the morning the reason a man of about forty-one came up that quay with a figure of his own in his own mouth is his own and is that he had said on Wednesday on a slate that he would rather have done once with somebody in it below him than go a whole winter without knowing he could, and that on Thursday he found out that he could, and that a man who finds that out at two o'clock in the afternoon in a boat in the cold has to tell somebody before supper or it will come out in a bar in a month as a story about a man with a glass; and that at about ten in the morning the reason the fifth line went under the fourth and not over it is mine and is that the fourth is January and the fifth is this month, and that a slate is a thing people read down, and that a stranger taking two figures off it wants the earlier one first, and that the order of two lines on a slate is the only argument that slate makes about which of two counts matters more; and that at about ten past eleven the reason I said no to the end stall is my own and is that in November a figure of mine was read out in that shed and was in a printed sheet in Veyr inside nine weeks, and that the figure was true and the sheet was true and that a true figure in a true sheet still cost me eleven weeks of being the man who said it, and that I have not been able to walk into that shed without somebody repeating that figure at me since, and that I am not doing it to eleven vessels as well; and that at about two in the afternoon the reason a woman of about thirty-one was allowed to say that and not stopped by anybody is hers and is that she has been in that room more times this winter than anybody and has never once been asked for the thing she holds, and that a person who has never been asked for anything will say a thing in the open the first time she is able to, and that the only reason it came out this morning is that there was a man on that quay who had come up and that is a thing a harbor only does about four times a year; and that at about four in the afternoon the reason there is no sixth line on that slate is mine and is that I have a figure of eleven and a figure of ninety-four seconds and a figure of one hundred and sixty-eight pence in and the same figure out, and that four figures on one slate start to look like a page of hours, and that a page of hours is a page a man can be sent out on, and that a column of counts is a page nobody can be sent out on, and that I would rather hold the second kind of page for the rest of my life; and that at about ten past six the reason the slate was left face up on that bench stone all evening and not put behind the press is mine and is that a slate face down is a slate somebody has decided about, and that on Monday nine men will walk past that bench stone and six of them will not stop and three of them will, and that the three are the only people in that harbor this month who are going to be curious about a count instead of about a man, and that I am not going to have made that decision for them in advance on a Friday afternoon.*
