# Chapter 588

*Monday the Twenty-Eighth Day of February, a Rota Day, the Hundred and Twenty-Ninth Charge on That Cistern at the Back of That Market at About Eight in the Morning in Eleven Trips by Hand and Walked on by None of It, the Name at the Head of That Rota Board Still Off It, Six Lines of a First Form of a Thing People Can Disagree With in Public Written in a Large Hand in Chalk on a Limewashed Wall Across the Lane From the Room Over That Market on Which Nothing Had Been Written, Read Out at About Eleven in the Morning in About Nine People and Told to About Eleven More Who Cannot Read, With Two Lines Under Them in a Smaller Hand Being That a Civil Ceremony Was Gone Through on the Twenty-Seventh and That Anybody Who Disagrees With Either of Those Two Things May Write Under It, Nobody Asked to Sign It and Nobody Asked to Agree With It and Nothing Written Under It by Anybody by the Close of the Day, a Man of About Thirty-Three Who Hires Hands Asking in About One Hundred and Seventy-Nine Words Whether the Fourth of Those Six Lines Means a Thing and a Woman of About Thirty-One Saying in About One Hundred and Fifty Words That the Second of Those Six Lines Is the Only One That Does Anything and That She Is Not Writing Under It This Afternoon, the Shortfall Said Out Loud at About Half Past Five in About Three Hundred and Eighty-Seven Words Unmoved at Ninety-Seven Thousand Six Hundred and Thirty-Six Pence, Being Six Hundred and Ten Marks and Three Shillings, the Bag at Twenty-Eight Pence, Being Two Shillings and Fourpence*

Monday the twenty-eighth day of February, in that harbor, in about four hundred yards of water and along a stone quay, and in a shed with a roof on it, a bench stone, and about nine people standing in it at half past five in the afternoon.

**The said Monday was a rota day and was not a wage Tuesday and was not a wipe. That cistern at the back of that market was charged for the hundred and twenty-ninth time at about eight in the morning and was walked on by none of it, and the name at the head of that rota board is still off it, and the water that went into that cistern was not used for anything at any hour of that day. Nothing went into that sheet in any hand in any line on that day and nothing came off it in any hand, and the six lines of chalk and the two lines under them are not on that sheet and are not on any sheet and are not in any minute. One hundred and sixty-eight pence came into that yard at about ten in the morning and one hundred and sixty-eight pence went out of it at about four in the afternoon, and one hundred and sixty-eight pence is one mark and eightpence, and there are twenty-eight pence in the bag at the close of the said day, being two shillings and fourpence, and the figure that bag is short of the wage of four marks is six hundred and twelve pence, being three marks eleven shillings. That sheet stands at one hundred and eight thousand two hundred and seventy-six pence out, being six hundred and seventy-six marks, nine shillings and eightpence, and ten thousand six hundred and forty pence in, being sixty-six marks, six shillings and eightpence, and it is ninety-seven thousand six hundred and thirty-six pence short, being six hundred and ten marks and three shillings, and that figure is against that yard's own book and was said out loud in that yard's own mouth and was not answered by anybody. Nothing has been in a tin in that shed since the twenty-ninth day of April past, and no penny of the whole of that day came out of that bag, out of the owner of any boat, out of the man with two papers, or out of the Crown.**

**The nine that a stranger in that harbor could have read off any wall are the nine below, and not one of them was found by looking: the notice of the seventeenth of May past, two hundred and eighty-six days; the bellwright of that harbor, not asked for two hundred and eighty-eight; the two objects of nobody's on that crown, six hundred and twenty-six days; the bench under that pump, bare for the three hundred and ninety-second night; the two lights at about a mile off that point, two hundred and ten days; the six-line sheet at the back of that end stall, its hundred and sixty-fifth day; the paper of four lines in Fish Street, its hundred and eightieth; the nine-line sheet with a seal on it in that front street, its hundred and ninety-seventh; and a man of about fifty-one who has said nothing to anybody about a page of six names for two hundred and twenty-eight days. The board at the head of that mole carries five papers and two of them have never been opened, and the basket with the second asking in it comes back up that coast on the morning. There are nine hulls about two sea miles off the head of that mole this afternoon. A man of about twenty-nine who came home on that boat can hear out of the left side of his head and it has been eighty-six days and no figure has been set against it and he is not on the water and nobody has sent for him.**

That cistern was charged for the hundred and twenty-ninth time at about eight in the morning and the covers were back on it by about a quarter past nine, and the butt behind that market stood full to the top of the second hoop until about four o'clock and no bucket came out of it, and there is still no figure anywhere in that harbor for a new length of the hose at the top of it, and the two men who carry it up that ladder have never once been on that rota board and have never once asked to be.

And at about ten o'clock in the morning a man of about twenty-seven, who wrote the six lines of an account on the wall of the room over that market on the second of this month and is on nobody's crew, took a piece of chalk and wrote on a limewashed end wall of a chandler's shop across the lane from that room. That wall had been whitewashed twice this winter and had nothing on it, and nobody asked him to write on it and he was not asked by anybody to do it, and he said out loud at the time that nobody had asked him and that he had told two people on Friday that he was going to do it and that neither of them had said anything about it.

He wrote it in a large hand so that a man standing at the wall can read it without going close, and there are six lines and there are six of them.

1. *Any person in this harbor may write under this wall anything they make of anything that has been said out loud in this harbor, and nothing on this wall is agreed to by anybody.*
2. *A person who writes under this wall may leave their name off it, and that is not a fault in what they wrote.*
3. *Nobody has to answer what is written under this wall, and nobody has to say they have read it.*
4. *A thing written under this wall is not a record, is not in any book, and does not put anybody in or out of a place.*
5. *The four lines of correction and the one line of refusal on the wall at the top of Fish Street stay where they are, and are not on this wall, and are not copied onto it.*
6. *This is the first form of this and it is not the whole of it, and nobody has agreed to any part of it.*

Under those six, in a smaller hand, the same man wrote two more lines.

*Two people went through a civil ceremony in a room at the top of Fish Street on the twenty-seventh day of this month. Anybody who disagrees with that, or with any of the six lines above it, may write under this wall.*

He read all eight of them out loud at about eleven in the morning in about nine people, and about eleven of the people of that market could not read the wall and were told what was on it by the man of about forty-six who mends nets on the third lay, and he told them the eight of them twice and got nothing wrong either time.

A man of about thirty-three who hires hands asked, once, out loud, whether the fourth of those six lines meant a thing, and the man of about twenty-seven answered him in about one hundred and seventy-nine words, standing under his own writing with the chalk still in his hand.

"**It means that nobody loses a berth or a place or a day of work for what they write under that wall, and it means nothing else at all, and I know which of those two is the important one and it is not the one people will like.** There is no office in this harbor that can act on a thing written under that wall, and there is no office in this harbor that can take a thing off it either, and that is why I wrote the fourth line before I wrote the second one. If there is a man in this harbor who can make a person lose a day for writing something down, then this wall is not a place to write on and I will take the chalk off it myself this afternoon and chalk it back in the morning, and nobody in that market has to be asked to agree to that condition, because it is in there already as a fact about the wall and not as a thing anybody voted on."

A woman of about thirty-one came past that wall at about half past one in the afternoon on her way to the end stall, stopped, read the six lines, and said out loud, in about one hundred and fifty words, that the second of those six lines is the only one that does anything, and that she was not writing under it this afternoon, and nobody had asked her to.

"**The first of those six is a thing a clerk would say and the third of them is a thing an office in another port would say, and the second of them is the one that is a door, and it is the only one of the three.** And I am not writing anything under that wall this afternoon and nobody has asked me to and I would have said no if they had. I have a particular and I am not giving it and I am not putting it on a wall in chalk, and there is a difference between not giving a thing and being allowed to be somebody who does not give things, and that second one is what the second of those six lines is for. Write under it whatever anybody likes. I am going to be one of the people standing in front of it."

Nobody took her up, and about four people wrote nothing at all and about nine people read the six lines twice, and by the close of that day there was nothing under that wall in any hand in that harbor, and the chalk was still on it, and nobody was asked to sign it and nobody was asked to agree with it and nobody was thanked for any part of it.

And at about half past five on that Monday the man who keeps the leaf said the shortfall out loud at that bench stone and used about three hundred and eighty-seven words on a wall with nothing under it, and nobody in that slip was asked what was supposed to go under it.

"Ninety-six thousand six hundred and thirty-six pence short, being six hundred and ten marks and three shillings. Unmoved, and nothing went onto that sheet and nothing came off it, and the bag is twenty-eight pence, and that cistern was charged this morning for the hundred and twenty-ninth time and not one bucket came out of it.

**Eight lines of chalk went up on a limewashed wall across the lane from that market this morning and by the middle of the afternoon there was nothing under them, and that is the correct state of that wall this month and I have written the day of it in that leaf because in nine weeks somebody is going to ask me when it started.** A wall that people can be disagreed on in public, with nothing written under it, is not a wall that has failed. It is a wall that has a door in it and nobody has wanted to come through the door yet, and there is a difference between those two things and this harbor has never been shown the difference because it has never had a door before.

The sixth of those six lines is that it is the first form of it and that nobody has agreed to any part of it, and that line is the one that keeps it honest, and it is the one that about four people read and did not use. I have had a page of two communities on this bench stone since the sixteenth with one of the two not named on it, and I have had a slate with five lines on it since the eleventh of this month, and a man of about thirty-two put a pin in a jar on the shelf on Thursday, and this morning a man of about twenty-seven put a door in a wall for nothing. None of those five things is a party and none of them is a body and none of them can send anybody anywhere, and I am not going to call any of them one this month.

The third column took nothing today and the fourth took nothing and stands at one hundred and twenty-five, and there is no date against it anywhere in this harbor and I am not going to put one on it."

Nobody was thanked for the arithmetic and nobody was thanked for the wall, and the wall stood across the lane from that market with eight lines of chalk on it and nothing under them, and the wedge was still on that bench stone.

EIGHT LINES WENT UP ON A LIMEWASHED WALL ACROSS THE LANE FROM THAT MARKET IN A LARGE HAND IN CHALK AT ABOUT TEN IN THE MORNING, BEING SIX LINES OF A FIRST FORM OF A THING PEOPLE CAN DISAGREE WITH IN PUBLIC AND TWO SMALLER LINES UNDER THEM, AND NOBODY ASKED THE MAN OF ABOUT TWENTY-SEVEN TO WRITE THEM AND HE IS ON NOBODY'S CREW. NOTHING WAS WRITTEN UNDER THAT WALL BY ANYBODY BY THE CLOSE OF THAT DAY. NOBODY WAS ASKED FOR THE REST OF IT AND NOBODY WAS THANKED. THE SHEET THAT CARRIES THE MONEY IS NINETY-SEVEN THOUSAND SIX HUNDRED AND THIRTY-SIX PENCE SHORT AGAINST THAT YARD'S OWN BOOK, BEING SIX HUNDRED AND TEN MARKS AND THREE SHILLINGS, AND THERE ARE TWENTY-EIGHT PENCE IN THE BAG UNDER THE SLATE.**

*Second column, not checkable from a mole, entered on the said Monday at about six in the evening in that shed, and no name is against it: that at about eight in the morning the reason that cistern paragraph is eleven words and has been eleven words every Monday and Thursday since the autumn is that a thing done one hundred and twenty-nine times does not improve with practice, it only gets more familiar, and that the only thing a rota gets better at is being ignored, and that I write the charge down every time because a charge that is not written down is a charge that in nine weeks four people will believe was never made;
