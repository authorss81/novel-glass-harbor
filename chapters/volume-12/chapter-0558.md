# Chapter 558

*Saturday the Twenty-Ninth Day of January, Not a Rota Day and Not a Wage Tuesday and Not a Wipe, a Man of About Thirty-Four of the Second Lay, Who Was in That Room over That Market on the Sixteenth and Said Nothing and Has Not Been Asked About It Since, Standing at the End of That Slip at About One o'Clock in the Afternoon in Front of About Nine People and Asking Out Loud, in About a Hundred and Ten Words, That the Man of About Thirty-Two Come and Ask Him in the Open, Being the First Time in That Harbor That a Person Has Asked to Be Asked, and the Reason Given by Him and Not Taken From Him, the Man of About Thirty-Two Saying Out Loud in About Thirty Words That He Is Not Going to Ask Him Today and That Today Is Not the Day and That When He Does Ask Him He Will Ask Him in Front of the Same Nine People, and That It Is Not a Promise, and the Asking Not Being Closed, Answered, Thanked, Agreed To or Taken Back Before the End of That Day, the Shortfall Said Out Loud at About Half Past Five Unmoved at Ninety-Six Thousand and Four Hundred and Fifty-Four Pence, Being Six Hundred and Two Marks, Eleven Shillings and Twopence, the Bag at Twenty-Eight Pence, Being Two Shillings and Fourpence*

Saturday the twenty-ninth day of January, in that harbor, in about four hundred yards of water and along a stone quay, and in a shed with a roof on it, a bench stone, and a door that stands open on a slip.

**The said Saturday was not a rota day and was not a wage Tuesday and was not a wipe, and no charge was placed on that cistern on it. Nothing went out on any line of the sheet that carries the money in any hand on that day and nothing went into it in any hand, and nobody was asked for anything. One hundred and sixty-eight pence came into that yard at about ten in the morning and one hundred and sixty-eight pence went out of it at about four in the afternoon, and there are twenty-eight pence in the bag at the close of the said day, being two shillings and fourpence, and the figure that bag is short of the wage of four marks is six hundred and twelve pence, being three marks eleven shillings. That sheet stands at one hundred and seven thousand and ninety-four pence out, being six hundred and sixty-nine marks, four shillings and sixpence, and ten thousand six hundred and forty pence in, being sixty-six marks, six shillings and eightpence, and it is ninety-six thousand and four hundred and fifty-four pence short, being six hundred and two marks, eleven shillings and twopence, and that figure is against that yard's own book and was said out loud in that yard's own mouth and was not answered by anybody. Nothing has been in a tin in that shed since the twenty-ninth day of April past, and no penny of the whole of that day came out of that bag, out of the owner of any boat, out of the man with two papers, or out of the Crown.**

**The nine that a stranger in that harbor could have read off any wall are the nine below, and not one of them was found by looking: the notice of the seventeenth of May past, two hundred and fifty-six days; the bellwright of that harbor, not asked for two hundred and fifty-eight; the two objects of nobody's on that crown, five hundred and ninety-six days; the bench under that pump, bare for the three hundred and sixty-second night; the two lights at about a mile off that point, one hundred and eighty days; the six-line sheet at the back of that end stall, its hundred and thirty-fifth day; the paper of four lines in Fish Street, its hundred and fiftieth; the nine-line sheet with a seal on it in that front street, its hundred and sixty-seventh; and a man of about fifty-one who has said nothing to anybody about a page of six names for a hundred and ninety-eight days. The five lines in chalk at the top of Fish Street have been up five days and the fifth of them has a man's own hand at the foot of it and has not been argued with by anybody in that street. The page of hours in that shed carries three names and a rule and nine words with no answer under them and the three of those names have not asked anybody for anything today. A man of about twenty-nine who came home on that boat can hear out of the left side of his head and it has been fifty-six days and he has not been asked to hold one of those three channels and he has not asked and nobody has thought of it.**

---

And a man of about thirty-four of the second lay, who was in the room over that market on the sixteenth of this month at the back and said nothing and has not been asked about it since, stood at the end of that slip at about one o'clock in the afternoon and asked the man of about thirty-two to come and ask him, out loud, in front of people, and he said the whole of it in about a hundred and ten words, and about nine people heard all of it, and he was not thanked for it, and nothing was put on any sheet of paper that day.

Nobody in that harbor had asked that man anything since the sixteenth. There is no reason on any page of that leaf for it and none is going to be entered and the reason he has not been asked is a reason nobody has put in writing.

"I am not asking for the channel. **I am asking you to ask me whether I will take it, in the open, where people can hear the answer, and I am asking you to do it whether I say yes or no, and I want that on a page in a hand that is not mine.**"

He said that he had been in a room on the sixteenth and had not said a word, and that a man who says nothing in a room is a man who has agreed to whatever was decided, and that he had not agreed to anything because nobody had put anything to him, and that he had gone home that night and had not been able to say, to himself or to anybody, whether that was cowardice or whether it was that there was nothing there to be brave about.

"And I cannot read. That is the whole of it and I am not ashamed of it and I am not going to pretend to you. On that slip on Friday there is a rule and three names and nine words, and I could not read a word of it, and a man of about forty-six wrote me nine words out of it in a hand on a slate and I have got that in my pocket and I have read it eleven times, and the first of the three words of it is not my name. **I have been handed a smooth page and I have had one a year, and there is a way to be handed a smooth page without ever being asked anything, and I have found it, and it is standing still.**"

---

The man of about thirty-two stood at the end of that slip and did not answer it for about a minute, and about nine people stood there, and the man of about fifty-three who owns a boat of about fourteen tons was at the end of the dock and did not come over, and the woman of about forty-four who holds one of those three channels said nothing and went back to her store at about ten past two.

Then the man of about thirty-two said one thing and it was about thirty words and he did not say it twice, and it was not a promise and nobody in that slip called it one.

"I am not going to ask you today. Today is not the day and there are three names on that page and one question under the rule with no answer under it, and I am not going to add a man to that page in the middle of asking a man for a slate. **When I ask you it will be out loud, in the open, and it will be in front of these nine, and it will be on a page in a hand that is not yours, and I will not ask you twice if the first time is a no.**"

He said nothing back. Nobody in that slip said anything back to him. The man of about thirty-four stood there for about a quarter of an hour after the two of them had stopped talking, and then he went and did his own work, and nobody sent for him and nobody followed him and the asking stood where it had been put at one o'clock in the afternoon and it was still standing at half past five and it is not going to be closed before the first of February.

---

And at about half past five in the afternoon the man who keeps the leaf said the shortfall out loud at that bench stone and said in about twenty-five words that a page of hours is not the place a man gets asked in and that he was wrong about that on the sixteenth and had been wrong about it for thirteen days, and nobody argued it and nobody agreed with it and nobody thanked him for saying it.

"Ninety-six thousand and four hundred and fifty-four pence short, being six hundred and two marks, eleven shillings and twopence. Unmoved. **A man stood at the end of that slip at one o'clock this afternoon and asked to be asked, and the whole of his asking is that he did not want to be handed a smooth page without ever being put anything, and I have spent thirteen days writing rules on a page for people who are not on the page, and that is a smooth page with a hand on it.**"

Not one standing item in that harbor was paid, advanced, reduced, bettered, struck or closed on that Saturday, and no figure has been asked of any person in that harbor to make it otherwise, and nobody was thanked for anything on that day, and nobody was thanked for the asking and nobody was thanked for the not-asking and nobody was thanked for the thirty words on the slate that were read eleven times.

---

**A MAN OF ABOUT THIRTY-FOUR OF THE SECOND LAY ASKED OUT LOUD, IN ABOUT A HUNDRED AND TEN WORDS, IN FRONT OF ABOUT NINE PEOPLE, TO BE ASKED WHETHER HE WILL HOLD ONE OF THREE CHANNELS, WHETHER HE SAYS YES OR NO, AND TO HAVE THE ASKING AND THE ANSWER PUT ON A PAGE IN A HAND THAT IS NOT HIS, AND HE SAID OUT LOUD THAT HE CANNOT READ AND THAT HE HAS BEEN HANDED A SMOOTH PAGE FOR A YEAR, AND HE WAS NOT THANKED AND NOBODY WAS ASKED FOR ANYTHING. THE MAN OF ABOUT THIRTY-TWO DID NOT ASK HIM ON THAT DAY AND SAID OUT LOUD THAT HE IS NOT GOING TO, AND THAT WHEN HE DOES IT WILL BE IN THE OPEN IN FRONT OF THE SAME NINE, AND HE SAID NOTHING BACK AND WAS NOT CALLED ON FOR IT. THE ASKING WAS STILL STANDING AT THE CLOSE OF THAT DAY AND NOTHING WAS PUT ON ANY SHEET OF PAPER. THE SHEET THAT CARRIES THE MONEY IS NINETY-SIX THOUSAND AND FOUR HUNDRED AND FIFTY-FOUR PENCE SHORT AGAINST THAT YARD'S OWN BOOK, BEING SIX HUNDRED AND TWO MARKS, ELEVEN SHILLINGS AND TWOPENCE, AND THERE ARE TWENTY-EIGHT PENCE IN THE BAG UNDER THE SLATE.**

*First column, entered on Saturday the twenty-ninth day of January at about six in the evening in that shed: that the said day was not a rota day and was not a wage Tuesday and was not a wipe, and that no charge was placed on that cistern on it; and that a man of about thirty-four of the second lay, who was in the room over that market on the sixteenth of this month at the back and said nothing and has not been asked about it since, stood at the end of that slip at about one o'clock in the afternoon in front of about nine people and asked the man of about thirty-two to come and ask him out loud in the open whether he will hold one of three channels, whether he says yes or no, and to put the asking and the answer on a page in a hand that is not his, and that he said it in about a hundred and ten words; and that he said out loud that he was not asking for the channel, and that he had been in that room on the sixteenth and had said nothing and had gone home that night and had not been able to say whether that was cowardice or whether there was nothing there to be brave about, and that he cannot read, and that a man of about forty-six who mends nets wrote nine words of that page of hours out in a hand on a slate and that he has read it eleven times and that the first of those three words is not his name, and that he has been handed a smooth page for a year and has found a way to be handed a smooth page without ever being asked anything; and that the man of about thirty-two said in about thirty words at about ten past one in the afternoon that he is not going to ask him that day, that the day is not the day, and that there are three names on that page and one question under the rule with no answer under it, and that when he does ask him it will be out loud in the open in front of those same nine people and will be on a page in a hand that is not his, and that he will not ask him twice if the first time is a no, and that that is not a promise and nobody in that slip called it one; and that the man of about thirty-two said nothing back and that nobody in that slip said anything back to him and that the woman of about forty-four who holds one of those three channels said nothing and went back to her store at about ten past two, and that the man of about fifty-three did not come over from the end of that dock; and that the asking was still standing at half past five and nothing was put on any sheet of paper that day about it and nobody was sent for and nobody was thanked; and that one hundred and sixty-eight pence came into that yard and one hundred and sixty-eight pence went out of it, and that twenty-eight plus one hundred and sixty-eight less one hundred and sixty-eight is twenty-eight pence at the close of the said day, being two shillings and fourpence, being short of the wage of four marks by six hundred and twelve pence, being three marks eleven shillings; and that that sheet stood at one hundred and seven thousand and ninety-four pence out and ten thousand six hundred and forty pence in and is ninety-six thousand and four hundred and fifty-four pence short, being six hundred and two marks, eleven shillings and twopence; and that nothing has been in a tin in that shed since the twenty-ninth day of April past.*

*Second column, not checkable from a mole, entered on the said Saturday at about six in the evening in that shed, and no name is against it: that the reason a day of the berth fund was paid on that Saturday and not on a fifth day of the week is his own, and is that a fund with a day in chalk pays a day for a day and that the two men who carried that water about the quay that Saturday did it at a quarter past ten in the morning and were not asked whether it was a working day; and that the reason the man of about thirty-four's asking went in no column and on no page is his own, and is that the first column takes one entry a day and the entry for a day is the day, and that a man asking to be asked is a thing that happened and not a thing that this leaf holds, and that the only place in that harbor where it is written down is nine words on the back of a slate in a man's pocket and that slate is a person and not a record; and that the reason the man of about thirty-two called his own thirteen days of page-writing a smooth page with a hand on it is his own, and is that a page of hours with three names on it and a rule about who may stop it is a page a man who cannot read can be shown nine words of and not the rest of, and that the rest of it is the part with his own name at the top of it; and that the reason nobody in that slip said anything back is theirs and not his, and is that about nine people had watched a man ask for the right to say no and not one of them had anything to give him, and that a man who has asked for that in the open in front of nine people has spent the whole of what he had, and that the second half of it is quiet, and that the quiet is not rudeness, and that it is nine people who have nothing to add and that the nothing to add is the correct answer to a man who has just asked for the right to say no.*
