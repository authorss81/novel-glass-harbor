# Chapter 575

*Tuesday the Fifteenth Day of February, the Sixty-Ninth Wage Tuesday, Not a Rota Day and Not a Wipe and No Charge Placed on That Cistern on It, the Wage Column on the Step at the Back of That Market Read Out at About Ten in the Morning in Four Minutes Before About Forty People, Standing at Twenty-Six Lines and One Hundred and Four Marks With Twenty Envelopes in a Chest and Not Moving, and the Sixty-Ninth Week Standing Fallen Due at the Close of the Day, a Page Made in That Shed at About Two o'Clock in the Afternoon Putting a Season's Work Up as Hours Off Crossings, Being Six Times Four Times Twenty-Six Being Six Hundred and Twenty-Four Crossings and Three Times Six Hundred and Twenty-Four Being One Thousand Eight Hundred and Seventy-Two Minutes and That Being Thirty-One Hours and Twelve Minutes and Three Days and Seven Hours and Twelve Minutes, Three Times One Hundred and Sixty-Eight Being Five Hundred and Four Pence and Not One Penny of It Going Onto That Sheet, the Shortfall Said Out Loud at About Half Past Five Unmoved at Ninety-Six Thousand Six Hundred and Twenty-Three Pence, Being Six Hundred and Three Marks, Eleven Shillings and Elevenpence, the Bag at Twenty-Eight Pence, Being Two Shillings and Fourpence*

Tuesday the fifteenth day of February, in that harbor, in about four hundred yards of water and along a stone quay, and in a shed with a roof on it, a bench stone, and about nine people standing in it at two o'clock in the afternoon.

**The said Tuesday was the sixty-ninth wage Tuesday and was not a rota day and was not a wipe, and no charge was placed on that cistern on it. The wage column in that harbor was read out at about ten in the morning and did not move, and nobody was paid a penny out of that chest, and a further week stands fallen due at the close of the said day, being the sixty-ninth. Nothing went into that sheet in any hand in any line on that day and nothing came off it in any hand, and the page of hours that was made in that shed at about two o'clock in the afternoon is a page of hours and is not on that sheet and is not a line on it. One hundred and sixty-eight pence came into that yard at about ten in the morning and one hundred and sixty-eight pence went out of it at about four in the afternoon, and one hundred and sixty-eight pence is one mark and eightpence, and there are twenty-eight pence in the bag at the close of the said day, being two shillings and fourpence, and the figure that bag is short of the wage of four marks is six hundred and twelve pence, being three marks eleven shillings. That sheet stands at one hundred and seven thousand two hundred and sixty-three pence out, being six hundred and seventy marks, five shillings and threepence, and ten thousand six hundred and forty pence in, being sixty-six marks, six shillings and eightpence, and it is ninety-six thousand six hundred and twenty-three pence short, being six hundred and three marks, eleven shillings and elevenpence, and that figure is against that yard's own book and was said out loud in that yard's own mouth and was not answered by anybody. Nothing has been in a tin in that shed since the twenty-ninth day of April past, and no penny of the whole of that day came out of that bag, out of the owner of any boat, out of the man with two papers, or out of the Crown.**

**The nine that a stranger in that harbor could have read off any wall are the nine below, and not one of them was found by looking: the notice of the seventeenth of May past, two hundred and seventy-three days; the bellwright of that harbor, not asked for two hundred and seventy-five; the two objects of nobody's on that crown, six hundred and thirteen days; the bench under that pump, bare for the three hundred and seventy-ninth night; the two lights at about a mile off that point, one hundred and ninety-seven days; the six-line sheet at the back of that end stall, its hundred and fifty-second day; the paper of four lines in Fish Street, its hundred and sixty-seventh; the nine-line sheet with a seal on it in that front street, its hundred and eighty-fourth; and a man of about fifty-one who has said nothing to anybody about a page of six names for two hundred and fifteen days. The figure of four hundred and fifty pence has not come down that board since the first of December and nothing standing in that harbor was paid or advanced or cut back or bettered or struck or closed on that day. The harbour returns of the vessels engaged in that line are being asked for a second time in that basket, the basket going down that coast on the Saturday. There are nine hulls about two sea miles off the head of that mole this afternoon. A man of about twenty-nine who came home on that boat can hear out of the left side of his head and it has been seventy-three days and no figure has been set against it and he is not on the water and nobody has sent for him.**

---

The column was read out on that step from ten o'clock in the morning and it took four minutes, and the reading was done by a man of about thirty-one of the second lay who reads it every Tuesday and has read it sixty-nine times, and about forty people were on the boards and about eleven of them could not see a line.

He read twenty-six lines and one hundred and four marks off the lid of that step, and the chest with the twenty envelopes in it stood shut behind that door from ten until four, and when he came to the end of the twenty-sixth line he stopped and said the figure and said that the figure had not moved, and that is what he says.

The sixty-ninth week stands fallen due at the close of that day. It stands behind the sixty-eighth, which stands behind the sixty-seventh, and nobody in that market said a word about any of them except the man reading, who says it every Tuesday because it is on his list.

---

At about two o'clock in the afternoon the person whose yard it is put a page on that bench stone, and about nine people were in that shed, and she prices hours and material and nobody in that room had asked her for it and she had said in about eleven words before she started that she was not pricing a refusal and that the two of those are different jobs.

Here is the whole of the page.

A season is twenty-six weeks. Six men keep that mark. The crossings come in four days a week. Six times four is twenty-four crossings a week, and twenty-four times twenty-six is six hundred and twenty-four crossings in a season.

A crossing takes that man's hand and his eye and about a minute and a half of the man at the harbour end, being three minutes all told, and three times six hundred and twenty-four is one thousand eight hundred and seventy-two minutes.

One thousand eight hundred and seventy-two minutes is thirty-one hours and twelve minutes, and a day's work in that harbor is eight hours, and three times eight is twenty-four, and thirty-one hours and twelve minutes less twenty-four hours is seven hours and twelve minutes. So a season of those crossings is **three days and seven hours and twelve minutes** of one man and one man at the harbour end, and it is the same figure every year and it does not depend on which boats come in.

Then she wrote the money underneath it and did not write it on the sheet.

Three times one hundred and sixty-eight pence is five hundred and four pence, being three marks and two shillings, and that is the three days a man can sell. **The seven hours and twelve minutes is not on that sheet and is not going onto that sheet, and no figure in this country prices it, and I am not going to invent one and put it under a man's name.**

A man of about thirty-three who hires hands said out loud, in about seventy-eight words, that that yard could have paid a day for it on the Saturday and that there were twenty-six weeks of it and that a man does not turn down five hundred and four pence.

"A day is a day and I would pay it. But I cannot check it. **A stranger with a leaf and a pencil can take that line off that slate in a minute, and I have never once in my life been able to check seven hours of a man's own eye on a dark morning, and I am not going to be the man who pretends the second of those is worth what the first one is.**"

Nobody argued with her. A man of about forty-four of the first lay was in that shed for the whole of it and was asked once whether the arithmetic was right, and he said it was right, and that is all he said in that room on that afternoon.

---

The page went onto that bench stone at about a quarter to three and stayed on it until the middle of the next morning, and about nine people looked at it, and about four of them could not read the six on the left of it and were told what was on the left of it, and nobody copied it out and nobody put a name on it and it is not on that sheet and it is not on the sheet of four heads in the room over that market and it is not in any minute.

And at about half past five on that Tuesday the man who keeps the leaf said the shortfall out loud at that bench stone and used about two hundred and five words on what a season of a man's own labour looks like when it is written down in hours, and nobody in that slip asked for the four hundred and fifty pence to be taken off any board.

"Ninety-six thousand six hundred and twenty-three pence short, being six hundred and three marks, eleven shillings and elevenpence. Unmoved, and the column was read out on that step at ten and did not move and the sixty-ninth week stands fallen due behind it, and that has happened sixty-nine times.

**A man's whole season of crossings came out of that shed this afternoon as three days and seven hours and twelve minutes, and the three days can be sold and the seven hours and twelve minutes cannot, and the difference between those two is the whole of what a person is worth on this coast and it does not fit in a line.** I have had nothing to write on that sheet for thirteen days and I have a page on that bench stone with a multiplication on it, and the multiplication is right and it checks, and it is the only arithmetic in this harbor this month that I can say is right without asking anybody.

I am not going to put it on. A page that a stranger can check and a page that nobody can check have to live in different places and this shed is the wrong place for both of them."

---

**THE WAGE COLUMN WAS READ OUT ON THAT STEP AT ABOUT TEN IN THE MORNING IN FOUR MINUTES BEFORE ABOUT FORTY PEOPLE AND IT STOOD AT TWENTY-SIX LINES AND ONE HUNDRED AND FOUR MARKS AND DID NOT MOVE, AND NOBODY WAS PAID A PENNY OUT OF THAT CHEST, AND THE SIXTY-NINTH WEEK STANDS FALLEN DUE AT THE CLOSE OF THAT DAY. A SEASON'S WORK WAS PUT ON A PAGE IN THAT SHED AT ABOUT TWO O'CLOCK IN THE AFTERNOON AS HOURS OFF CROSSINGS, BEING SIX MEN AT FOUR DAYS A WEEK FOR TWENTY-SIX WEEKS, BEING SIX HUNDRED AND TWENTY-FOUR CROSSINGS, AND THREE MINUTES A CROSSING BEING ONE THOUSAND EIGHT HUNDRED AND SEVENTY-TWO MINUTES, BEING THIRTY-ONE HOURS AND TWELVE MINUTES, BEING THREE DAYS AND SEVEN HOURS AND TWELVE MINUTES AT EIGHT HOURS A DAY. THREE TIMES ONE HUNDRED AND SIXTY-EIGHT PENCE IS FIVE HUNDRED AND FOUR PENCE, BEING THREE MARKS AND TWO SHILLINGS, AND THE SEVEN HOURS AND TWELVE MINUTES IS NOT ON THAT SHEET AND IS NOT GOING ONTO THAT SHEET AND NO FIGURE IN THAT COUNTRY PRICES IT. A MAN OF ABOUT THIRTY-THREE WHO HIRES HANDS SAID THAT HE WOULD PAY A DAY FOR IT AND THAT HE CANNOT CHECK IT, AND A MAN OF ABOUT FORTY-FOUR SAID THE ARITHMETIC IS RIGHT AND SAID NOTHING ELSE. THE PAGE STAYED ON THAT BENCH STONE AND WAS NOT COPIED AND CARRIES NO NAME AND IS ON NO OTHER SHEET. NOBODY WAS THANKED. THAT SHEET IS NINETY-SIX THOUSAND SIX HUNDRED AND TWENTY-THREE PENCE SHORT ON THAT YARD'S OWN BOOK, AND THE FIGURE IN THE BAG UNDER THE SLATE IS TWENTY-EIGHT PENCE, BEING TWO SHILLINGS AND FOURPENCE.**

and that that sheet stood at one hundred and seven thousand two hundred and sixty-three pence out and ten thousand six hundred and forty pence in and is ninety-six thousand six hundred and twenty-three pence short, being six hundred and three marks, eleven shillings and elevenpence, and that nothing went into that sheet in any hand in any line on that day, an hour of a man's own eye not being a thing that yard prices.*

*Second column, not checkable from a mole, entered on the said Tuesday at about six in the evening in that shed, and no name is against it: that at about ten in the morning the reason he wrote four minutes down as the length of that reading is his own, and is that a reading is the only thing in that harbor that happens every week and takes the same time, and that a thing which takes the same time for sixty-nine weeks running and costs four minutes is the cheapest instrument in that harbor and the only one nobody has ever proposed replacing; and that at about two in the afternoon the reason he let the page be made and did not make it himself is his own, and is that he does not price hours and that the person who does was standing in that room, and that a man who gets a figure from a person who prices things for a living gets a figure that was argued over before he ever saw it, and that is the whole difference between a figure and a guess; and that at about a quarter to three the reason the page was left lying on that bench stone until the middle of the next morning instead of being put away is his own, and is that a page that goes into a drawer on the day it is made is a page nobody will ever find out is wrong, and that a season of a man's crossings is the first thing this harbor has produced in a month that is worth finding out is wrong, because the man it belongs to will be asked about it at the end of a winter by somebody; and that at about half past five the reason he said that the multiplication is the only arithmetic he can say is right without asking anybody is his own, and is that six times four times twenty-six is the third thing this month that has come out right, and that the other two are three figures of ninety-four seconds and a count of nine, and that all three of them are about other people and none of them is about him, and that he has now been the man who keeps a leaf for nineteen days and has not once entered a figure of his own labour on any instrument in this harbor and is not going to; and that at about ten past six the reason the seven hours and twelve minutes is on a bench stone and not in a room is his own, and is that it was said out loud in that shed at about two o'clock in front of about nine people and about four of them could not read it and were told it, and that a figure told to eleven people in a market by somebody else is a figure that belongs to the teller by the end of the day, and that the only way to keep this one belonging to the man it is about is to leave it on a stone where anybody can come and look at it and where nobody has to stand up and say it again.*
