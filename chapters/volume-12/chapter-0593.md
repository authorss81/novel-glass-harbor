# Chapter 593

*Saturday the Fifth Day of March, Not a Rota Day and Not a Wage Tuesday and Not a Wipe and No Charge Placed on That Cistern on It, Four Lines Going onto a Sheet at the End Stall at the Back of That Market at About Ten in the Morning in a Large Hand, Being What Is Not Said, Who Is Not Saying It, What They Are Entitled to Keep and What Would Have to Happen for It to Be Opened, Four Gaps Printed Against That Coast This Day and Each of the Four Carrying All Four Things, About Eleven People of That Market Told What Was on It by a Man of About Forty-Six Who Mends Nets and Nobody Asked to Agree to Any of It, the Foot of That Sheet Carrying in Its Own Words That a Person Who Cannot See a Gap Is a Person Who Has Been Given a Smooth Page, a Man of About Thirty-Three Who Hires Hands Asking in About one hundred and six Words Who Decides Which Gaps Get Printed and a Woman of Thirty of No Office Answering in About one hundred and sixty-five Words, the Shortfall Said Out Loud at About Half Past Five in About four hundred and fifty-four Words Unmoved at Ninety-Seven Thousand Six Hundred and Thirty-Six Pence, Being Six Hundred and Ten Marks and Three Shillings, the Bag at Twenty-Eight Pence, Being Two Shillings and Fourpence*

Saturday the fifth day of March, in that harbor, in about four hundred yards of water and along a stone quay, and at the end stall at the back of that market from six in the morning with about nine people on the boards at ten.

**The said Saturday was not a rota day and was not a wage Tuesday and was not a wipe, and no charge was placed on that cistern on it, and the covers were on it all day. Nothing went into that sheet in any hand in any line on that day and nothing came off it in any hand, and the four lines that went up in a large hand at that end stall are on no sheet in this harbor and are in no minute, and neither is anything printed against them. Into that yard came a hundred and sixty-eight pence at about ten in the morning and out of it went a hundred and sixty-eight pence at about four in the afternoon, a hundred and sixty-eight pence being one mark and eightpence, and twenty-eight pence lay in the bag at the close of the said day, being two shillings and fourpence, which is three marks eleven shillings, being six hundred and twelve pence, behind a wage of four marks. That sheet stands at one hundred and eight thousand two hundred and seventy-six pence out, being six hundred and seventy-six marks, nine shillings and eightpence, and at ten thousand six hundred and forty pence in, being sixty-six marks, six shillings and eightpence, and is ninety-seven thousand six hundred and thirty-six pence short, being six hundred and ten marks and three shillings, and that figure sits in that yard's own book, was spoken out loud in that yard's own mouth, and drew no answer. There is nothing in a tin in that shed and there has not been since the twenty-ninth day of April past, and not a penny of the cost of that day was drawn from that bag, or from the owner of any boat, or from the man with two papers, or from the Crown, and that includes the whole of what four gaps cost anybody, which was nothing, and that is not the same as what four gaps will cost.**

**The nine that a stranger in that harbor could have had off any wall in a morning are these, and not one of them was found by looking: the notice of the seventeenth of May past, two hundred and ninety-one days; the bellwright of that harbor, not asked for two hundred and ninety-three; the two objects of nobody's on that crown, six hundred and thirty-one days; the bench under that pump, bare for the three hundred and ninety-seventh night; the two lights at about a mile off that point, two hundred and fifteen days; the six-line sheet at the back of that end stall, its hundred and seventieth day; the paper of four lines in Fish Street, its hundred and eighty-fifth; the nine-line sheet with a seal on it in that front street, its hundred and ninety-second; and a man of about fifty-one who has said nothing to anybody about a page of six names for two hundred and thirty-three days. The board at the head of that mole carries five papers, of which two have never been opened by anybody in this harbor, and the basket with the second asking in it is still standing in that boathouse with the paper in it. Nine hulls lie about two sea miles off the head of that mole this afternoon and have lain there since the twenty-first of January. A man of about twenty-nine who came home on that boat can hear out of the left side of his head and it has been ninety-one days and no figure has been set against it and he is not on the water and nobody has sent for him.**

---

That end stall opens at six and the fish came in off one boat before seven on a Saturday and off a second boat at about half past eight, and the weight went on the beam both times and the chalk figure on the lid of that slate has been in the same place since the first of December and does not know what day of the week it is.

A boy of twenty-two stood on a stool at that stall from about half past six and wrote the weights into a book as they came over the beam, and nobody said anything to him about the water and nobody asked him where he had been and he is not on the water.

---

At about ten in the morning the woman of thirty of no office nailed a sheet onto the upright post at the end of those boards and wrote on it in a large hand, in front of about nine people, so that a man standing at it can read it without going close. She wrote four lines at the head of it and there are four of them.

1. *What is not said.*
2. *Who is not saying it.*
3. *What they are entitled to keep.*
4. *What would have to happen for it to be opened.*

She said out loud, before she wrote any of the four, that nothing on that sheet is agreed to by anybody in this harbor, and that no person is to be asked anything, and that the four lines are not a form and are not an order and are not anybody's list of anybody.

Under those four lines she printed four gaps, and there are four of them, and every one of the four carries all four of the lines above it. They were entered by four different people in four different mouths, and the man of about forty-six who mends nets on the third lay wrote them in and then read the whole of it out loud twice in front of about nine people, of whom about four could not read it, and about eleven people of that market at large were told what was on it by him.

**One.** *What was in that boat of the third lay before she went out on the second of this month.* Who is not saying it: the five who were in her. What they are entitled to keep: what she carried and who stowed it. What would have to happen for it to be opened: the five of them and the man who owns her, in one room at one hour, and one of the five agreeing out loud, and nobody being asked a second time.

**Two.** *What is on the fourth mark.* Who is not saying it: nobody, because there is no person on that mark to say it. What they are entitled to keep: the fact that there is no person on it. What would have to happen for it to be opened: a person taking the keeping of it, which is not going to happen this month and is not anybody's business to ask about.

**Three.** *What the port four days down that coast is not saying about the crossing column it has kept in one book in one hand for eleven years.* Who is not saying it: the man who keeps that book, and the people who have read it and gone away. What they are entitled to keep: eleven years of an hour. What would have to happen for it to be opened: the keeper being asked by somebody, and being free to refuse, and being free to refuse twice without anybody remarking on it.

**Four.** *What the place about nine miles up that coast has not said about the two notes heard in three harbors on the night of the twenty-second of January.* Who is not saying it: that place. What they are entitled to keep: what they heard and in what order and by whom. What would have to happen for it to be opened: a person in that place saying it in their own mouth in a working place on a working day, and the day going on the foot of the paper in a hand that is not a clerk's.

At the foot of that sheet, in the same hand and under a line, is one sentence, and it is the only sentence on that sheet that is about a person.

*A person who cannot see a gap is a person who has been given a smooth page, and a smooth page is a page that cannot be argued with, and a page that cannot be argued with has already won something and nobody had to do anything to win it.*

Nobody was asked for the rest of it and nobody was thanked for four gaps. That sheet stayed on that post until about four in the afternoon, and when the wind came round off the water about three it lifted at the top corner and a man of about fifty-six who lands fish put a stone on the bottom edge of it and left it there, and the stone is not on any sheet in that harbor.

---

A man of about thirty-three who hires hands asked one question about those four lines and asked it in about 106 words, standing on the boards with the sheet behind him.

"**Who decides which gaps get printed.** That is the whole of my objection to it and I have had it since about ten past ten. You have put four gaps on a sheet at a fish stall, which is four more than there were on Monday, and I can see the shape of the next four, and the next four will be a list of everybody in this harbor who has not said something, and a list like that is the fourth of those six lines of chalk in the opposite order, and I do not want one of those in this harbor in my lifetime."

The woman of thirty of no office answered him in about 165 words and did not unpin the sheet.

"**Nobody decides, and that is not a gap in it, that is the whole of the design, and I have been waiting three days for somebody to ask me that and you have saved me a morning.** A gap is printed by the people the gap belongs to and by nobody else. A gap four days down that coast cannot be printed by me and cannot be printed by you and cannot be printed by this harbor on their behalf this month, and the one that is on that sheet about the fourth mark was entered by the man who landed fish at that stall because he crews that mark and not by anybody who thought it would be a good thing to have on a page. If a place does not come and print its own gap, then this harbor has one fewer thing to argue with, and that is the correct number of things to argue with on any morning of the week."

He said he would take the answer and would not take it as settled, and about four people read that sheet twice and about nine people did not read it at all, and nobody's name went on it, and it is not in any book.

And at about half past five on that Saturday the man who keeps the leaf said the shortfall out loud at that bench stone and used about 454 words on four gaps printed in a large hand at a fish stall, and nobody in that slip was asked for a fifth.

"Ninety-seven thousand six hundred and thirty-six pence short, being six hundred and ten marks and three shillings. Unmoved, and nothing went onto that sheet this morning and nothing came off it, and the bag is twenty-eight pence, and no charge went on that cistern and the covers were on it all day, and the four gaps that went up at that end stall cost nothing and are worth nothing and that is not the same thing, and I will come back to the difference between those two at some point in the next five days and probably get it wrong.

**Four things were printed against every gap on that coast this morning, being what is not said, who is not saying it, what they are entitled to keep, and what would have to happen for it to be opened, and four gaps were entered against them, and about eleven people of that market were told what was on the sheet instead of reading it.** The last line of that sheet is that a person who cannot see a gap is a person who has been given a smooth page, and that is the sentence this whole month has been walking towards and nobody in this harbor wrote it and I did not write it either, a woman of thirty of no office wrote it in a large hand on a nail and went to sell net.

Three of those four gaps are about a boat, a mark and a port, and the fourth of them is about a place nine miles up that coast, and every one of the four can be checked by asking the people it belongs to whether it is on the sheet in their own words, and not one of the four of them can be checked off this bench stone, and that is what a gap is for. Nobody decided which four got printed. The people the gaps belong to printed them and nobody else can, and if a place does not come and print its own gap then this harbor has one fewer thing to be angry about and that is the correct number of things to be angry about on a wet Saturday in March.

Nothing went onto the third column today and nothing onto the fourth, and the fourth stands at one hundred and twenty-five with no hundred and twenty-sixth line in it, and nobody in this harbor is going to be told where that reason is from me. I put one line in on the first column and five on the second at about six this evening, and I have not read the first two of those figures out loud in any room this month."

Nobody was thanked for the arithmetic and nobody was thanked for four gaps, and that shed stood empty by about six and the door stood open on a slip with nobody on it, and the sheet with four lines on it was still on the post at the end of those boards with a stone against the bottom edge of it.

A MAN OF ABOUT THIRTY-THREE ASKED IN ABOUT 106 WORDS WHO DECIDES WHICH GAPS GET PRINTED, AND A WOMAN OF THIRTY OF NO OFFICE ANSWERED IN ABOUT 165 WORDS THAT NOBODY DECIDES, AND THAT A GAP IS PRINTED BY THE PEOPLE THE GAP BELONGS TO AND BY NOBODY ELSE, AND THAT IF A PLACE DOES NOT COME AND PRINT ITS OWN GAP THEN THIS HARBOR HAS ONE FEWER THING TO ARGUE WITH AND THAT IS THE CORRECT NUMBER OF THINGS TO ARGUE WITH ON ANY MORNING OF THE WEEK. NOBODY WAS ASKED FOR THE REST OF IT AND NOBODY WAS THANKED FOR FOUR GAPS, AND NOBODY'S NAME WENT ON THAT SHEET. THE SHEET THAT CARRIES THE MONEY IS NINETY-SEVEN THOUSAND SIX HUNDRED AND THIRTY-SIX PENCE SHORT AGAINST THAT YARD'S OWN BOOK, BEING SIX HUNDRED AND TEN MARKS AND THREE SHILLINGS, AND THERE ARE TWENTY-EIGHT PENCE IN THE BAG UNDER THE SLATE.**
