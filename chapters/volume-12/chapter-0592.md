# Chapter 592

*Friday the Fourth Day of March, Not a Rota Day and Not a Wage Tuesday and Not a Wipe and No Charge Placed on That Cistern on It, a Woman of Thirty of No Office Teaching a Counter-Plan Out Loud at About Ten in the Morning in That Shed in About Nine People in About three hundred and seventy Words and Not One of Those Nine Agreeing to It, a Man of About Thirty-Three Who Hires Hands Asking in About one hundred and thirty Words Whether Being Taught a Thing and Agreeing to a Thing Are the Same, and a Page of Hours Going onto That Bench Stone at About Ten in the Morning With an Hour Against Each of Its Six Parts, Read Out Twice, Saying in Its Own Words What a Gap Is and What a Gap Is Not, the Shortfall Said Out Loud at About Half Past Five in About four hundred and ninety-seven Words Unmoved at Ninety-Seven Thousand Six Hundred and Thirty-Six Pence, Being Six Hundred and Ten Marks and Three Shillings, the Bag at Twenty-Eight Pence, Being Two Shillings and Fourpence*

Friday the fourth day of March, in that harbor, in about four hundred yards of water and along a stone quay, and in a shed with a roof on it, a bench stone, and about nine people standing in it at ten o'clock in the morning.

**The said Friday was not a rota day and was not a wage Tuesday and was not a wipe, and no charge was placed on that cistern on it, being a Monday and a Thursday thing, and the covers were on it at eight in the morning and were on it at four in the afternoon. Nothing went into that sheet in any hand in any line on that day and nothing came off it in any hand, and the page of hours that went onto that bench stone at about ten in the morning is on no sheet in this harbor and is in no minute and carries no name at the head of it and no signature at the foot of it. A hundred and sixty-eight pence came over the threshold of that yard at about ten in the morning and a hundred and sixty-eight pence went back out of it at about four in the afternoon, a hundred and sixty-eight pence being one mark and eightpence, and the bag closed that day with twenty-eight pence in it, being two shillings and fourpence, which is six hundred and twelve pence, being three marks eleven shillings, behind the wage of four marks. That sheet stands at one hundred and eight thousand two hundred and seventy-six pence out, being six hundred and seventy-six marks, nine shillings and eightpence, against ten thousand six hundred and forty pence in, being sixty-six marks, six shillings and eightpence, so that it is ninety-seven thousand six hundred and thirty-six pence short, being six hundred and ten marks and three shillings, and that figure is carried in that yard's own book, was given out loud in that yard's own mouth this evening, and was answered by nobody. Nothing has gone into a tin in that shed since the twenty-ninth day of April past, and not one penny of what that day cost came out of that bag, or out of the owner of any boat, or out of the man with two papers, or out of the Crown.**

**The nine that a stranger in that harbor could have read off any wall are the nine below, and not one of them was found by looking: the notice of the seventeenth of May past, two hundred and ninety days; the bellwright of that harbor, not asked for two hundred and ninety-two; the two objects of nobody's on that crown, six hundred and thirty days; the bench under that pump, bare for the three hundred and ninety-sixth night; the two lights at about a mile off that point, two hundred and fourteen days; the six-line sheet at the back of that end stall, its hundred and sixty-ninth day; the paper of four lines in Fish Street, its hundred and eighty-fourth; the nine-line sheet with a seal on it in that front street, its hundred and ninety-first; and a man of about fifty-one who has said nothing to anybody about a page of six names for two hundred and thirty-two days. The board at the head of that mole carries five papers and two of them have never been opened, and the basket with the second asking in it is still standing in that boathouse with the paper in it unopened. There are nine hulls about two sea miles off the head of that mole this afternoon. A man of about twenty-nine who came home on that boat can hear out of the left side of his head and it has been ninety days and no figure has been set against it and he is not on the water and nobody has sent for him.**

That market opens at six on a Friday and by seven there were two boats' fish over the beam at the end stall and a boy of twenty-two on a stool writing the weights into a book, and nobody said anything to him about the water and nobody asked him where he had been and he is not on the water.

---

And at about ten in the morning the woman of thirty of no office stood on the boards at the back of that market with about nine people round her and taught them a counter-plan out loud, and it took her about 370 words, and not one of the nine agreed to it.

"**I am going to say this once and I am going to say it in the open and about half of you cannot read it and I am going to say it slowly twice, and I am not asking anybody to agree to a single part of it and I am not going to ask again.** The name of it is a counter-plan. Here is the whole of it. First: a community that wants its own hours checked by anybody who turns up writes down what it is willing to have checked, in the hands of the people who keep it, and no office puts a seal on it. Second: every place a community is not willing to have checked gets four things printed against it, and I am not telling you what the four are today. Third: nothing goes up at any hour except the hour, and the hour is on the page, and if a place wants a different hour it says so before the morning and not on it. Fourth: a community that has not agreed is named as having not agreed and is not asked a second time and is not argued with. Fifth: nothing in this may be answered by one person for anybody else, because a thing somebody wrote is a thing and it cannot be asked anything. Sixth: any person in this harbor who cannot read may have a page read out to them in a working place on a working day, and the day goes on the foot of the paper in a hand that is not the clerk's.

That is the whole of it. **Nobody in this shed has agreed to it, nobody has refused it, and the difference between those two is the whole reason I am standing here in the open instead of in a room with a table.** I have taught it to about nine people this morning and I will teach it to any person who asks me for it and I will not take a name against it from anybody, and the first of those two is a thing you can check and the second of them is a thing I have given up on purpose."

A man of about thirty-three who hires hands asked her one question and it was the right one, and he asked it in about 130 words.

"You have taught nine people a thing in about four minutes and nobody has agreed to it, and I want to be sure I have got this the right way round, because I have watched about nine people agree to something in this market in about a minute and I did not enjoy it. If I have learned it, am I in it. If I have heard it and I go home and I do nothing with it, am I still in it. And if the answer is that I am in it by having heard it, then you have just built the same smooth thing in a shed and called it a method, and I would rather be in the old one where I can see what it is."

She answered him in about 124 words without moving off the boards.

"**You are not in it. You have learned it and that is all that has happened to you this morning, and I put those two in that order on purpose and I have watched nine people in this harbor go the other way round for eleven weeks.** A person learns a thing and then decides, or a person decides and then learns, and the second of those is how this coast ended up with a page nobody can argue with. If you go home and do nothing with it then you have still learned it, and that is a thing worth more than a signature, and I have no power to make you do anything and there is nobody in this harbor who can."

He said that he had not been able to find the flaw in that and that he would keep looking for it, and about four people in that circle said out loud that they had understood it, and none of the nine said the word agreed.

At about ten in the morning a page of hours went onto that bench stone, and it had six parts on it and an hour against every one of the six, and the man of about forty-six who mends nets on the third lay read the whole of it out loud twice and got nothing wrong on either reading.

1. *About ten in the morning: this was taught out loud in this shed, in about nine people, with the market trading underneath it, and nobody in that room agreed to any part of it and nobody refused it.*
2. *About half past ten: a question was asked in this room and it was what a gap is and what a gap is not, and it is answered in the words of the last of these six parts and not in the words of anybody in this room.*
3. *About a quarter to eleven: the six parts of the counter-plan were written out with an hour against each of them, and the writing is not an agreement and the hour is not a promise.*
4. *About eleven o'clock: every person in this room who cannot read what is in that hand was told what is on that page by the man of about forty-six, and about four of about nine could not read it.*
5. *About half past eleven: that page was read out a second time in the same words, and the day of that reading went on the foot of that page in a hand that is not the clerk's, and the paper went back to the woman of thirty of no office and into the inside of a coat.*
6. *About noon: that page went on the flat of that bench stone with nothing at the head of it and nothing at the foot of it, and it is not on any sheet in this harbor.*

Under those six, the page carries what a gap is and what a gap is not, in the same hand and in the same words both times it was read.

*A gap is a thing a person or a community is entitled to keep and has not given up. A gap is not an omission. A gap is not a mistake. A gap is not a thing done in a hurry, and nobody has to account for a gap to anybody. What a gap is not: it is not a hole cut by a person who did not want the trouble of writing a name, and it is not the place where a record stops being written, and it does not stand for anybody being wrong, and it is not a thing anybody has to be quiet about. A gap with its edges named is a thing a stranger can go and be angry at. A gap with its edges not named is a thing a stranger cannot find at all, and that is the one that gets filled in by somebody else inside three years.*

Nobody was asked for the rest of it and nobody was thanked for learning it out loud. The page went onto the bench stone at about noon and stayed there until about four in the afternoon, and the two men who carry that cistern up that ladder came past the door of that shed at about eleven and one of them read the last of those six parts over the shoulder of the other one and neither of them said anything about it afterwards.

And at about half past five on that Friday the man who keeps the leaf said the shortfall out loud at that bench stone and used about 497 words on a page of hours and on what a gap is, and nobody in that slip was asked whether a gap is a hole.

"Ninety-seven thousand six hundred and thirty-six pence short, being six hundred and ten marks and three shillings. Unmoved, and nothing went onto that sheet this morning and nothing came off it, and the bag is twenty-eight pence, and no charge went on that cistern and the covers were on it at eight and on it at four.

**A woman of thirty of no office taught a counter-plan out loud on the boards at the back of that market at ten this morning to about nine people, and not one of the nine agreed to it, and she said out loud that the whole reason she was standing there was that being taught and agreeing are two different things and that the second of those is how this coast ended up with a page nobody can argue with.** That is a method and not a body and it binds a community that takes it and nobody else, and a man of about thirty-three tried for about a hundred words to find the flaw in that and could not and said so out loud, and about four people said they had understood it and not one of the nine said the word agreed.

A page of hours went onto that bench stone at about ten with an hour against each of six parts and it was read out twice and it says in its own words what a gap is and what a gap is not, and the last line of it is that a gap with its edges named is a thing a stranger can go and be angry at and a gap with its edges not named is a thing a stranger cannot find at all. **Nobody in that shed learned a thing and got asked to sign a thing, and there is no name at the head of that page and no signature at the foot of it and the day of the reading went on the foot of the paper in a hand that is not a clerk's.**

The third of those columns took nothing today and the fourth took nothing and is at one hundred and twenty-five with no hundred and twenty-sixth line in it, and what is behind that is not on a page of mine and is not going on one this week. One line went in on the first column and five on the second at about six this evening, and neither of the first two figures of that leaf has been read out loud in a room this month. I entered one line on the first column and five on the second at about six this evening, and I have not read either of the first two figures of that leaf out loud in any room this month, and a man of about thirty-one came up that coast on Saturday with a no in his own mouth and the page that carries it is behind a press in this shed."

Nobody was thanked for the arithmetic and nobody was thanked for the six parts, and the shed stood empty by about six and the door stood open on a slip with nobody on it, and the wedge was still on the corner of that bench stone and the jar with the pin in it was still on the shelf with three other things in it.
