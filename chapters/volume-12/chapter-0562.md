# Chapter 562

*Wednesday the Second Day of February, Not a Rota Day and Not a Wage Tuesday and Not a Wipe and No Charge Placed on That Cistern on It, a Man of About Twenty-Seven of the Second Lay Writing the Six Lines on the Wall of the Room over That Market in a Large Hand and Stopping at the Seal and Writing No Name Under It and Having Been Asked to Do It by Nobody, a Room over That Floor Full at About One o'Clock in the Afternoon With About Twenty Up Those Stairs of Whom About Eleven Cannot Read What Is on That Wall, a Man of About Fifty-Three Who Owns a Boat of About Fourteen Tons Going at Those Six Lines One at a Time in Front of All of Them for About an Hour and a Half and Not Getting One of Them Down, a Man of About Thirty-Four of the Second Lay Asking in About Forty-Six Words to Be Told What He Cannot Read on a Wall, the Shortfall Said Out Loud at About Half Past Five Unmoved at Ninety-Six Thousand and Four Hundred and Fifty-Four Pence, Being Six Hundred and Two Marks, Eleven Shillings and Twopence, the Bag at Twenty-Eight Pence, Being Two Shillings and Fourpence*

Wednesday the second day of February, in that harbor, in about four hundred yards of water and along a stone quay, and in a room over the floor of that fish market with about twenty people in it.

**The said Wednesday was not a rota day and was not a wage Tuesday and was not a wipe, and no charge was placed on that cistern on it. Nothing went out on any line of the sheet that carries the money in any hand on that day and nothing went into it in any hand, and nobody was asked for anything. One hundred and sixty-eight pence came into that yard at about ten in the morning and one hundred and sixty-eight pence went out of it at about four in the afternoon, and there are twenty-eight pence in the bag at the close of the said day, being two shillings and fourpence, and the figure that bag is short of the wage of four marks is six hundred and twelve pence, being three marks eleven shillings. That sheet stands at one hundred and seven thousand and ninety-four pence out, being six hundred and sixty-nine marks, four shillings and sixpence, and ten thousand six hundred and forty pence in, being sixty-six marks, six shillings and eightpence, and it is ninety-six thousand and four hundred and fifty-four pence short, being six hundred and two marks, eleven shillings and twopence, and that figure is against that yard's own book and was said out loud in that yard's own mouth and was not answered by anybody. Nothing has been in a tin in that shed since the twenty-ninth day of April past, and no penny of the whole of that day came out of that bag, out of the owner of any boat, out of the man with two papers, or out of the Crown.**

**The nine that a stranger in that harbor could have read off any wall are the nine below, and not one of them was found by looking: the notice of the seventeenth of May past, two hundred and sixty days; the bellwright of that harbor, not asked for two hundred and sixty-two; the two objects of nobody's on that crown, six hundred days; the bench under that pump, bare for the three hundred and sixty-sixth night; the two lights at about a mile off that point, one hundred and eighty-four days; the six-line sheet at the back of that end stall, its hundred and thirty-ninth day; the paper of four lines in Fish Street, its hundred and fifty-fourth; the nine-line sheet with a seal on it in that front street, its hundred and seventy-first; and a man of about fifty-one who has said nothing to anybody about a page of six names for two hundred and two days. The sealed sheet is still on the board at the head of that mole and has not been taken down, and the two papers that were on that board before it have not been opened, and the mark at the foot of the wall in that room is a ring of black chalk the size of a crown piece and there is no name under it on that wall and there is no name under it anywhere but on the paper itself. The sheet that carries the money is on no wall in that room and is not in that room. There are nine hulls about two sea miles off the head of that mole this afternoon. A man of about twenty-nine who came home on that boat can hear out of the left side of his head and it has been sixty days and no figure has been set against it and he has not been to the road with the pump on it.**

---

That room was let out at about one o'clock in the afternoon and about twenty people went up those stairs, and about eleven of them cannot read what was put in front of them. Before anybody came up, a man of about twenty-seven of the second lay had written the six lines on the wall of that room in a large hand, the way he writes the five lines at the top of Fish Street, and he had done it in about three quarters of an hour and had not been asked to do it by anybody.

He stopped where the seal is. He put a ring of black chalk on that wall the size of a crown piece and he did not write a name under it, and when a man of about forty-six who mends nets asked him out loud, on the stairs, whose name went under the seal, he said that nobody in that harbor was going to find out from him and that he was not going to be the one to write it on a wall where it could be rubbed out with a wet cloth.

That is a copy. It is not the paper, it is not sealed, and about eleven people in that room cannot read it.

---

And a man of about fifty-three who owns a boat of about fourteen tons went at those six lines one at a time in front of about twenty people for about an hour and a half, and he was careful about it, and he went slowly, and he took each line in the order they are on and he said before he started that he was going to try to take them down and that he was not going to say one word about the wall.

He could not take one of them down. He took the first one and asked how a man knows that seven of them came back before six o'clock, and there was no answer in that room, and he said out loud that there was no answer because the line does not claim a man knows it, it puts an hour on it and the hour is the thing to go and look at. He took the second and asked how anybody knows there was nobody standing on a boat off that water at half past four in the morning, and a man of about thirty-three who hires hands said out loud that that was the wrong question and that the right one was what happens to a man who asks it, and the man of about fifty-three said he knew what happens to him and that is why he asked it.

He spent the longest on the fifth. He stood in front of the wall for about ten minutes with his hands behind his back and said out loud that every other line on that wall gives a person something to go and check and that the fifth gives a person nothing at all to check, and that a paper whose middle line says that it stops where its source stops is a paper that has told the person reading it exactly where to stop looking, and that he could not get round it in an hour and a half and he had tried.

---

And then a man of about thirty-four of the second lay, who was in that room and cannot read, said out loud, in about forty-six words, that he would like somebody to tell him what he cannot read on that wall, and the man of about thirty-three who hires hands said that the man of about fifty-three had just spent an hour and a half on six lines in front of twenty people and about eleven of them had not heard a word of it.

"**I cannot read a word of that wall and I would like to know what it says, and I would like to know it from the man who is going at it, not from a man who has to be asked to go and get it.**"

The man of about fifty-three said out loud that the man of about twenty-seven had written it on that wall in a large hand in about three quarters of an hour and could be asked to read it out, and that a man who cannot read a wall could stand in front of the man writing it and watch his mouth move and would know in about a minute whether the man was reading it or making it up. Nobody asked him to do it. The man of about thirty-four said he would rather it was read than that it was explained, and then he did not say anything else and he was not thanked for asking for it in front of twenty people.

---

And the man of about fifty-three said out loud, at about three o'clock in the afternoon, in about a hundred and thirty-two words, why he stopped, and nobody in that room asked him a second question afterwards and nobody was thanked for the hundred and thirty-two words.

"I stopped because of a woman of about thirty-one who was in this room on the twenty-fourth of January. She was asked, out loud, in front of all of us, for a particular about that night, and she stood up and said that she had not been on the water and did not know, and that she would say nothing else. **I have been at that paper for nine days and there is nothing on it I can get round, and that is the only reason I have any doubt left, and it is not a doubt about the paper.** I do not know why she said it. She has not been asked and she is not going to be, and I am not going to be the one who starts it."

A man of about thirty-five of the first lay said out loud that a paper can be right and still be a thing that was written by somebody who did not have to be in the room. The man of about fifty-three said that was a different argument and that he had not made it and was not going to, and that the eleven words he said in a room in this harbor four hundred yards from that bench stone were still on the page and were not going to be improved by a better one.

---

And the door of that shed stood open until about six that evening, and at about half past five, on the flat of that bench stone, the man who keeps the leaf said the shortfall out loud and said in about a hundred and twenty-eight words what a copy on a wall costs and what it is worth, and nobody argued it and nobody agreed with it.

"Ninety-six thousand and four hundred and fifty-four pence short, being six hundred and two marks, eleven shillings and twopence. Unmoved. **A careful man spent an hour and a half this afternoon trying to take six lines down and took none of them down, and he stopped on the strength of a woman standing up in a room and saying she did not know, and a man of about twenty-seven wrote all six on a wall in three quarters of an hour for nothing, and about eleven people in that room read neither the paper nor the wall. That is a day of work in this harbor and none of it is on any sheet and none of it can be added up and all of it is real.**"

Nothing on that slip was asked of anybody for the rest of it, not one voice in that room got as far as the words people use when a thing is handed to them, and about eleven people went down those stairs at about four o'clock knowing exactly as much as they had at one.

---

**A MAN OF ABOUT TWENTY-SEVEN WROTE THE SIX LINES ON THE WALL OF THAT ROOM IN A LARGE HAND AND STOPPED AT THE SEAL AND WROTE NO NAME UNDER IT, HAVING BEEN ASKED BY NOBODY. A MAN OF ABOUT FIFTY-THREE WENT AT THOSE SIX LINES ONE AT A TIME IN FRONT OF ABOUT TWENTY PEOPLE FOR ABOUT AN HOUR AND A HALF AND TOOK NONE OF THEM DOWN, AND SAID OUT LOUD THAT HE STOPPED BECAUSE OF A WOMAN OF ABOUT THIRTY-ONE WHO STOOD UP IN THAT ROOM ON THE TWENTY-FOURTH OF JANUARY AND SAID SHE DID NOT KNOW, AND THAT HE DOES NOT KNOW WHY SHE SAID IT AND IS NOT GOING TO ASK. A MAN OF ABOUT THIRTY-FOUR, WHO CANNOT READ, ASKED TO BE TOLD WHAT HE CANNOT READ ON THAT WALL FROM THE MAN WHO WAS GOING AT IT, AND WAS NOT ASKED TO READ IT OUT AND WAS NOT THANKED FOR ASKING. THE SEALED SHEET IS STILL ON THAT BOARD AND THE TWO PAPERS BESIDE IT HAVE NOT BEEN OPENED. THE SHEET THAT CARRIES THE MONEY IS ON NO WALL IN THAT ROOM. THE SHEET THAT CARRIES THE MONEY IS NINETY-SIX THOUSAND AND FOUR HUNDRED AND FIFTY-FOUR PENCE SHORT AGAINST THAT YARD'S OWN BOOK, BEING SIX HUNDRED AND TWO MARKS, ELEVEN SHILLINGS AND TWOPENCE, AND THERE ARE TWENTY-EIGHT PENCE IN THE BAG UNDER THE SLATE.**

---

*First column, entered on Wednesday the second day of February at about six in the evening in that shed: that the said day was not a rota day and was not a wage Tuesday and was not a wipe, and that no charge was placed on that cistern on it; and that a man of about twenty-seven of the second lay wrote the six lines of the account that came ashore on the twenty-fourth of January on the wall of the room over the floor of that market in a large hand in about three quarters of an hour and was not asked to do it by anybody, and that he made a ring of black chalk on that wall at the foot of the six lines and did not write a name under it, and that a man of about forty-six who mends nets asked him out loud whose name goes under the seal and that he said nobody in that harbor would find out from him; and that that room was let out at about one o'clock in the afternoon and about twenty people went up those stairs and about eleven of them cannot read what was put in front of them; and that a man of about fifty-three who owns a boat of about fourteen tons went at those six lines one at a time in front of all of them for about an hour and a half and took none of them down, and that he took the first and asked how a man knows that seven came back before six o'clock and that there was no answer in that room, and that he took the third and asked how anybody knows there was nobody standing on a boat off that water at half past four in the morning, and that he stood in front of the fifth for about ten minutes and said out loud that every other line gives a person something to go and check and that the fifth gives a person nothing at all to check; and that a man of about thirty-four of the second lay, who cannot read, said out loud in about forty-six words that he would like somebody to tell him what he cannot read on that wall from the man who was going at it, and that the man of about fifty-three said out loud that the man of about twenty-seven could be asked to read it out and that a man who cannot read a wall could stand in front of the man writing it and watch his mouth move, and that nobody asked him to do it; and that the man of about fifty-three said out loud at about three o'clock in the afternoon in about a hundred and thirty-two words that he stopped because of a woman of about thirty-one who stood up in that room on the twenty-fourth of January and said she had not been on the water and did not know, and that he does not know why she said it and that she has not been asked and that he is not going to be the one who starts it; and that the eleven days from Saturday the twenty-second day of January to this day stand on that leaf at three hundred and five and one thousand three hundred and ninety-nine and none and one hundred and twenty-five, and that neither the first of those figures nor the second has been read back out loud in any room or on any slip on any of those eleven days; and that one hundred and sixty-eight pence came into that yard and one hundred and sixty-eight pence went out of it, and that twenty-eight plus one hundred and sixty-eight less one hundred and sixty-eight is twenty-eight pence at the close of this day, being two shillings and fourpence, being short of the wage of four marks by six hundred and twelve pence, being three marks eleven shillings; and that that sheet stood at one hundred and seven thousand and ninety-four pence out and ten thousand six hundred and forty pence in and is ninety-six thousand and four hundred and fifty-four pence short, being six hundred and two marks, eleven shillings and twopence.*

*Second column, not checkable from a mole, entered on the said Wednesday at about six in the evening in that shed, and no name is against it: that the reason a copy of those six lines went onto a wall in that harbor is his own and is the man of about twenty-seven's and not his, and is that a harbor which has one copy of a thing nailed to a board two hundred yards from where the argument is being had has a problem of access and not a problem of truth, and that a wall in the room where the arguing happens is the cheapest thing in this harbor and it is the only instrument that reaches the eleven people who are not in the argument at all; and that the reason the man of about twenty-seven left the name off the wall is his own and not the man's, and is that a name written under a seal on a wall is a name that can be taken off a wall, and that the name is on the paper and the paper is on a board and the board is in the open, and that a thing which is safe on one of those three and not on another of them is a fact about the wall and not about the name; and that the reason the man of about fifty-three stopped where he stopped is not his to write down and he wrote it down anyway, and is that a paper which is technically right and which is going to be believed is a thing this coast has not had in two winters, and that a man who has watched eleven people in a market believe a figure on a lid every Tuesday for eleven weeks is not in a hurry to be told that the figures were never going to move, and that the woman of about thirty-one is the only person he knows of who has put a hole in a thing everybody else was about to sign; and that the reason nobody asked him a second question is his own, and is that a careful man who has spent an hour and a half failing to take six lines down has one thing left and that thing is his own account of why he stopped, and that if anybody in that room had gone at it he would have had to produce a second reason by about Thursday and he has not got one.*
