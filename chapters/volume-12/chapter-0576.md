# Chapter 576

*Wednesday the Sixteenth Day of February, Not a Rota Day and Not a Wage Tuesday and Not a Wipe and No Charge Placed on That Cistern on It, a Room over the Floor of That Market Let Out at About Two o'Clock in the Afternoon With About Nine People In It, a Page With Two Columns Set Side by Side on That Bench Stone, the Left-Hand Column Being a Community That Has Sealed Everything and the Right-Hand Column Being a Community That Has Permitted Everything and the Name of the Right-Hand One Not Being on the Page, the Left-Hand Column Holding and That Being on the Page as a Fact and Not as an Argument, a Man of About Thirty-Four of the Second Lay Who Cannot Read Asking What a Column Is For and a Man of About Forty-Six Reading Both Columns Out Loud and the Two of Them Sounding the Same, the Page Left on That Bench Stone at About a Quarter to Three and Nobody Asked to Agree With It and Nobody Thanked, the Shortfall Said Out Loud at About Half Past Five Unmoved at Ninety-Six Thousand Six Hundred and Twenty-Three Pence, Being Six Hundred and Three Marks, Eleven Shillings and Elevenpence, the Bag at Twenty-Eight Pence, Being Two Shillings and Fourpence*

Wednesday the sixteenth day of February, in that harbor, in about four hundred yards of water and along a stone quay, and in a shed with a roof on it, a bench stone, and a room over the floor of that market with about nine people in it at two o'clock in the afternoon.

**The said Wednesday was not a rota day and was not a wage Tuesday and was not a wipe, and no charge was placed on that cistern on it. Nothing went into that sheet in any hand in any line on that day and nothing came off it in any hand, and the page of two columns that was made in that shed is a page of figures and is not on that sheet, and the four heads on the wall of the room over that market were not mentioned in it and are not on it. One hundred and sixty-eight pence came into that yard at about ten in the morning and one hundred and sixty-eight pence went out of it at about four in the afternoon, and one hundred and sixty-eight pence is one mark and eightpence, and there are twenty-eight pence in the bag at the close of the said day, being two shillings and fourpence, and the figure that bag is short of the wage of four marks is six hundred and twelve pence, being three marks eleven shillings. That sheet stands at one hundred and seven thousand two hundred and sixty-three pence out, being six hundred and seventy marks, five shillings and threepence, and ten thousand six hundred and forty pence in, being sixty-six marks, six shillings and eightpence, and it is ninety-six thousand six hundred and twenty-three pence short, being six hundred and three marks, eleven shillings and elevenpence, and that figure is against that yard's own book and was said out loud in that yard's own mouth and was not answered by anybody. Nothing has been in a tin in that shed since the twenty-ninth day of April past, and no penny of the whole of that day came out of that bag, out of the owner of any boat, out of the man with two papers, or out of the Crown.**

**The nine that a stranger in that harbor could have read off any wall are the nine below, and not one of them was found by looking: the notice of the seventeenth of May past, two hundred and seventy-four days; the bellwright of that harbor, not asked for two hundred and seventy-six; the two objects of nobody's on that crown, six hundred and fourteen days; the bench under that pump, bare for the three hundred and eightieth night; the two lights at about a mile off that point, one hundred and ninety-eight days; the six-line sheet at the back of that end stall, its hundred and fifty-third day; the paper of four lines in Fish Street, its hundred and sixty-eighth; the nine-line sheet with a seal on it in that front street, its hundred and eighty-fifth; and a man of about fifty-one who has said nothing to anybody about a page of six names for two hundred and sixteen days. A whole sheet lies in a locked case in a room over that market with three hands on it and a broken pressed impression and a name, and it is not a confession and it is not a verdict and it has never given anybody an answer and it was not opened on that day. The sheet that carries the money is on no wall in any room over any fish market and it was not in that room this afternoon. There are nine hulls about two sea miles off the head of that mole this afternoon. A man of about twenty-nine who came home on that boat can hear out of the left side of his head and it has been seventy-four days and no figure has been set against it and he is not on the water and nobody has sent for him.**

---

The room over the floor of that market was let out at about two o'clock in the afternoon and about nine people went up those stairs, and the six lines on the wall of that room in a large hand with a ring of black chalk at the foot of them were behind them the whole time and nobody looked at them.

The page was on the bench stone in the shed below, and it is a page of two columns and there is nothing on it but figures of the same kind in both of them, and a stranger with a leaf and a pencil can take any line off either side.

Here are the six rows of it, and the two of them are the same shape on purpose.

1. *Who holds the crossing column.* Left: one man, named at the head of the book, eleven years. Right: four men, and the page does not name them because the page is not the book.
2. *Who may read it.* Left: any person in that harbor who can get into the room it is kept in. Right: any person on any quay in that harbor, at any hour, and about nine people read a crossing column this week.
3. *What it cost in February to put one hour on it.* Left: nothing, being one book and one hand, copied at the close of each week. Right: nothing, being a slate, and a wrong hour is a conversation somebody has to sit down for.
4. *Questions asked of it in the last eleven weeks, and how long the answer took.* Left: three, and an hour each, and the answer was the same every time because one man had all of it. Right: eleven, and four days, and the answer was different every time because eleven people had all of it.
5. *What is on it now.* Left: a figure no person has changed in eleven weeks. Right: three hours of the same night with three different figures in them and a note against each.
6. *How many persons the record carries.* Left: one name. Right: no name, and eleven people who have been told what is on it in eleven ways.

At the foot of the page, in the same hand, is one line, and it is the only line on that page that is not a figure.

*The left-hand column is the one that holds. That is on this page because it is a fact and not because it is an argument, and nobody in the room this afternoon was asked to agree with this page and nobody is going to be.*

---

A man of about thirty-four of the second lay was in that shed and he cannot read, and he asked one question at about half past two, and it was the first thing he had asked in that room in nineteen days.

He asked what a column is for, and a man of about forty-six who mends nets read the whole of the left-hand column out loud, and then read the whole of the right-hand column out loud, and put the page down.

They sounded the same. That is the point of them and it is not a trick and it is not an argument; they are the same kind of figure in the same kind of ink, and a man who cannot read cannot tell which of the two he has just been given, and neither could a man who can, unless he has been in one of the two harbors.

"That is what a column is for," the man of about thirty-four said. "**A column is a thing that does not know which of the two of them it is being read out of.**"

A man of about thirty-three who hires hands said out loud that he has said out loud in a room on the ninth of this month that one book would have worked, and that this page is the first piece of paper in nineteen days that has not argued with him, and that he is still not going to agree with a page in a room. Nobody argued with him about that and nobody agreed with him either.

---

And at about a quarter to three a woman of about thirty-one of the first lay asked out loud, in about twenty-eight words, what the name of the right-hand column is, and the answer was that it is not on the page.

"It is not on the page and it is not going on the page, and everybody in this shed knows which one I mean and about four of us cannot read the page, and that is the second time this month that a thing has been left off a page on purpose."

Nobody asked her what she holds and she did not offer it, and nobody asked the man who keeps the leaf to put the name on, and he said out loud, in about fifty-two words, that he had written the left-hand one because a stranger could go and look at that harbor and there is nobody in this room who can be asked whether the six rows of the left-hand column are true, and that the whole of the page is two things a stranger can check and one thing nobody can check, and that he has left the one he cannot check off the page and put it in the room instead, where it can be argued with by whoever is standing in it.

The page went onto that bench stone at about a quarter to three and was still on it at about six that evening. Nobody was asked to agree with it. Nobody copied it out. It carries no name and it is not on that sheet and it is not on the sheet of four heads in the room over that market, and the two of them were named out loud separately in that room before anybody went down those stairs.

---

And at about half past five on that Wednesday the man who keeps the leaf said the shortfall out loud at that bench stone with a page of two columns lying behind him, and used about two hundred and twenty-five words on what a page that will not say its own name out loud is for, and nobody in that slip was asked to be on either side of it.

"Ninety-six thousand six hundred and twenty-three pence short, being six hundred and three marks, eleven shillings and elevenpence. Unmoved, and there is a page on that bench stone behind me with two columns on it and the left-hand one holds and I have not got a word to add to that and I am not going to find one.

**Six rows, the same shape on both sides, one named and one not, and the man who cannot read told the room this afternoon what a column is for, and he is right, and I have had that argument in my mouth since the ninth of February and a man of about thirty-four has said it better in one sentence than I have said it in eleven days.** And the reason the right-hand name is not on that page is that the six rows of the other one are a thing a stranger can go and check, and the six rows of this one are a thing only this room can check, and a page that carries both and names both is a page that nine people would be asked to believe in a shed on a Wednesday.

A page with a gap in the middle of it is worth more to me than a page without one, and I have no figure for that either."

---

**A PAGE WITH SIX ROWS AND TWO COLUMNS WAS PUT ON THAT BENCH STONE AT ABOUT TWO O'CLOCK IN THE AFTERNOON IN FRONT OF ABOUT NINE PEOPLE AND WAS READ OUT ALOUD IN BOTH COLUMNS BY A MAN OF ABOUT FORTY-SIX WHO MENDS NETS, AND THE TWO OF THEM SOUNDED THE SAME. THE LEFT-HAND COLUMN IS A COMMUNITY THAT HAS SEALED EVERYTHING AND IT IS NAMED AND THE RIGHT-HAND COLUMN IS A COMMUNITY THAT HAS PERMITTED EVERYTHING AND IT IS NOT NAMED, AND THE FOOT OF THE PAGE SAYS IN ONE LINE THAT THE LEFT-HAND COLUMN IS THE ONE THAT HOLDS, THAT THAT IS A FACT AND NOT AN ARGUMENT, AND THAT NOBODY IN THAT ROOM WAS ASKED TO AGREE WITH IT. A MAN OF ABOUT THIRTY-FOUR OF THE SECOND LAY, WHO CANNOT READ, ASKED WHAT A COLUMN IS FOR AND SAID THAT A COLUMN IS A THING THAT DOES NOT KNOW WHICH OF THE TWO OF THEM IT IS BEING READ OUT OF. A MAN OF ABOUT THIRTY-THREE WHO HIRES HANDS SAID THE PAGE IS THE FIRST PIECE OF PAPER IN NINETEEN DAYS THAT HAS NOT ARGUED WITH HIM AND THAT HE IS STILL NOT GOING TO AGREE WITH IT IN A ROOM. THE PAGE CARRIES NO NAME AND IS ON NO OTHER SHEET. NOBODY WAS ASKED TO AGREE WITH ANYTHING AND NOBODY WAS THANKED. THE SHEET THAT CARRIES THE MONEY IS NINETY-SIX THOUSAND SIX HUNDRED AND TWENTY-THREE PENCE SHORT AGAINST THAT YARD'S OWN BOOK, AND THERE ARE TWENTY-EIGHT PENCE IN THE BAG UNDER THE SLATE.**

and that the room over the floor of that market was let out at about two o'clock in the afternoon and about nine people went up those stairs and the six lines on the wall of that room were behind them the whole time and nobody looked at them; and that the page was on that bench stone and is a page of two columns with figures of the same kind in both of them and no other thing on it, and that the six rows of it are, being who holds the crossing column, being one man named at the head of the book for eleven years on the left and four men unnamed on the right, being who may read it, being any person in that harbor who can get into the room it is kept in on the left and any person on any quay in that harbor at any hour on the right, being what it cost in February to put one hour on it, being nothing for one book and one hand copied at the close of each week on the left and nothing for a slate with a wrong hour being a conversation somebody has to sit down for on the right, being questions asked of it in the last eleven weeks and how long the answer took, being three and an hour each and the same answer every time because one man had all of it on the left and eleven and four days and a different answer every time because eleven people had all of it on the right, being what is on it now, being a figure no person has changed in eleven weeks on the left and three hours of the same night with three different figures in them and a note against each on the right, and being how many persons the record carries, being one name on the left and no name and eleven people told what is on it in eleven ways on the right; and that at the foot of that page is one line and it is the only line on it that is not a figure, being that the left-hand column is the one that holds, that that is on the page because it is a fact and not because it is an argument, and that nobody in that room this afternoon was asked to agree with the page and nobody is going to be; and that a man of about thirty-four of the second lay, who cannot read, asked at about half past two what a column is for, which was the first thing he had asked in that room in nineteen days, and that a man of about forty-six read the whole of the left-hand column out loud and then the whole of the right-hand column out loud and put the page down, and that the two of them sounded the same, and that the man of about thirty-four said that a column is a thing that does not know which of the two of them it is being read out of; and that a man of about thirty-three who hires hands said out loud that he said in a room on the ninth of this month that one book would have worked and that this page is the first piece of paper in nineteen days that has not argued with him and that he is still not going to agree with a page in a room, and that nobody argued with him and nobody agreed with him; and that at about a quarter to three a woman of about thirty-one of the first lay asked in about twenty-eight words what the name of the right-hand column is and was told it is not on the page and is not going on the page, and that about four people in that shed could not read the page, and that nobody asked her what she holds and she did not offer it and nobody asked the man who keeps the leaf to put the name on, and that he said out loud in about fifty-two words that he had written the left-hand one because a stranger could go and look at that harbor and that the whole of the page is two things a stranger can check and one thing nobody can check and that he has left the one he cannot check off the page and put it in the room; and that the page went onto that bench stone at about a quarter to three and was still on it at about six that evening and nobody was asked to agree with it and nobody copied it out and it carries no name and it is not on that sheet and it is not on the sheet of four heads in the room over that market, the two of them being named out loud separately in that room before anybody went down those stairs; and that the twenty-six days from Saturday the twenty-second day of January to this day stand on that leaf at three hundred and nine and one thousand four hundred and nineteen and none and one hundred and twenty-five, and that neither the first of those figures nor the second has been read back out loud in any room or on any slip on any of those twenty-six days; and that one hundred and sixty-eight pence came into that yard and one hundred and sixty-eight pence went out of it, and that twenty-eight plus one hundred and sixty-eight less one hundred and sixty-eight is twenty-eight pence at the close of this day, being two shillings and fourpence, being short of the wage of four marks by six hundred and twelve pence, being three marks eleven shillings; and that that sheet stood at one hundred and seven thousand two hundred and sixty-three pence out and ten thousand six hundred and forty pence in and is ninety-six thousand six hundred and twenty-three pence short, being six hundred and three marks, eleven shillings and elevenpence, and that nothing went into that sheet in any hand in any line on that day.*

*Second column, not checkable from a mole, entered on the said Wednesday at about six in the evening in that shed, and no name is against it: that at about two in the afternoon the reason the six rows are in the same shape on both sides is his own, and is that a page with two columns of different shapes is a page where the eye goes to the longer one and comes away with a comparison, and that the whole of what happened in that shed this afternoon was that a man who cannot read could not tell them apart when they were read out loud, and that this was arrived at by accident and he is going to leave it exactly as it is; and that at about half past two the reason he let the man of about thirty-four ask his question in a room and did not answer it is his own, and is that a man of about thirty-four said no in the open on the fifth of this month and put a page about it in another man's hand and has not been asked anything since, and that a man who asks to be asked once and is not asked is a man who will ask something else if somebody lets him, and that this was the first thing he had asked in nineteen days and it was not a question about the sheet, and that the answer to it was given by a reader and not by him; and that at about a quarter to three the reason the right-hand column is not named is his own, and is that he is not going to put his own harbor on a page that says the other one holds, because a page with this harbor named on it becomes a claim that this harbor is better, and that there is nothing in this harbor this month that anybody should be claiming, and that a woman of about thirty-one in that room put the same question he would have put and he is not going to thank her for it in a room; and that at about half past five the reason he said that a man of thirty-four has said it better in one sentence than he has said it in eleven days is his own, and is that he has been standing in that yard for twenty days defending a leaf nobody can check against a sheet everybody can, and that the whole of the difference between the two is a name at the head of one of them, and that a name is not a sheet and a sheet is not a name, and that the man of about thirty-four has that in one sentence and he has had it since the ninth and has been spending it on the wrong end of the yard; and that at about ten past six the reason the page is still lying on that bench stone at six in the evening and was not put away is his own, and is that a page that has been read out to nine people and left there overnight is a page that four people in that shed are going to describe to somebody else tomorrow in their own words, and that he would rather have that than have the page kept by one man in a drawer where it can be found out is wrong only by him.*
