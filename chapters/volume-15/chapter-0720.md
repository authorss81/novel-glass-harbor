# Chapter 720

*Sunday the Tenth Day of July, a Wipe and the Hundred and Fourteenth Time, Thirty-One Minutes Off the Wall and Eighteen Standing Bare and Thirty-One to Get It Back, Being Eighty the Whole of It, a Fisher Pilot of About Fifty-Eight Out on the Old Channel With a Set He Remembers Truly and a Sea That Has Moved, Coming Up to Two Bells at About Two on the Second Try and About a Boat's Breadth East of the Water He Was Born On, the Bag at Twenty-Eight Pence, Being Two Shillings and Fourpence, the First Column at Four Hundred and Fifty-Three and the Second at Two Thousand One Hundred and Thirty-Nine*

Sunday the tenth day of July, with that market shut all day and a west wind getting up in the afternoon, a yard below that market with a shed standing in it, and about nine people in that shed at half past five that afternoon.

**A wipe and not a rota day and not a wage Tuesday, and that wall behind a reeve's bench taken down at about ten past ten and bare by about forty to eleven and back on its holders at about half past eleven, at the angle it has carried since the twelfth of January, and the thing above the frame still not whole. Thirty-one minutes off the wall, eighteen standing bare, thirty-one to get it back, which is eighty minutes the whole of it, against thirty and seventeen and thirty last Sunday. No charge put on that cistern and its covers lying on it from nine in the morning until four in the afternoon. A fisher pilot of about fifty-eight went out on that tide at about eleven in the morning with two men and came back at about four and brought the Cinder Bell's own bell up to answer two bells at that landing, on the second try, at about a boat's breadth east of the water he was born on, and no state has been put on that name by anybody. A hundred and sixty-eight pence came into that yard at about ten in the morning and a hundred and sixty-eight went out of it at about four in the afternoon, and under that slate at the close of the day is twenty-eight pence, being two shillings and fourpence, and no tin in that shed has been opened since the twenty-ninth of April past.**

**Nine counters about things that are not being done ran a day on this morning. That notice of the seventeenth of May past stands on its four hundred and eighteenth day. Four hundred and twenty days have gone by without anybody asking a bellwright of this harbor, and his chest stays shut. Two objects of nobody's on that crown are into their seven hundred and fifty-eighth day. That bench under that pump has stood bare for the five hundred and twenty-fourth night. Two lights about a mile off that point have spent three hundred and forty-second days with no figure set against them. A six-line sheet at the back of that end stall has reached its two hundred and ninety-seventh day. The paper of four lines in Fish Street is on its three hundred and twelfth. A nine-line sheet with a seal on it in that front street has stood three hundred and twenty-ninth day. A man of about fifty-one has kept three hundred and sixtieth days of not saying anything to anybody about a page of six names. That third column is empty and unappointed and a boy's hand of twenty-two is at the head of it and his surname is on no page and he is off the water. That fourth column stands at one hundred and twenty-five and has taken no line and is neither shut nor struck. About two sea miles off that mole head lie nine hulls this afternoon and nine lay there on the twenty-first of January, and this harbor has not said a word about any of them easing. A man of about twenty-nine hears out of the left side of his head and it is two hundred and eighteen days, one more than the day before, no figure is set against it, and he is not on the water.**

---

Two men began at about ten past ten and the wall was bare by about forty to eleven and back on its holders at about half past eleven. About four people watched from the doorway and did not go in. Thirty-one minutes off the wall, eighteen standing bare, thirty-one to get it back, which is eighty the whole of it, and it is a minute longer each way and a minute longer bare than last Sunday, and nobody has said why, and the yard has stopped asking the two men who do it.

---

The pilot is about fifty-eight and he fishes eels and has worked that water since he was a boy in a boat with a sail his father cut. He went out at about eleven on the tide with two men from that community and no one from this yard, and this yard did not send anybody and did not ask to be told what to send.

He said at about four, standing in the shed with the west wind behind him, that the set off the old channel was where it had always been and that he could have put a boat on it asleep. He said the old channel itself had gone, and that it had gone the way a ditch goes, being slowly and then all at once, and that it had cut about a boat's breadth to the east and taken the deep water with it.

He said the first thing he was sure of was that the sea had moved and he was not sorry to it and had no complaint against it. The second thing he was sure of was that his water was in the right place on the chart and not on the ground, and that those two things are both true and that the fault is in the chart.

---

He said what happened in the boat in the plainest words and about four people were there for it. He said they came up on the set he remembered at about half past one and there was nothing there, being flat and hard and shallow, and that he was not lost and was not afraid and that he knew before he got there that he would find that.

He said he stood her up in his own water until the ebb began to run out of it, which is the mistake of a man who trusts his memory, and that the ebb ran out of it in about twenty minutes, and that the man at the oars said the word about his father, which he did not repeat and did not want written down.

Then he said the thing he had actually come down to say, which was that he and the two men agreed between the three of them, in the boat, on the ebb, that he would hold her to the set he remembered and they would watch where the water went, and that they would go where the two of them together pointed and no further east than the watch allowed.

---

They brought that bell up to answer two bells at that landing at about two in the afternoon, on the second try, at about a boat's breadth east of the water he was born on. The keeper wrote all three things down with the hour against each, being the set he remembered, the flat hard ground he found on it, and the half breadth east where the two of them together landed, and he wrote that the compromise was made in the boat between three men and that no part of it was made in that shed.

He wrote the name of that route against the hour as well, being the Cinder Bell, and he wrote nothing at all against the name, because a state is that route's to put on itself and this yard puts one on nobody. About four people asked him what the writing of a name with nothing against it means and he said it means that a bell answered.

About four people said the old channel had moved years ago. He said it had moved by a yard at a time for about nine years and that it had only finished moving this winter, and that nobody on this coast had put a mark where it went because there was nothing to put a mark on.

---

Nobody said his memory was right and nobody said it was wrong, and the page of that week carries both things side by side in one line and neither of them in a column of its own. The man of about thirty-two was not on the water and was not asked to be and read the three entries at about half past four and said that a compromise carried on by three men in a boat is a better thing than anything nine people could have agreed ashore, and about four people agreed with him and about four thought the yard should have been asked.

---

The shortfall was read out loud at that bench stone at about half past five on that Sunday, in about nine hundred words, to about nine people, and about four of those nine had been in that boat on that tide.

"**That wall came down at about ten past ten and was bare by about forty to eleven and back at about half past eleven, being thirty-one off and eighteen bare and thirty-one back, which is eighty the whole of it, and it is a minute longer each way than last Sunday. Nobody has said why it is getting slower and I have stopped asking the two men who do it.**

"**A fisher pilot of about fifty-eight went out at about eleven on that tide with two men of his own community and no one of ours, and came in at about four, and says the set off the old channel is where it has always been and that he could have put a boat on it asleep, and that the channel itself has gone, slowly and then all at once, and has cut about a boat's breadth to the east and taken the deep water with it. He says the sea has moved and he has no complaint against it, and that his water is right on the chart and not on the ground, and that both are true and the fault is in the chart.**

"**He came up on the set he remembered at about half past one and found flat, hard and shallow, and he was not lost and was not afraid, and he stood her up in his own water until the ebb began to run out of it, which is what happens to a man who trusts his memory. Then the three of them agreed in the boat that he would hold her to the set he remembered and they would watch where the water went, and they would go where the two of them together pointed and no further east than the watch allowed. They brought that bell up to answer two bells at that landing at about two, on the second try, at about a boat's breadth east of the water he was born on, and I wrote the name of that route against the hour as well and nothing at all against the name, because a state is that route's to put on itself and this yard puts one on nobody. The compromise was made in the boat by three men and no part of it was made in that shed.**

"**The sheet stands at one hundred and sixteen thousand and fourteen pence out and one hundred and five thousand three hundred and seventy-four pence short. Four hundred and fifty pence is still on that board in the front street. A hundred and sixty-eight pence in at about ten and a hundred and sixty-eight out at about four, and twenty-eight plus one hundred and sixty-eight less one hundred and sixty-eight is twenty-eight, and under that slate is twenty-eight pence, being two shillings and fourpence, and that is six hundred and twelve pence short of the wage of four marks, being three marks eleven shillings. No tin in that shed has been opened since the twenty-ninth of April past and this yard is now one hundred and thirty-three days without one.**

"**And the last of it. That cistern was not charged and its covers lay on it from nine until four and nobody walked on the water. The far bench was occupied from ten until four with nothing put on it. About two sea miles off that mole head there are nine hulls this afternoon and there were nine there on the twenty-first of January. A man of about twenty-nine hears out of the left side of his head and it is two hundred and eighteen days today and nothing done on that tide touched it by a day.**"

Nobody was thanked for the arithmetic and nobody was thanked for standing a boat in his own water until the ebb ran out of it, and that shed stood empty by about six with its door standing open on a slip nobody was on, and that page of that week had twenty-two lines on it, and that wooden tally slate was still lying flat on that bench stone with nothing on it.

---

**THE WALL CAME DOWN AT ABOUT TEN PAST TEN AND WAS BARE BY ABOUT FORTY TO ELEVEN AND BACK AT ABOUT HALF PAST ELEVEN, BEING THIRTY-ONE OFF AND EIGHTEEN BARE AND THIRTY-ONE BACK, WHICH IS EIGHTY THE WHOLE OF IT. A PILOT'S WATER IS RIGHT ON THE CHART AND NOT ON THE GROUND AND THE COMPROMISE WAS MADE IN THE BOAT BETWEEN THREE MEN, BEING THAT HE WOULD HOLD TO WHAT HE REMEMBERED AND THEY WOULD WATCH THE WATER AND THEY WENT WHERE THE TWO OF THEM TOGETHER POINTED. TWO BELLS ANSWERED AT ABOUT TWO ON THE SECOND TRY. THE SHEET STANDS AT ONE HUNDRED AND SIXTEEN THOUSAND AND FOURTEEN PENCE OUT AND ONE HUNDRED AND FIVE THOUSAND THREE HUNDRED AND SEVENTY-FOUR PENCE SHORT. THE BAG HOLDS TWENTY-EIGHT PENCE, BEING TWO SHILLINGS AND FOURPENCE.**

---

*First column, entered on Sunday the tenth day of July at about six in the evening in that shed: that the said day was a wipe and was not a rota day and was not a wage Tuesday, being the hundred and fourteenth time, with two men beginning at about ten past ten and the wall bare by about forty to eleven and back on its holders at about half past eleven, and the figures being thirty-one minutes off the wall and eighteen standing bare and thirty-one to get it back, which is eighty minutes the whole of it, against thirty and seventeen and thirty on the Sunday before; and that a fisher pilot of about fifty-eight went out at about eleven with two men of his own community and none of this yard and came in at about four; and that he said the set off the old channel is where it has always been and that the channel has gone slowly and then all at once and has cut about a boat's breadth to the east and taken the deep water with it; and that he came up on the set he remembered at about half past one and found flat and hard and shallow, and stood her up in his own water until the ebb began to run out of it; and that the three of them agreed in the boat that he would hold to the set he remembered and they would watch where the water went, and that they brought that bell up to answer two bells at that landing at about two in the afternoon on the second try at about a boat's breadth east of the water he was born on, and that the name of that route, being the Cinder Bell, stands against that hour on that page with nothing written against the name, because a state is that route's to put on itself.*

*Second column, not checkable from a mole, entered on the said Sunday at about six in the evening in that shed, and no name is against it: that at about four in the afternoon the reason the two things are entered in one line and neither in a column is that a memory and a bearing of ground are both true and the fault is in the chart, and two columns would make one of them an excuse; that at about half past four the reason the first try is entered and is not left out is that a page with only the working on it teaches four people in the next nine runs of days that this coast's waters can be trusted out of a book, and about four of those nine have believed that already; that at about half past four the reason the compromise is entered as made in the boat is that a settlement made ashore about somebody else's water is a settlement the somebody else was not asked for; and that at about five the reason no figure of money stands against a day of a man's work on the water is that a pilot is paid by the boat he takes out and a figure of pence printed beside a thing he chose to try would turn the next bad guess into an argument about money.*
