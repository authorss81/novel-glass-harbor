# Chapter 751

*Wednesday the Tenth Day of August, Line Eleven Settled Into the Foot of That Money Sheet at About Four Carrying Two Days on Its Face and the Foot Read at the Close of the Day as It Stood Before the Sum Went Down at One Hundred and Twenty-One Thousand One Hundred and Fifty-Four Pence Out and One Hundred and Ten Thousand Five Hundred and Fourteen Pence Short, a Boat of the Orison Landing Away Over the Bar at About Six in the Morning and Alongside That Mole Again at About Eight in the Evening With the Ninth Section of That Bell and Two of the Other Eight on Her Deck and Nothing Taken Down, Not a Rota Day and Not a Wage Tuesday and Not a Wipe, the Bag at Twenty-Eight Pence, the First Column at Four Hundred and Eighty-Four and the Second at Two Thousand Two Hundred and Ninety-Four*

Wednesday the tenth day of August, with that market open from about nine and a clear morning with the wind still in the north and the swell coming off that bar lighter than it was on Monday, a yard below that market with a shed standing in it, and about nine people in that shed at half past five that afternoon.

**Not a rota day and not a wage Tuesday and not a wipe, and no charge put on that cistern and its covers lying on it from nine in the morning until four in the afternoon. Line eleven went into the foot of that money sheet at about four, carrying two days on its face, being Monday the eighth day of August for the work and Tuesday the ninth for the day the mechanism broke, and the keeper said both days out loud in about nine people before he wrote a single figure, being seven hundred and twenty-eight pence, being four marks, seven shillings and fourpence, in four parts. The foot of that sheet is read at the close of this day as it stood before the sum went down and stands at one hundred and twenty-one thousand one hundred and fifty-four pence out and one hundred and ten thousand five hundred and fourteen pence short, and from tomorrow it stands at one hundred and twenty-one thousand eight hundred and eighty-two pence out and one hundred and eleven thousand two hundred and forty-two pence short, against ten thousand six hundred and forty pence in. A hundred and sixty-eight pence came into that yard at about ten and a hundred and sixty-eight went out of it at about four, and under that slate is twenty-eight pence, being two shillings and fourpence, and no tin in that shed has been opened since the twenty-ninth of April past.**

**Nine counters ran a morning here, being what did not happen. The notice of the seventeenth of May past stands on its four hundred and forty-ninth day. Four hundred and fifty-one days have gone by with nobody asking that bellwright a question. Those two objects of nobody's on that crown are into the seven hundred and eighty-ninth day. That bench under that pump has now stood bare for the five hundred and fifty-fifth night running. Those two lights about a mile off that point have gone three hundred and seventy-third days with no figure set against them. The six-line sheet at the back of that end stall has reached the three hundred and twenty-eighth day of its standing. The paper of four lines in Fish Street is on the three hundred and forty-third. The nine-line sheet with a seal on it in that front street has stood three hundred and sixtieth day. And the man of about fifty-one has kept three hundred and ninety-one days of saying nothing to anybody about a page of six names. That third column is empty and unappointed with a boy's hand of twenty-two at the head of it, his surname is on no page, and he is off the water. That fourth column stands at one hundred and twenty-five and has taken no line and is neither shut nor struck. About two sea miles off that mole head there are nine hulls this afternoon and nine lay there on the twenty-first of January, and this harbor has not said a word about any of them easing. A man of about twenty-nine hears out of the left side of his head and it is two hundred and forty-nine days today, one more than the day before, no figure is set against it, and he is not on the water.**

---

That boat of the Orison landing went out over the bar at about six with the tide under her and lay alongside that mole again at about eight in the evening, and the tide had turned under her twice while she was gone.

She had the ninth section of that bell on her deck and two of the other eight, and she had them there for a working reason and the reason was given out loud at about ten on that quay. The frame above that room is coming down in parts under the order of the people of that landing, the chain above it having parted on the Saturday, and the ninth section was lying across the door of a room where nine people work, and they wanted it out of the room and not out of the harbor.

---

It took nine men and a bar of iron to bring the ninth section down the eight steps from that room, and it was set on three pieces of dunnage and chocked with two more, and it did not slide a finger's width in two days of wind. A man of about thirty-four of the second lay said the crack across the seat of one of the other two was the widest he had seen in iron and that it would be cut out here and not there, because there is a vice at the end of that bench and a bed under it, and neither a vice nor a bed on that landing.

---

The man of about thirty-eight came down the ramp at about nine with a sheet in his own hand, and the keeper read the hours at the door of that shed and wrote them with the day against them. The hours say the frame is down to two of nine and that both of those two are hung and will be taken on the twenty-fourth and the twenty-eighth, being the two days the people of that landing named themselves. The hours say the two sections that came off the frame on the Saturday were carried into the end of that room and put on the floor where they would not be walked on. The hours say nobody at that landing has asked for a boat since, and that this one was asked for by name on the Sunday night and not this morning.

---

The two of the eight that came on her deck are going along the bench in that shed, because the bench is long enough and there is a vice at the end of it. The man of about thirty-eight said out loud that a section of that bell can be struck on its own and that the note of one of them is not the note of any of the other seven, and that a thing which cannot be made to answer together has to be kept where it can be worked on, and that this is the only bench on this coast long enough to lay one out on.

---

At about one the man of about thirty-two said out loud at the flat of that bench stone, in about nine people, that a boat had gone and what she had gone for, and that he had stayed ashore. About four agreed with him that the reason was a working one and about four said nothing at all. Nobody in that nine asked him why he was not on her and nobody in that nine said he should have been.

---

At about half past three the woman of thirty of no office wrote the boat and the reason and the hour into her own book, and said out loud that a book is not kept by two people, and that the two books in that harbor do not carry one another's entries and are not going to start. He said he had not asked her for hers, and about four agreed with her and about four said she was right to keep it, and nobody said he was wrong, and the matter is open and no page in that shed carries a resolution of it.

---

At about four the keeper took that sheet off its nail and laid it flat on the dry end of that stone in front of about nine people, and he said Monday the eighth out loud and then he said Tuesday the ninth out loud, and read the four parts with the multiplications and then the sum. Two days of a boat hired at the figure in chalk at two times one hundred and sixty-eight for three hundred and thirty-six pence. Rope and shackles at sixty pence. Nine men at two days at sixteen pence a man-day at nine times two times sixteen for two hundred and eighty-eight pence. Sounding line and oil at forty-four pence. Three hundred and thirty-six plus sixty plus two hundred and eighty-eight plus forty-four is seven hundred and twenty-eight pence, and the sum went into the foot of that sheet, and the foot was read again at the close of the day and stood at one hundred and twenty-one thousand one hundred and fifty-four pence out, and he wrote on the face of the line that from tomorrow it stands at one hundred and twenty-one thousand eight hundred and eighty-two pence out and wrote the day it begins on.

---

The ordinary work of that Wednesday went on and was not stopped by any of it. A boat of about six tons was hauled up on the west slip with her garboard off and two men under her from about ten, and the pump ran clean from about nine and the bottom was washed down with a bucket, and the second lay was tied twice in what was left of that swell. The sheet with the fourteen gaps at the end stall stood all day with no figure moved by one, and the far bench was empty and nothing was put on it.

---

Line eleven went into the foot of that money sheet at about four and at about half past five on that Wednesday the whole of that day was put out loud at that stone, in about one thousand one hundred and seventy-eight words, to about nine people, and about four of those nine had stood at that flat stone at about four and watched a sum go down into the foot of a sheet.

"**A boat of the Orison landing went out over the bar at about six this morning with the tide under her and was alongside this mole again at about eight this evening, and the tide had turned under her twice while she was gone. She had the ninth section of that bell on her deck and two of the other eight and she had them there for a working reason, being that the frame above that room is coming down in parts under the order of the people of that landing, the chain above it having parted on the Saturday, and the ninth section was lying across the door of a room where nine people work, and they wanted it out of the room and not out of the harbor.**"

"**Nine men of this yard went by name and none of them went for a look at the bell. It took nine men and a bar of iron to bring the ninth section down those eight steps and it was set on three pieces of dunnage and chocked with two more, and it did not slide a finger's width in two days of wind. A man of about thirty-four of the second lay said the crack across the seat of one of the other two was the widest he had seen in iron and that it will be cut out here and not there, because there is a vice at the end of that bench and a bed under it and neither a vice nor a bed on that landing.**"

"**The man of about thirty-eight came down the ramp at about nine with a sheet in his own hand and I read the hours at the door of this shed and wrote them with the day against them. The hours say the frame is down to two of nine and both of those are hung and will be taken on the twenty-fourth and the twenty-eighth, being the two days the people of that landing named themselves. The hours say the two sections that came off the frame on the Saturday were put on the floor at the end of that room where nobody walks on them. The hours say nobody at that landing has asked for a boat since, and that this one was asked for by name on the Sunday night and not this morning.**"

"**The two of the eight that came on her deck are going along that bench because the bench is long enough and there is a vice at the end of it. The man of about thirty-eight said out loud that a section of that bell can be struck on its own and the note of one of them is not the note of any of the other seven, and that a thing which cannot be made to answer together has to be kept where it can be worked on, and that this is the only bench on this coast long enough to lay one out on.**"

"**At about one the man of about thirty-two said out loud at the flat of this stone, in about nine of us, that a boat had gone and what she had gone for, and that he had stayed ashore. About four agreed with him that the reason was a working one and about four said nothing at all. Nobody in that nine asked him why he was not on her and nobody in that nine said he should have been.**"

"**At about half past three the woman of thirty of no office wrote the boat and the reason and the hour into her own book, and said out loud that a book is not kept by two people and that the two books in this harbor do not carry one another's entries and are not going to start. He said he had not asked her for hers, and about four agreed with her and about four said she was right to keep it and nobody said he was wrong, and this is not settled and no page in this shed settles it.**"

"**At about four I took that sheet off its nail and laid it flat on the dry end of this stone in about nine of us and I said Monday the eighth out loud and then I said Tuesday the ninth out loud. Two days of a boat hired at the figure in chalk at two times one hundred and sixty-eight for three hundred and thirty-six pence. Rope and shackles at sixty pence. Nine men at two days at sixteen pence a man-day at nine times two times sixteen for two hundred and eighty-eight pence. Sounding line and oil at forty-four pence. Three hundred and thirty-six plus sixty plus two hundred and eighty-eight plus forty-four is seven hundred and twenty-eight pence, being four marks, seven shillings and fourpence, and the sum went into the foot of that sheet. The foot was read again at the close of the day and stood at one hundred and twenty-one thousand one hundred and fifty-four pence out and one hundred and ten thousand five hundred and fourteen pence short, and I wrote on the face of the line that from tomorrow it stands at one hundred and twenty-one thousand eight hundred and eighty-two pence out and one hundred and eleven thousand two hundred and forty-two pence short, and I wrote the day it begins on.**"

"**And the rest of it. A boat of about six tons was hauled up on that west slip with her garboard off and two men under her from about ten, and the pump ran clean from about nine and the bottom was washed down with a bucket, and the second lay was tied twice in what was left of that swell, and nobody in this shed stopped work because of what was said at about one. The sheet with the fourteen gaps stood at that end stall all day with no figure moved by one. The column on the lid of that step was not read out today because today is not a wage Tuesday. About two sea miles off that mole head there are nine hulls this evening and there were nine there on the twenty-first of January. No tin in this shed has been opened since the twenty-ninth of April past and this yard is now one hundred and sixty-four days without one. Twenty-eight plus two thousand one hundred and eighty-one plus one thousand eight hundred and forty-eight less four thousand and twenty-nine is twenty-eight, and under that slate is twenty-eight pence, being two shillings and fourpence, and that is six hundred and twelve pence short of the wage of four marks, being three marks eleven shillings, with a hundred and sixty-eight pence in at about ten and a hundred and sixty-eight out at about four. A man of about twenty-nine hears out of the left side of his head and it is two hundred and forty-nine days today and nothing said on this quay touched that figure by a day.**"

Nobody was thanked for the arithmetic and nobody was thanked for the iron, and that shed stood empty by about six with its door standing open on a slip nobody was on, and that page of that week had twenty-nine lines on it, and that wooden tally slate was still lying flat on that bench stone with nothing on it.

---

**THE BOAT OF THAT LANDING WENT OUT OVER THE BAR AT ABOUT SIX AND CAME ALONGSIDE THAT MOLE AGAIN AT ABOUT EIGHT, WITH THE NINTH SECTION OF THAT BELL AND TWO OF THE OTHER EIGHT ON HER DECK AND NOTHING TAKEN DOWN AND NOBODY ASKED TO GO FOR A LOOK AT IT. LINE ELEVEN WENT INTO THE FOOT OF THAT MONEY SHEET AT ABOUT FOUR, BEING SEVEN HUNDRED AND TWENTY-EIGHT PENCE, BEING FOUR MARKS, SEVEN SHILLINGS AND FOURPENCE, IN FOUR PARTS, AND BOTH DAYS ARE ON THE FACE OF THAT LINE. THE FOOT OF THAT SHEET WAS READ AT THE CLOSE OF THE DAY AS IT STOOD BEFORE THE SUM WENT DOWN. THIS IS NOT A WAGE TUESDAY AND THE COLUMN ON THE LID OF THAT STEP WAS NOT READ OUT. THE BAG HOLDS TWENTY-EIGHT PENCE, BEING TWO SHILLINGS AND FOURPENCE.**

*First column, entered on Wednesday the tenth day of August at about six in the evening in that shed: that the said day was not a rota day and was not a wage Tuesday and was not a wipe, and that no charge was put on that cistern and its covers lay on it from nine until four; and that a boat of the Orison landing went out over the bar at about six in the morning and was alongside that mole again at about eight in the evening, and that she carried the ninth section of that bell and two of the other eight on her deck, and that the reason given out loud at about ten was that the frame above that room is coming down in parts under the order of the people of that landing and that the ninth section was lying across the door of a room where nine people work; and that it took nine men and a bar of iron to bring the ninth section down those eight steps and that it was set on three pieces of dunnage and chocked with two more; and that the man of about thirty-four of the second lay said the crack across the seat of one of the other two would be cut out in that yard and not on that landing, there being a vice and a bed in that yard and neither on that landing; and that the man of about thirty-eight came down the ramp at about nine with a sheet in his own hand, being that the frame is down to two of nine and that both are hung and will be taken on the twenty-fourth and the twenty-eenth, being the twenty-fourth and the twenty-eighth, those being the two days the people of that landing named themselves, and that the two sections which came off the frame on the Saturday were put on the floor at the end of that room, and that nobody at that landing has asked for a boat since and that this one was asked for by name on the Sunday night; and that the two sections that came are going along that bench, there being a vice at the end of it, and that the man of about thirty-eight said a section of that bell can be struck on its own and that the note of one is not the note of any of the other seven; and that at about one the man of about thirty-two said out loud in about nine that a boat had gone and what she had gone for and that he had stayed ashore, and that about four agreed with him and about four said nothing and that nobody asked him why he was not on her; and that at about half past three the woman of thirty of no office wrote the boat and the reason and the hour into her own book and said out loud that a book is not kept by two people, and that he said he had not asked her for hers, and that this is not settled; and that line eleven was settled into the foot of that money sheet at about four carrying two days on its face, being the eighth for the work and the ninth for the day the mechanism broke, both days having been said out loud before any figure was written, being two times one hundred and sixty-eight for three hundred and thirty-six pence of boat hired at the figure in chalk and sixty pence of rope and shackles and nine times two times sixteen for two hundred and eighty-eight pence and forty-four pence of sounding line and oil, being seven hundred and twenty-eight pence; and that the foot of that sheet was read at the close of the day as it stood before the sum went down, being one hundred and twenty-one thousand one hundred and fifty-four pence out and one hundred and ten thousand five hundred and fourteen pence short, and that it stands at one hundred and twenty-one thousand eight hundred and eighty-two pence out and one hundred and eleven thousand two hundred and forty-two pence short from the eleventh, that day being written on the face of the line; and that a boat of about six tons was hauled up on that west slip with her garboard off and two men under her from about ten.*

*Second column, not checkable from a mole, entered on the said Wednesday at about six in the evening in that shed, and no name is against it: that at about ten the reason the boat was sent and not withheld is that a section of that bell was lying across a door that nine people go in and out of, and a yard that keeps a bench is a bench iron can be be laid out on, and this yard offered it and the people of that landing asked for it by name; that at about one the reason nobody asked the man of about thirty-two why he was not on that boat is that a man is not a hand on a rope and where he stands on a working day is his own and a question put to him about it is a question about going down wearing a different coat; that at about half past three the reason she wrote the boat into her own book and did not into his is that two records kept by two people are the only thing on this coast that has not been agreed to by anybody, and a record one of them can write into is a record one of them can take back; that at about four the reason both days were said out loud before any figure was written is that a line which carries the day the work was done and the day it settles is a line a stranger can check, and a keeper who writes the second day in silence leaves a reader to guess which of the two days the money belongs to.*
