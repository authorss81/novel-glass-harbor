# Chapter 739

*Friday the Twenty-Ninth Day of July, Not a Rota Day and Not a Wage Tuesday and Not a Wipe, the Count of Those Asked Twice Taken Off a Page of Days at About Eleven and Written Down Once and Said Nowhere, the Three Columns of the Page of Routes Written at About Three at Three and Three and Three, the Sheet at One Hundred and Eighteen Thousand Seven Hundred and Eighty-Four Pence Out, the Bag at Twenty-Eight Pence, the First Column at Four Hundred and Seventy-Two and the Second at Two Thousand Two Hundred and Thirty-Four*

Friday the twenty-ninth day of July, with that market busy from nine and a warm wind up the coast road by noon, a yard below that market with a shed standing in it, and about nine people in that shed at half past five that afternoon.

**Not a rota day and not a wage Tuesday and not a wipe, and no charge put on that cistern and its covers lying on it from nine in the morning until four in the afternoon. The count of those asked twice was taken off a page of days at about eleven in the morning and written down once and said nowhere, being zero and one and zero and two and zero and one and three and zero and one and zero and two and zero and one, and eleven is the sum of those thirteen figures, with the highest day at three against the twenty-third. The three columns of the page of routes were written at about three in the afternoon at three and three and three, and three plus three plus three is nine. The sheet stands at one hundred and eighteen thousand seven hundred and eighty-four pence out and one hundred and eight thousand one hundred and forty-four pence short, against ten thousand six hundred and forty pence in. A hundred and sixty-eight pence came into that yard at about ten and a hundred and sixty-eight went out of it at about four, and under that slate is twenty-eight pence, being two shillings and fourpence, and no tin in that shed has been opened since the twenty-ninth of April past.**

**Nine counters about things that are not being done ran a day on this morning. That notice of the seventeenth of May past stands on its four hundred and thirty-seventh day. Four hundred and thirty-nine days have gone by without anybody asking a bellwright of this harbor, and his chest stays shut. Two objects of nobody's on that crown are into their seven hundred and seventy-seventh day. That bench under that pump has stood bare for the five hundred and forty-third night. Two lights about a mile off that point have spent three hundred and sixty-first days with no figure set against them. A six-line sheet at the back of that end stall has reached its three hundred and sixteenth day. The paper of four lines in Fish Street is on its three hundred and thirty-first. A nine-line sheet with a seal on it in that front street has stood three hundred and forty-eighth day. A man of about fifty-one has kept three hundred and seventy-ninth days of not saying anything to anybody about a page of six names. That third column is empty and unappointed and a boy's hand of twenty-two is at the head of it and his surname is on no page and he is off the water. That fourth column stands at one hundred and twenty-five and has taken no line and is neither shut nor struck. About two sea miles off that mole head lie nine hulls this afternoon and nine lay there on the twenty-first of January, and this harbor has not said a word about any of them easing. A man of about twenty-nine hears out of the left side of his head and it is two hundred and thirty-seven days, one more than the day before, no figure is set against it, and he is not on the water.**

---

The work was ordinary all morning. The sail was bent on and off again and the lead agreed with the harbour side, and about four from the boards stood at the end stall until about one and none came into the shed.

---

At about ten one person put the same words to the man of about thirty-two at the flat of that bench stone. About four people heard it and about four were sorting line and did not lift their heads, and he said he had heard the words and the keeper wrote the hour and no name against it, and no sum is set against that hour on any sheet in that yard.

---

At about eleven the man who keeps the leaf took the page of days off its nail. On that page the column headed with the asking stands against thirteen days from the seventeenth to the twenty-ninth, and beside it the refusal of the twenty-third stands with the hour against it and no name against it.

---

He set the thirteen days down whole against the thirteen days. The figures are zero and one and zero and two and zero and one and three and zero and one and zero and two and zero and one, and eleven is the sum of them. The three is against the twenty-third and the two figures of two are against the twentieth and the twenty-seventh, and one each is against the eighteenth and the twenty-second and the twenty-fifth and the twenty-ninth, and the one against this twenty-ninth is the putting made at about ten and was on the page before the sum was set down.

---

He put the day and the hour against the sum and did not say the sum out loud, and nobody in that shed asked him for it, and it is not in this slip and it is not in the first column in words. About nine people were in that shed at eleven because they come at that hour, and the figures were not read out, and the reason they were not read out is in the second column.

---

It is a count of asking and never a count of consent and never a count of refusal and never a count of times he said yes. A separate person who put the same words to him on a day is on it once for that day and on no other sheet of this yard, and the one who wrote the hours of the asking did not write one word about why any of them asked.

---

At about noon nobody put the same words to the man of about thirty-two a second time, and the page of days carries no second hour against this day. About four people went on sorting line and about four went to the butt for water, and he carried oakum for the boat alongside and spoke of nothing but the seams, and the quiet after the taking settled nothing and proved nothing.

---

The woman of thirty of no office looked at the column at about one and said out loud that nobody in that shed should try to make it smaller or larger by telling the story twice. About four agreed and the man of about thirty-two said nothing at all about it.

---

At about three he took the page of routes off its nail and wrote three figures at the head of its three columns, being three and three and three, and three plus three plus three is nine. The three that are held in the order they are always read in are the Saltmarch Weatherway and the Morrow Line and the Cinder Bell, and the three that are restricted are the Ashtide Run and the Ternwatch Passage and the Tidewatch Channel, and the three that are dormant are the Lowfen Reed and the Pale Shoals and the Orison Descent.

---

That is the second of three writings and the third is on the last day, and neither this one nor the one on the second of July was said out loud in a shed and neither is in any slip. Every state on that page stands there because the people of that route put it there in their own hand, and this yard put none of the nine there and did not ask any of them for one, and where the reason is written down it is on that community's own page and in the second column and is not called a count in a room.

---

The disagreement was in that shed again at about four in about nine people and was not settled. He said the second book prints a refusal twice and she said one sentence in two places is one sentence, and he said two marks on one outside cannot be lifted by one hand and she said one custody is not two owners, and about four agreed with her and about four with him and the matter is open and no page here carries a resolution of it.

---

The bell off that mole answered the bell off a shoal four days down that coast road at about half past ten and again at about three, and the two answers were in hours and not in names, and the keeper wrote the hours and no reason against them.

---

The page came off its nail at about eleven in the morning and the reading went out at that bench stone at about half past five on that Friday in about five hundred and seventy-one words to about nine people, and about four of those nine had been standing at the flat of that stone at about eleven.

"**The sail was bent on and off again and the lead agreed with the harbour side, and that bell off that mole answered the bell off a shoal four days down that coast road at about half past ten and again at about three. At about eleven I took the page of days off its nail and set a column down whole against thirteen days and put the day and the hour against it and did not say anything out loud, and it is not in this slip and not in the first column in words, and beside it stands a refusal with the hour against it and no name against it.**

"**At about ten one person put words to the man of about thirty-two at the flat of this stone and I wrote the hour and no name, and nobody put the same words a second time today and the page carries no second hour. The woman of thirty of no office looked at the column at about one and said eleven is a small number to have carried off nine lines in a fortnight and nobody should try to make it smaller or larger by telling the story twice, and about four agreed and about four said nothing and he said nothing at all about it.**

"**At about three I took the page of routes off its nail and wrote three figures at the head of its three columns, and every state on that page stands there because the people of that route put it there in their own hand, and this yard put none of the nine there and did not ask any of them for one. That is the second of three and the third is on the last day, and neither this one nor the first was said out loud in a shed and neither is in any slip.**

"**The sheet stands at one hundred and eighteen thousand seven hundred and eighty-four pence out and one hundred and eight thousand one hundred and forty-four pence short, against ten thousand six hundred and forty pence in. Four hundred and fifty pence is still on that board in the front street. A hundred and sixty-eight pence in at about ten and a hundred and sixty-eight out at about four, and twenty-eight plus two thousand one hundred and eighty-one less two thousand one hundred and eighty-one is twenty-eight, and under that slate is twenty-eight pence, being two shillings and fourpence, and that is six hundred and twelve pence short of the wage of four marks, being three marks eleven shillings. No tin in that shed has been opened since the twenty-ninth of April past and this yard is now one hundred and fifty-two days without one.**

"**And the last of it. That cistern was not charged and its covers lay on it from nine until four. The far bench was occupied from ten until four with nothing put on it. The disagreement was in this shed again at about four and is not settled and no page here settles it. About two sea miles off that mole head there are nine hulls this afternoon and there were nine there on the twenty-first of January. A man of about twenty-nine hears out of the left side of his head and it is two hundred and thirty-seven days today and nothing counted or written this afternoon touched it by a day.**"

Nobody was thanked for the arithmetic and nobody was thanked for a column of asking, and that shed stood empty by about six with its door standing open on a slip nobody was on, and that page of that week had thirty-one lines on it, and that wooden tally slate was still lying flat on that bench stone with nothing on it.

---

**THE COUNT OF THOSE ASKED TWICE WAS TAKEN OFF A PAGE OF DAYS AT ABOUT ELEVEN AND WRITTEN DOWN ONCE AND SAID NOWHERE, BEING ZERO AND ONE AND ZERO AND TWO AND ZERO AND ONE AND THREE AND ZERO AND ONE AND ZERO AND TWO AND ZERO AND ONE, AND ELEVEN IS THE SUM, WITH THE HIGHEST DAY AT THREE AGAINST THE TWENTY-THIRD AND A REFUSAL ENTERED WITH THE HOUR AGAINST IT AND NO NAME AGAINST IT. THE THREE COLUMNS OF THE PAGE OF ROUTES WERE WRITTEN AT ABOUT THREE AT THREE AND THREE AND THREE, AND THREE PLUS THREE PLUS THREE IS NINE. THE SHEET STANDS AT ONE HUNDRED AND EIGHTEEN THOUSAND SEVEN HUNDRED AND EIGHTY-FOUR PENCE OUT AND ONE HUNDRED AND EIGHT THOUSAND ONE HUNDRED AND FORTY-FOUR PENCE SHORT. THE BAG HOLDS TWENTY-EIGHT PENCE, BEING TWO SHILLINGS AND FOURPENCE.**

*First column, entered on Friday the twenty-ninth day of July at about six in the evening in that shed: that the said day was not a rota day and was not a wage Tuesday and was not a wipe, and that no charge was placed on that cistern and that the covers lay on it from nine in the morning until four in the afternoon; and that at about eleven the page of days was taken off its nail and a column was set down whole against thirteen days, with the day and the hour put against it, and that it was not said out loud and is not in this slip and not in this column in words; and that beside it stands a refusal with the hour against it and no name against it; and that at about ten the same words were put once to the man of about thirty-two and the hour was written with no name, and that nobody put them a second time on that day; and that at about three the page of routes was taken off its nail and three figures written at the head of its three columns, every state on that page standing there because the people of that route put it there in their own hand, and that this yard put none of the nine there; and that neither this writing nor the one on the second of July was said out loud in a shed and neither is in any slip.*

*Second column, not checkable from a mole, entered on the said Friday at about six in the evening in that shed, and no name is against it: that at about eleven the reason the sum was written and not said is that a count said twice in a room stops being a count and becomes a sentence nine people own, and that this is the third of the three counts this yard has made and the first two have been written once and not said; that at about ten the reason the single asking was met with hearing and no hour for an answer is that an asking answered with an hour becomes a sitting, and a fortnight that ends with a sitting has ended with one man owning the hour; that at about three the reason the page of routes was written without a word out loud is that a state read out in a shed becomes this yard's state, and a state a route puts on itself has to stay in the hand that put it there; and that at about four the reason the disagreement was left to stand into the morrow is that a movement that closes with a settlement has closed its forward pull, and a movement that closes with work still open leaves nine lines to be held by their own people and not by this shed.*
