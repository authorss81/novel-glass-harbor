# Chapter 708

*Tuesday the Twenty-Eighth Day of June, the Eighty-Seventh Wage Tuesday, the Lid Read Out at About Ten in Four Minutes by a Man of About Thirty-One of the Second Lay and Standing at Twenty-Six Lines and One Hundred and Four Marks and Not Moved, the Chest Shut From Ten Until Four, a Woman of About Thirty-Four Asking in About Nine People to Give Up the Choosing of the Times for About a Week and Getting No Answer and No Refusal but the Thing That Would Have to Be True, the Bag at Twenty-Eight Pence, Being Two Shillings and Fourpence, the First Column at Four Hundred and Forty-One and the Second at Two Thousand and Seventy-Nine*

Tuesday the twenty-eighth day of June, with that market open from about nine and the lid of that step uncovered by ten, a yard below that market with a shed standing in it, and about nine people in that shed at half past five that afternoon.

**The eighty-seventh wage Tuesday and not a rota day and not a wipe, and the lid read out at about ten in four minutes by a man of about thirty-one of the second lay and standing at twenty-six lines and one hundred and four marks and not moved, and the chest with the twenty envelopes in it shut from ten until four, and the eighty-seventh week standing fallen due at the close of that day behind the eighty-sixth. A woman of about thirty-four asked in about nine people to give up the choosing of the times for about a week, and she was not answered and she was not refused, and what she got instead was the thing that would have to be true. No charge put on that cistern and its covers lying on it from nine until four. A hundred and sixty-eight pence came into that yard at about ten and a hundred and sixty-eight went out of it at about four, and under that slate is twenty-eight pence, being two shillings and fourpence, and no tin in that shed has been opened since the twenty-ninth of April past.**

**Nine counters about things that are not being done ran a day on this morning. That notice of the seventeenth of May past stands on its four hundred and sixth day. Four hundred and eight days have gone by without anybody asking a bellwright of this harbor, and his chest stays shut. Two objects of nobody's on that crown are into their seven hundred and forty-sixth day. That bench under that pump has stood bare for the five hundred and twelfth night. Two lights about a mile off that point have spent three hundred and thirtieth days with no figure set against them. A six-line sheet at the back of that end stall has reached its two hundred and eighty-fifth day. The paper of four lines in Fish Street is on its three hundredth. A nine-line sheet with a seal on it in that front street has stood three hundred and seventeenth day. A man of about fifty-one has kept three hundred and forty-eight days of not saying anything to anybody about a page of six names. That third column is empty and unappointed and a boy's hand of twenty-two is at the head of it and his surname is on no page and he is off the water. That fourth column stands at one hundred and twenty-five and has taken no line and is neither shut nor struck. About two sea miles off that mole head lie nine hulls this afternoon and nine lay there on the twenty-first of January, and this harbor has not said a word about any of them easing. A man of about twenty-nine hears out of the left side of his head and it is two hundred and six days, one more than the day before, no figure is set against it, and he is not on the water.**

---

The lid was read out at about ten in four minutes and did not move for the eighty-seventh time. The man of about thirty-one stood on that step with the lid open and read the twenty-six lines steady, and about nine people listened without writing anything down. The chest with the twenty envelopes stood shut from ten until four, and the eighty-seventh week stands fallen due at the close of the day behind the eighty-sixth, and the column has not moved on any of the eighty-seven.

The woman is about thirty-four and she fishes off that road and she asked in about nine people and about four of them heard all of it. She said eight days of one voice would get nine boats off that coast before the fourteenth, and that nine times that do not match will not get one, and that the seventh clause on the sheet that came ashore on Thursday is going to be true about some of them in a fortnight. Her reasons are the reasons the yard has been putting on pages since May, and she is not wrong about any of them, and nobody in that nine said she was wrong.

---

What she asked was that the man of about thirty-two give up the choosing of the times for about a week, so that one person can be the one voice for eight days. She did not name the person and she did not say it should be her, and about four people in that nine looked at the floor when she finished. The man of about thirty-two listened without moving and without writing, and about four people watched his hands on the edge of the bench stone.

He did not say no to her. He said the one thing that would have to be true, being that whoever holds the choosing for a week can be named by the nine and dismissed by the nine, and nobody in that nine can name anybody at all this week, so the thing that would have to be true is not true this week. He said it plainly and without raising his voice, and he did not answer her and he did not say no to her either. She wrote his words down in her own hand at about half past three and put the page in her coat and went down the road at about four.

---

The yard did not discuss what he had said after she went. About four people in that nine thought the answer was fair and about four thought it was too narrow, and nobody said either out loud in that shed. The man who keeps the leaf wrote the Tuesday line of the page of that week with the request and the reply against it, and he wrote that she took his words home in her own hand. He did not write that the matter was settled, because it was not settled and no page of the days to come will say it was.

At about five the man of about thirty-two went up the steps by the rail again and nobody said anything about it. He stood at the top for a while looking toward the mole, and then he came down and swept the floor of the shed without being asked. About four people helped him move the benches, and the floor was clean by about half past five when the slip was read.

---

The lid of that step was read at about ten in four minutes and at about half past five on that Tuesday the man who keeps the leaf took the shortfall out loud at that bench stone, in about five hundred and eighteen words, to about nine people, and about four of those nine had heard the request at about noon.

"**The lid was read out at about ten in four minutes by a man of about thirty-one of the second lay and stands at twenty-six lines and one hundred and four marks and did not move for the eighty-seventh time, and the chest with the twenty envelopes in it is shut from ten until four, and the eighty-seventh week stands fallen due at the close of this day behind the eighty-sixth.**

**A woman of about thirty-four asked in about nine people to give up the choosing of the times for about a week so that one person can be the one voice for eight days, and her reasons are the reasons this yard has been putting on pages since May, and she is not wrong about any of them. What she said was that eight days of one voice would get nine boats off that coast before the fourteenth, and that nine times that do not match will not get one, and that the seventh clause is going to be true about some of them in a fortnight.**

**He did not say no to her. What he said back was that whoever holds it can be named by the nine and dismissed by the nine, and that this week nobody in that nine can name anybody, so it is not going to happen this week, and he did not say no to her and he did not answer her and she wrote his words down in her own hand at about half past three and put the page in her coat and went down the road at about four.**

**The sheet stands at one hundred and thirteen thousand two hundred and ten pence out, against ten thousand six hundred and forty pence in, being sixty-six marks, six shillings and eightpence, and it is one hundred and two thousand five hundred and seventy pence short. Four hundred and fifty pence is still on that board in the front street.**

**A hundred and sixty-eight pence in at about ten and a hundred and sixty-eight out at about four, and twenty-eight plus one hundred and sixty-eight less one hundred and sixty-eight is twenty-eight, and under that slate is twenty-eight pence, being two shillings and fourpence, and that is six hundred and twelve pence short of the wage of four marks, being three marks eleven shillings. No tin in that shed has been opened since the twenty-ninth of April past and this yard is now one hundred and twenty-one days without one.**

**And the last of it. That cistern was not charged and its covers lay on it from nine until four and nobody walked on the water. The wall in that shed is still two lines and about eleven inches of empty plaster. About two sea miles off that mole head there are nine hulls this afternoon and there were nine there on the twenty-first of January. A man of about twenty-nine hears out of the left side of his head and it is two hundred and six days today and nothing asked in that nine touched it by a day.**"

Nobody was thanked for the arithmetic and nobody was thanked for a request, and that shed stood empty by about six with its door standing open on a slip nobody was on, and that page of that week had ten lines on it, and that wooden tally slate was still lying flat on that bench stone with nothing on it.

---

**THE LID WAS READ OUT AT ABOUT TEN IN FOUR MINUTES AND STANDS AT TWENTY-SIX LINES AND ONE HUNDRED AND FOUR MARKS AND DID NOT MOVE FOR THE EIGHTY-SEVENTH TIME, WITH THE CHEST SHUT FROM TEN UNTIL FOUR. A WOMAN OF ABOUT THIRTY-FOUR ASKED IN ABOUT NINE PEOPLE TO GIVE UP THE CHOOSING OF THE TIMES FOR ABOUT A WEEK, AND SHE WAS NOT ANSWERED AND NOT REFUSED, AND WHAT SHE GOT WAS THE THING THAT WOULD HAVE TO BE TRUE, BEING THAT WHOEVER HOLDS IT CAN BE NAMED BY THE NINE AND DISMISSED BY THE NINE, AND NOBODY IN THAT NINE CAN NAME ANYBODY THIS WEEK. THE SHEET STANDS AT ONE HUNDRED AND THIRTEEN THOUSAND TWO HUNDRED AND TEN PENCE OUT AND ONE HUNDRED AND TWO THOUSAND FIVE HUNDRED AND SEVENTY PENCE SHORT. THE BAG HOLDS TWENTY-EIGHT PENCE, BEING TWO SHILLINGS AND FOURPENCE.**

---

---

*First column, entered on Tuesday the twenty-eighth day of June at about six in the evening in that shed: that the said day was the eighty-seventh wage Tuesday and was not a rota day and was not a wipe, and that the lid was read out at about ten in four minutes by a man of about thirty-one of the second lay and stands at twenty-six lines and one hundred and four marks and did not move, with the chest with the twenty envelopes in it shut from ten until four, and the eighty-seventh week standing fallen due at the close of the day behind the eighty-sixth; and that a woman of about thirty-four asked in about nine people to give up the choosing of the times for about a week so that one person can be the one voice for eight days, with reasons about nine boats before the fourteenth and nine times that do not match and the seventh clause in a fortnight; and that the man of about thirty-two did not say no to her and did not answer her, and said that whoever holds the choosing can be named by the nine and dismissed by the nine, and that nobody in that nine can name anybody this week; and that she wrote his words down in her own hand at about half past three and put the page in her coat and went down the road at about four.*

*Second column, not checkable from a mole, entered on the said Tuesday at about six in the evening in that shed, and no name is against it: that at about ten in the morning the reason the lid is read in four minutes is that eighty-seven readings the same length are a promise kept, and a longer reading on a day of asking would be heard as a warning; that at about noon the reason her reasons are written down as not wrong is that a yard that writes a request down as foolish will not be brought another request, and this yard needs requests brought to it; that at about half past three the reason his reply is written as no answer and no refusal is that it was both and neither, and a page that calls it one will be read next week as a decision; and that at about four the reason she took his words home in her own hand is that words about naming and dismissing belong to the community that must do the naming, and not to this shed.*
