# Chapter 707

*Monday the Twenty-Seventh Day of June, a Rota Day and the Hundred and Sixty-First Charge on That Cistern, Eleven Trips by Hand and Walked on by None of It, a Fourth Page Down That Road With a Time of Twenty Past Six in the Morning and a Condition of Nine Words Under It in the Same Hand, the Condition Read Out Once and Then Again for Those Who Cannot Read a Column, Accepted by Being Obeyed and Not by Being Agreed To, the Bag at Twenty-Eight Pence, Being Two Shillings and Fourpence, the First Column at Four Hundred and Forty and the Second at Two Thousand and Seventy-Four*

Monday the twenty-seventh day of June, with that market open from about nine and the boards at the back of it swept clean, a yard below that market with a shed standing in it, and about nine people in that shed at half past five that afternoon.

**A rota day and not a wage Tuesday and not a wipe, and that cistern charged in eleven trips by hand between about eight in the morning and about eleven and walked on by none of it, being the hundred and sixty-first charge, and the covers left lying on it from about eleven until about four in the afternoon, and that butt behind the market covered from eight in the morning until four in the afternoon with no charge on it either. A fourth page came down that road with a time on it of twenty past six in the morning and a condition of nine words under it in the same hand. A hundred and sixty-eight pence came into that yard at about ten in the morning and a hundred and sixty-eight went out of it at about four in the afternoon, and under that slate at the close of the day is twenty-eight pence, being two shillings and fourpence, and no tin in that shed has been opened since the twenty-ninth of April past.**

**Nine counters about things that are not being done ran a day on this morning. That notice of the seventeenth of May past stands on its four hundred and fifth day. Four hundred and seven days have gone by without anybody asking a bellwright of this harbor, and his chest stays shut. Two objects of nobody's on that crown are into their seven hundred and forty-fifth day. That bench under that pump has stood bare for the five hundred and eleventh night. Two lights about a mile off that point have spent three hundred and twenty-ninth days with no figure set against them. A six-line sheet at the back of that end stall has reached its two hundred and eighty-fourth day. The paper of four lines in Fish Street is on its two hundred and ninety-ninth. A nine-line sheet with a seal on it in that front street has stood three hundred and sixteenth day. A man of about fifty-one has kept three hundred and forty-seven days of not saying anything to anybody about a page of six names. That third column is empty and unappointed and a boy's hand of twenty-two is at the head of it and his surname is on no page and he is off the water. That fourth column stands at one hundred and twenty-five and has taken no line and is neither shut nor struck. About two sea miles off that mole head lie nine hulls this afternoon and nine lay there on the twenty-first of January, and this harbor has not said a word about any of them easing. A man of about twenty-nine hears out of the left side of his head and it is two hundred and five days, one more than the day before, no figure is set against it, and he is not on the water.**

---

That cistern went its eleventh trip at about eleven for the hundred and sixty-first time and the covers went on and came off at the same hours as every other charge. The men who carry it did not speak about the sheet that came ashore on Thursday, and about four people who came for water asked about the households on the hill and were told they had what they needed for the night. The man who keeps the leaf wrote the charge number against the day and the hours of the covering, as he does on every rota day.

The fourth page came down the road at about ten with a time on it and a condition under it in the same hand. The time is twenty past six in the morning, and the condition is nine words long and stands under the time in the same small pressing hand as the second page. The page is the same size as the first two and folded the same way, and it lay on the bench stone until about eleven before anybody touched it.

---

A man of about thirty-eight read the condition out in about nine people and would not read it twice and would not say where he had it from and was not asked twice. He stood at the back of the nine with his hands behind him, and he read in a low voice and then stepped back. The condition says the two people who ring that route's bell may not be the same two on two consecutive days, and that the change is that route's own business.

The man of about thirty-two read it himself, out loud, twice, because about five of the nine cannot read a column. He read slowly the second time and he did not add a word to it and did not take a word from it. Then he said the thing that holds the day together, being that a condition on a bell is that bell's own business and that this yard is not a party to it and cannot enforce it and would not try. About four people in that nine said the condition was a good one and about four said nothing and nobody said it was a condition this yard had agreed to.

---

The turn of the day is obedience without agreement. The condition is accepted by being obeyed and not by being agreed to, and the yard's own page says it accepted nothing and has changed nothing. The two people who will ring that bell on the days of that time are two people neither of whom has been on this yard's page, and this yard will not write their description down. A page that names the hands that ring is a page that can be shown to neighbours, and this yard keeps no such page.

At about three the woman of thirty of no office wrote the Monday line of the page of that week with the time and the fact of the condition against it, but not the condition itself in full. She wrote that a fourth time came with a condition of nine words, and that the condition concerns who rings and on which days, and that this yard accepted nothing. The man who keeps the leaf read what she had written and left it standing, because her hand on that page is her work and not his to correct.

---

A condition of nine words came down that road under a time and at about half past five on that Monday the man who keeps the leaf took the shortfall out loud at that bench stone, in about four hundred and fifty-four words, to about nine people, and about four of those nine had heard the condition read twice at about eleven.

"**That cistern went its eleventh trip at about eleven this morning for the hundred and sixty-first time, and the covers went on and came off and nobody walked on the water. A fourth page came down the road with a time of twenty past six in the morning and a condition of nine words under it in the same hand, and a man of about thirty-eight read the condition out in about nine people and would not read it twice and would not say where he had it from and was not asked twice.**

**The man of about thirty-two read it himself, out loud, twice, because about five of the nine cannot read a column. What he said after it was that a condition on a bell is that bell's own business and that this yard is not a party to it and cannot enforce it and would not try. About four in that nine said the condition was a good one and about four said nothing and nobody said it was a condition this yard had agreed to.**

**The condition is accepted by being obeyed and not by being agreed to, and our page says we accepted nothing and have changed nothing. The two who will ring are two this yard has never written down, and this yard will not write them down now.**

**The sheet stands at one hundred and thirteen thousand two hundred and ten pence out, against ten thousand six hundred and forty pence in, being sixty-six marks, six shillings and eightpence, and it is one hundred and two thousand five hundred and seventy pence short. Four hundred and fifty pence is still on that board in the front street.**

**A hundred and sixty-eight pence in at about ten and a hundred and sixty-eight out at about four, and twenty-eight plus one hundred and sixty-eight less one hundred and sixty-eight is twenty-eight, and under that slate is twenty-eight pence, being two shillings and fourpence, and that is six hundred and twelve pence short of the wage of four marks, being three marks eleven shillings. No tin in that shed has been opened since the twenty-ninth of April past and this yard is now one hundred and twenty days without one.**

**And the last of it. The wall in that shed is still two lines and about eleven inches of empty plaster. About two sea miles off that mole head there are nine hulls this afternoon and there were nine there on the twenty-first of January. A man of about twenty-nine hears out of the left side of his head and it is two hundred and five days today and nothing with a condition on it touched it by a day.**"

Nobody was thanked for the arithmetic and nobody was thanked for a page, and that shed stood empty by about six with its door standing open on a slip nobody was on, and that page of that week had nine lines on it, and that wooden tally slate was still lying flat on that bench stone with nothing on it.

---

**A FOURTH PAGE CAME DOWN THAT ROAD WITH A TIME OF TWENTY PAST SIX IN THE MORNING AND A CONDITION OF NINE WORDS UNDER IT IN THE SAME HAND, READ OUT ONCE BY A MAN OF ABOUT THIRTY-EIGHT AND THEN TWICE BY THE MAN OF ABOUT THIRTY-TWO FOR THOSE WHO CANNOT READ A COLUMN. THE CONDITION CONCERNS WHO RINGS AND ON WHICH DAYS AND IS THAT BELL'S OWN BUSINESS. IT IS ACCEPTED BY BEING OBEYED AND NOT BY BEING AGREED TO, AND THE YARD'S PAGE SAYS IT ACCEPTED NOTHING AND HAS CHANGED NOTHING. THAT CISTERN WAS CHARGED FOR THE HUNDRED AND SIXTY-FIRST TIME. THE SHEET STANDS AT ONE HUNDRED AND THIRTEEN THOUSAND TWO HUNDRED AND TEN PENCE OUT AND ONE HUNDRED AND TWO THOUSAND FIVE HUNDRED AND SEVENTY PENCE SHORT. THE BAG HOLDS TWENTY-EIGHT PENCE, BEING TWO SHILLINGS AND FOURPENCE.**

---

---

*First column, entered on Monday the twenty-seventh day of June at about six in the evening in that shed: that the said day was a rota day and was not a wage Tuesday and was not a wipe, and that that cistern was charged in eleven trips by hand between about eight in the morning and about eleven and was walked on by none of it, being the hundred and sixty-first charge, with the covers lying on it from about eleven until about four; and that a fourth page came down the road with a time of twenty past six in the morning and a condition of nine words under it in the same hand; and that a man of about thirty-eight read the condition out in about nine people and would not read it twice and would not say where he had it from and was not asked twice; and that the man of about thirty-two read it himself out loud twice, because about five of the nine cannot read a column, and then said that a condition on a bell is that bell's own business and that this yard is not a party to it and cannot enforce it and would not try; and that about four in that nine said the condition was a good one and about four said nothing and nobody said it was a condition this yard had agreed to.*

*Second column, not checkable from a mole, entered on the said Monday at about six in the evening in that shed, and no name is against it: that at about eleven in the morning the reason the first reading was not repeated is that a condition read twice by a man who will not say where he had it from is a condition heard once too often, and the second reading belonged to a voice the nine trust; that at about noon the reason obedience without agreement is written down is that a yard that writes it agreed to a condition will be taken to enforce it next week, and a yard that writes it obeyed what it must live inside keeps its hands off; that at about three the reason the condition itself is not copied in full is that nine words on another community's page are that community's, and a copy in this shed would be a second version for neighbours to argue with; and that at about half past five the reason the slip ends on the nine hulls is that a day with a condition on it is a day this harbor will test, and the hulls that did not move are the thing that waited while it was tested.*
