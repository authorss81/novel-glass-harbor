# Chapter 731

*Thursday the Twenty-First Day of July, a Rota Day and the Hundred and Sixty-Eighth Charge on That Cistern, Line Eight Settled Into the Foot of That Money Sheet at About Four With the Nineteenth for the Work and This Day for the Settling, the Bag at Twenty-Eight Pence, Being Two Shillings and Fourpence, the First Column at Four Hundred and Sixty-Four and the Second at Two Thousand One Hundred and Ninety-Four*

Thursday the twenty-first day of July, with that market open from about nine and a bright morning with cloud building after noon, a yard below that market with a shed standing in it, and about nine people in that shed at half past five that afternoon.

**A rota day and not a wage Tuesday and not a wipe, and that cistern charged in eleven trips by hand between about eight in the morning and about eleven and walked on by none of it, being the hundred and sixty-eighth charge, and the covers left lying on it from about eleven until about four in the afternoon. Line eight was settled into the foot of that money sheet at about four, carrying Tuesday the nineteenth day of July for the work and this Thursday for the settling, being nine hundred and ninety pence, being six marks, two shillings and sixpence, in five parts. The sheet stands at one hundred and sixteen thousand seven hundred and forty-six pence out and one hundred and six thousand one hundred and six pence short, against ten thousand six hundred and forty pence in. A hundred and sixty-eight pence came into that yard at about ten and a hundred and sixty-eight went out of it at about four, and under that slate is twenty-eight pence, being two shillings and fourpence, and no tin in that shed has been opened since the twenty-ninth of April past.**

**Nine tallies of matters left to stand ran one day on this morning. That notice of the seventeenth of May past moves onto its four hundred and twenty-ninth day. Four hundred and thirty-one days have passed with nobody asking a bellwright of this harbor, and his chest stays shut. Two objects of nobody's on that crown stand into their seven hundred and sixty-ninth day. That bench under that pump has lain bare for the five hundred and thirty-fifth night. Two lights about a mile off that point have kept three hundred and fifty-three days with no figure put against them. A six-line sheet at the back of that end stall has come to its three hundred and eighth day. The paper of four lines in Fish Street moves onto its three hundred and twenty-third. A nine-line sheet with a seal on it in that front street has reached its three hundred and fortieth day. A man of about fifty-one has spent three hundred and seventy-one days saying nothing to anybody about a page of six names. That third column is empty and unappointed and a boy's hand of twenty-two is at the head of it and his surname is on no page and he is off the water. That fourth column stands at one hundred and twenty-five and has taken no line and is neither shut nor struck. About two sea miles off that mole head lie nine hulls this afternoon and nine lay there on the twenty-first of January, and this harbor has not said a word about any of them easing. A man of about twenty-nine hears out of the left side of his head and it is two hundred and twenty-nine days, one more than the day before, no figure is set against it, and he is not on the water.**

---

That cistern went its eleventh trip at about eleven for the hundred and sixty-eighth time and the covers went on at the same hour as the last charge. About four people came for water after four and were served without waiting, and the keeper wrote the charge number and the hours. He wrote beside them that the next charge falls on a Monday, and he read the entry back to about four who stood at the butt.

---

The morning work besides the water was the mending of gaskets and the splicing of a chafed length of line. About four men cut out two worn places and whipped the splices and tarred them, and about four more lifted the pump housing and set it true. The boat that was washed yesterday lay dry along the harbour side and her seams were gone over for weeping. By noon the splices were hard and the housing sat even and the yard was clear for the settling.

---

The public copy of first minutes stayed on the dry end of that bench stone with the pressure sheet beside it under the same four stones. Nobody moved either sheet before noon and about nine people passed them in the course of the morning. About four of that nine cannot read a column and the keeper left the hours facing outward so that they could be read without lifting a stone. At about noon he turned both sheets to the light and read nothing out, and the stones went back on the corners.

---

The man of about thirty-two worked on the line all morning with his hands on the splice and the tar. He carried water at about ten and carried gaskets at about eleven, and he spoke of the housing and the splice and nothing else. Nobody in that shed put to him the words about going down on this day, and no hour of that kind was written. About four people spoke with him of the coil and about four spoke of the butt, and the day went by without that putting.

---

At about two the woman of thirty of no office took the second of the two books off the shelf above that butt and opened it on the bench stone. She read the entries of the nineteenth and the twentieth out for about five who cannot read a column, once through without haste. About four people said the figures were plain and about four said nothing at all, and the book went back on the shelf before three. The first book stayed shut on the flat of the stone with its wet corner turned outward.

---

The disagreement between the two people was spoken of at about three and was left where it was. He said a refusal kept in two places looks kept by more hands than hold it, and that a page printing who did not agree makes a refusal answerable in the street. She said what she keeps in both is what she was told and never a name against a person, and that one hand writing twice is one keeping and not two followings. About four agreed with her and about four agreed with him and nobody said either was wrong, and the matter is open between them and is not settled and no page in that yard carries a resolution of it.

---

At about four the keeper took the money sheet off its nail in about nine people and laid it on the flat of that bench stone. He said the work was done on Tuesday the nineteenth day of July and the settling is this Thursday, and both days are on the face of the line. He named the five parts with the multiplications before he wrote any sum, and about nine listened without moving the stones on the other two sheets.

---

He read ninety yards of line at three pence the yard for two hundred and seventy pence, and two bell seats cut and faced at eighteen pence apiece for thirty-six pence. He read a watch for the tender and an independent line at sixty pence, and nine men at two days at sixteen pence a man-day at nine times two times sixteen for two hundred and eighty-eight pence. He read two days of a boat hired at the figure in chalk at two times one hundred and sixty-eight for three hundred and thirty-six pence, and two hundred and seventy plus thirty-six plus sixty plus two hundred and eighty-eight plus three hundred and thirty-six is nine hundred and ninety.

---

He wrote the sum down after the reading and not before it, being nine hundred and ninety pence, being six marks, two shillings and sixpence. He said the foot of the sheet stands where it stood through yesterday, at one hundred and sixteen thousand seven hundred and forty-six pence out, and that the new sum steps into the foot from tomorrow. About four people said they had heard the parts and about four said they had followed the reckoning, and nobody asked for the reading twice.

---

The bell off that mole answered the bell off a shoal four days down that coast road at about half past ten and again at about three, and both answers were in hours and not in names. About nine people waited at the end stall until about ten past six and then went home, and the keeper wrote the hour they stopped waiting and no reason against it. The coil stayed on the pin and the housing held when it was tried at about half past four, and the covers lay on that cistern until four.

---

At about half past five on that Thursday the settling and the water were read out at that bench stone in about five hundred and thirty words to about nine people, and about four of those nine had carried water from that butt after four.

"**Eleven trips went up by hand between eight and eleven for the hundred and sixty-eighth time on that butt at the back. The covers lay on it from eleven until four. About four came for water after four and were served without waiting, and I wrote the number and the hours and that the next charge falls on a Monday.**

"**Line eight went into the foot of the money sheet at about four with two days on its face. The work was done on Tuesday the nineteenth and the settling is this Thursday. I named the five parts with the reckonings before any sum was written. Ninety yards of line at three pence the yard is two hundred and seventy, and two seats at eighteen pence apiece is thirty-six, and a watch and an independent line is sixty. Nine men at two days at sixteen pence a man-day is nine times two times sixteen for two hundred and eighty-eight, and two days of a boat at the figure in chalk is two times one hundred and sixty-eight for three hundred and thirty-six. The five together are nine hundred and ninety, being six marks, two shillings and sixpence, and the foot stands through today at one hundred and sixteen thousand seven hundred and forty-six.**

"**The public copy and the pressure sheet lay on the dry end all day under four stones and neither was moved before noon. About nine passed them in the morning and about five had the hours left facing outward. At noon I turned both to the light and read nothing out, and the stones went back. The man of about thirty-two worked on the splice and the tar all morning and carried water and gaskets, and no words of that one kind were put to him on this day and no hour of that kind was written.**

"**The sheet stands at one hundred and sixteen thousand seven hundred and forty-six pence out and one hundred and six thousand one hundred and six pence short, against ten thousand six hundred and forty pence in. Four hundred and fifty pence is still on that board in the front street. A hundred and sixty-eight pence in at about ten and a hundred and sixty-eight out at about four, and twenty-eight plus two thousand one hundred and eighty-one less two thousand one hundred and eighty-one is twenty-eight, and under that slate is twenty-eight pence, being two shillings and fourpence, and that is six hundred and twelve pence short of the wage of four marks, being three marks eleven shillings. No tin in that shed has been opened since the twenty-ninth of April past and this yard is now one hundred and forty-four days without one.**

"**And the last of it. The far bench was occupied from ten until four with nothing put on it. About two sea miles off that mole head there are nine hulls this afternoon and there were nine there on the twenty-first of January. A man of about twenty-nine hears out of the left side of his head and it is two hundred and twenty-nine days today and nothing settled at four touched it by a day.**"

Nobody was thanked for the arithmetic and nobody was thanked for eleven trips of water by hand, and that shed stood empty by about six with its door standing open on a slip nobody was on, and that page of that week had thirty lines on it, and that wooden tally slate was still lying flat on that bench stone with nothing on it.

---

**LINE EIGHT WAS SETTLED AT ABOUT FOUR WITH BOTH DAYS ON ITS FACE AND THE FIVE PARTS READ WITH THE RECKONINGS. THAT CISTERN WAS CHARGED FOR THE HUNDRED AND SIXTY-EIGHTH TIME AND WALKED ON BY NONE OF IT. NO WORDS OF THE ONE KIND WERE PUT ON THIS DAY. THE SHEET STANDS AT ONE HUNDRED AND SIXTEEN THOUSAND SEVEN HUNDRED AND FORTY-SIX PENCE OUT AND ONE HUNDRED AND SIX THOUSAND ONE HUNDRED AND SIX PENCE SHORT. THE BAG HOLDS TWENTY-EIGHT PENCE, BEING TWO SHILLINGS AND FOURPENCE.**

---

---

*First column, entered on Thursday the twenty-first day of July at about six in the evening in that shed: that the said day was a rota day and was not a wage Tuesday and was not a wipe, and that that cistern was charged in eleven trips by hand between about eight and about eleven and was walked on by none of it, being the hundred and sixty-eighth charge, with the covers lying on it from about eleven until about four; and that line eight was settled into the foot at about four, carrying Tuesday the nineteenth for the work and this Thursday for the settling, being nine hundred and ninety pence in five parts with the reckonings named before the sum; and that the public copy and the pressure sheet lay on the dry end under four stones and neither was moved before noon; and that the man of about thirty-two worked on splice and tar all morning and no words of the one kind were put to him and no hour of that kind was written; and that at about three the disagreement between two people was spoken of and is not settled.*

*Second column, not checkable from a mole, entered on the said Thursday at about six in the evening in that shed, and no name is against it: that at about eleven the reason the covers were left lying until four is that water drawn through an uncovered butt in a dusty yard is water that has to be drawn twice, and a cover lifted for airing is a cover that lets in the yard; that at about noon the reason the hours were left facing outward is that about five of that party cannot read a column upside down, and a sheet turned for their sake is a sheet that stays unmoved; that at about two the reason the second book was read and the first left shut is that a wet corner turned outward is a warning against handling, and a dry copy read once is handling spared to the damaged leaf; and that at about four the reason the five parts were named before any sum is that a sum heard before its parts is a sum taken on trust, and this yard does not ask nine people to take a sum on trust.*
