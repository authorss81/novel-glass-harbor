# Chapter 712

*Saturday the Second Day of July, Not a Rota Day and Not a Wage Tuesday and Not a Wipe, Nine in Contact by Bell and by a Person With a Hand on a Rope by About Ten in the Morning, the Count of the Hands That Rang Taken Off a Page of Days at About Half Past Three and Written Down Once and Said Nowhere, Being Six and Five and Four and Four and Three and Three and Two and Two and One, Being Thirty in Sum, the Three Columns of the Page of Routes Written for the First of Three, Being Two and Three and Four, Line Five Settled and the Sheet at One Hundred and Fourteen Thousand Six Hundred and Sixty-Six Pence Out, the Bag at Twenty-Eight Pence, Being Two Shillings and Fourpence, the First Column at Four Hundred and Forty-Five and the Second at Two Thousand and Ninety-Nine*

Saturday the second day of July, with that market open from about nine and the end stall crowded by ten, a yard below that market with a shed standing in it, and about nine people in that shed at half past five that afternoon.

**Not a rota day and not a wage Tuesday and not a wipe, and no charge put on that cistern and its covers lying on it from nine in the morning until four in the afternoon, and that butt behind the market covered from eight in the morning until four in the afternoon with no charge on it either. Nine bells answered one another by about ten in the morning, the ninth answering the eighth after the eighth had answered the seventh at about nine. The count of the hands that rang was taken off a page of days at about half past three and written down once and said nowhere. The three columns of the page of routes were written for the first of three at about the same hour. Line five was settled into the total at about four in the afternoon and the sheet stands at one hundred and fourteen thousand six hundred and sixty-six pence out and one hundred and four thousand and twenty-six pence short. A hundred and sixty-eight pence came into that yard at about ten and a hundred and sixty-eight went out of it at about four, and under that slate is twenty-eight pence, being two shillings and fourpence, and no tin in that shed has been opened since the twenty-ninth of April past.**

**Nine counters about things that are not being done ran a day on this morning. That notice of the seventeenth of May past stands on its four hundred and tenth day. Four hundred and twelve days have gone by without anybody asking a bellwright of this harbor, and his chest stays shut. Two objects of nobody's on that crown are into their seven hundred and fiftieth day. That bench under that pump has stood bare for the five hundred and sixteenth night. Two lights about a mile off that point have spent three hundred and thirty-fourth days with no figure set against them. A six-line sheet at the back of that end stall has reached its two hundred and eighty-ninth day. The paper of four lines in Fish Street is on its three hundred and fourth. A nine-line sheet with a seal on it in that front street has stood three hundred and twenty-first day. A man of about fifty-one has kept three hundred and fifty-two days of not saying anything to anybody about a page of six names. That third column is empty and unappointed and a boy's hand of twenty-two is at the head of it and his surname is on no page and he is off the water. That fourth column stands at one hundred and twenty-five and has taken no line and is neither shut nor struck. About two sea miles off that mole head lie nine hulls this afternoon and nine lay there on the twenty-first of January, and this harbor has not said a word about any of them easing. A man of about twenty-nine hears out of the left side of his head and it is two hundred and ten days, one more than the day before, no figure is set against it, and he is not on the water.**

---

At about ten in the morning the ninth bell answered the eighth, and the eighth had answered the seventh at about nine, and the man who keeps the leaf wrote the times down in a column with no route names in it. A page with route names in it and a time against it is a schedule and this is not that, and the column carries hours and no names. By about ten all nine were in contact by bell and by a person with a hand on a rope, and the man of about thirty-two stood on that mole and listened to the last of them come in and did not raise a hand.

All nine in contact is not the same thing as nine ready, and the shed knew it. Some pages carry a time and some carry a reason and one carries nothing at all, and all nine rang. The sheet of the hands that rang has been running for twelve days with a base of nothing on it, and at about half past three the man who keeps the leaf took the page of days off its nail and set the column down whole against all twelve days. The yard did not send for anybody to witness it, and about nine people were there because they come at that hour.

---

The figures at the close of today stand at six and five and four and four and three and three and two and two and one, and six plus five plus four plus four plus three plus three plus two plus two plus one is thirty. He put the day and the hour against the sum and did not say the sum out loud and nobody in that shed asked him for it, and it is not in this evening's slip and it is not in the first column in words. It is a count of hands and not a count of persons, not a head-count, not a roll, not a count of bells, not a count of strokes and not a count of boats, and a community that put no hand on its own rope on a day is nowhere on it and is not counted against anybody.

At about the same hour he took the page of routes off its nail and wrote three figures at the head of its three columns, being two and three and four, and two plus three plus four is nine. The two that are held are the Saltmarch Weatherway and the Morrow Line, and the Morrow Line put itself there on Saturday morning in its own hand and this yard did not put it there and did not ask it to. The three that are restricted are the Ashtide Run, the Ternwatch Passage and the Lowfen Reed, and the four that are dormant are the Cinder Bell, the Tidewatch Channel, the Pale Shoals and the Orison Descent. That is the first of three times and the other two are in the last movement and on the last day, and neither of them is in a shed's mouth.

---

Line five was settled into the total at about four in the afternoon, being two seats at eighteen pence for thirty-six pence, and a shackle and a swivel for forty pence, and six men at one day at sixteen pence for ninety-six pence, and two days of a boat at one hundred and sixty-eight for three hundred and thirty-six pence, and those four are five hundred and eight pence, being three marks, two shillings and fourpence. The sheet stands at one hundred and fourteen thousand six hundred and sixty-six pence out, being more than seven hundred marks, against ten thousand six hundred and forty pence in, being sixty-six marks, six shillings and eightpence, and it is one hundred and four thousand and twenty-six pence short. The three lines of these twelve days are four hundred and ninety-four and nine hundred and forty-eight and five hundred and eight pence, being one thousand nine hundred and fifty pence, and every one of them carries its multiplication on the face of the sheet.

The woman of thirty of no office stood at the end stall at about five and looked at the page of days where the thirty stands with the hour against it. She did not read it out and she did not copy it, and she said out loud that nine bells in contact is a thing to keep and thirty hands written down once is the way to keep it. The man of about thirty-two said nothing to that, and the disagreement between them stands open and is not settled and no page carries a resolution of it.

---

And at about half past five on that Saturday the man who keeps the leaf took the shortfall out loud at that bench stone, in about nine hundred words, to about nine people, and about four of those nine had heard the ninth bell answer at about ten in the morning.

"**Nine bells answered one another by about ten this morning, the ninth answering the eighth after the eighth had answered the seventh at about nine, and I wrote the hours in a column with no names, because a page with names and a time is a schedule and this is not that. All nine are in contact by bell and by a person with a hand on a rope, and all nine in contact is not the same as nine ready.**

"**At about half past three I took the page of days off its nail and set the column down whole, and I put the day and the hour against the sum and did not say the sum out loud and nobody asked me for it, and it is not in this slip and not in the first column in words. At about the same hour I took the page of routes off its nail and wrote three figures at the head of its three columns, and one of the nine changed its own state on its own saying and this yard did not put it there. That is the first of three and the other two are not in a shed's mouth.**

"**Line five was settled at about four, being two seats for thirty-six, and a shackle and a swivel for forty, and six men at one day for ninety-six, and two days of a boat for three hundred and thirty-six, and those four are five hundred and eight pence, being three marks, two shillings and fourpence. The sheet is one hundred and fourteen thousand six hundred and sixty-six pence out and one hundred and four thousand and twenty-six pence short, and the three lines of these twelve days are four hundred and ninety-four and nine hundred and forty-eight and five hundred and eight, being one thousand nine hundred and fifty pence, with every multiplication on the face of it. Four hundred and fifty pence is still on that board in the front street.**

"**A hundred and sixty-eight pence in at about ten and a hundred and sixty-eight out at about four, and twenty-eight plus one hundred and sixty-eight less one hundred and sixty-eight is twenty-eight, and under that slate is twenty-eight pence, being two shillings and fourpence, and that is six hundred and twelve pence short of the wage of four marks, being three marks eleven shillings. No tin in that shed has been opened since the twenty-ninth of April past and this yard is now one hundred and twenty-five days without one.**

"**And the last of it. That cistern was not charged and its covers lay on it from nine until four and nobody walked on the water. The wall in that shed is still two lines and about eleven inches of empty plaster. About two sea miles off that mole head there are nine hulls this afternoon and there were nine there on the twenty-first of January. A man of about twenty-nine hears out of the left side of his head and it is two hundred and ten days today and nothing counted on those pages touched it by a day.**"

Nobody was thanked for the arithmetic and nobody was thanked for ringing, and that shed stood empty by about six with its door standing open on a slip nobody was on, and that page of that week had fourteen lines on it, and that wooden tally slate was still lying flat on that bench stone with nothing on it.

**NINE BELLS IN CONTACT BY ABOUT TEN IN THE MORNING, THE NINTH ANSWERING THE EIGHTH AFTER THE EIGHTH HAD ANSWERED THE SEVENTH AT ABOUT NINE, WITH HOURS IN A COLUMN AND NO NAMES. THE COUNT TAKEN ONCE AT ABOUT HALF PAST THREE AND WRITTEN DOWN ONCE AND SAID NOWHERE, WITH THE DAY AND THE HOUR AGAINST IT. THE THREE COLUMNS OF THE PAGE OF ROUTES WRITTEN FOR THE FIRST OF THREE AT ABOUT THE SAME HOUR, BEING TWO AND THREE AND FOUR. LINE FIVE SETTLED AT ABOUT FOUR, BEING FIVE HUNDRED AND EIGHT PENCE, BEING THREE MARKS, TWO SHILLINGS AND FOURPENCE, AND THE SHEET AT ONE HUNDRED AND FOURTEEN THOUSAND SIX HUNDRED AND SIXTY-SIX PENCE OUT AND ONE HUNDRED AND FOUR THOUSAND AND TWENTY-SIX PENCE SHORT. THE BAG HOLDS TWENTY-EIGHT PENCE, BEING TWO SHILLINGS AND FOURPENCE.**

*First column, entered on Saturday the second day of July at about six in the evening in that shed: that the said day was not a rota day and was not a wage Tuesday and was not a wipe, and that no charge was placed on that cistern and that the covers lay on it from nine in the morning until four in the afternoon; and that nine bells answered one another by about ten in the morning, the ninth answering the eighth after the eighth had answered the seventh at about nine, with the hours in a column and no names; and that at about half past three the page of days was taken off its nail and the column set down whole, with the day and the hour against the sum, and the sum not said out loud and not in this slip and not in this column in words; and that at about the same hour the page of routes was taken off its nail and three figures written at the head of its three columns, with one of the nine having changed its own state on its own saying; and that line five was settled at about four, being two seats for thirty-six pence and a shackle and swivel for forty pence and six men at one day for ninety-six pence and two days of a boat for three hundred and thirty-six pence, being five hundred and eight pence, being three marks, two shillings and fourpence.*

*Second column, not checkable from a mole, entered on the said Saturday at about six in the evening in that shed, and no name is against it: that at about ten in the morning the reason hours stand without names is that a page with names and a time is a schedule, and this movement was asked when it can be in contact and not what hour it promises; that at about half past three the reason the sum is written and not said is that a second time a thing is said in a room is the time it stops being a count and becomes a sentence nine people own, and this is the first of three and the other two are not in a shed's mouth; that at about the same hour the reason a state put on by its own people is entered as what it is is that a route putting itself in a column is that route's to do, and this party calling it a count in a room would make it this party's; and that at about four the reason three lines stand with every multiplication on the face of the sheet is that a total four of the nine cannot check is a total five own, and this sheet is now checked by any stranger with a pencil.*
