# Chapter 706

*Sunday the Twenty-Sixth Day of June, a Wipe and the Hundred and Twelfth Time, Twenty-Nine Minutes Off the Wall and Sixteen Standing Bare and Twenty-Nine to Get It Back, Being Seventy-Four the Whole of It, Three Sundays in a Row on One Line for the First Time in Nine Runs of Days, the Count of the Reasons Zero and the Count of the Questions Nobody Has Asked Zero, the Bag at Twenty-Eight Pence, Being Two Shillings and Fourpence, the First Column at Four Hundred and Thirty-Nine and the Second at Two Thousand and Sixty-Nine*

Sunday the twenty-sixth day of June, with that market shut all day and the office on the west basin shut with it, a yard below that market with a shed standing in it, and about nine people in that shed at half past five that afternoon.

**A wipe and not a rota day and not a wage Tuesday, and that wall behind a reeve's bench taken down at about ten past ten and bare by about forty to eleven and back on its holders at about seventeen minutes past eleven, at the angle it has carried since the twelfth of January, and the thing above the frame still not whole. Twenty-nine minutes off the wall, sixteen standing bare, twenty-nine to get it back, which is seventy-four minutes the whole of it. No charge put on that cistern and its covers lying on it from nine in the morning until four in the afternoon. A hundred and sixty-eight pence came into that yard at about ten in the morning and a hundred and sixty-eight went out of it at about four in the afternoon, and under that slate at the close of the day is twenty-eight pence, being two shillings and fourpence, and no tin in that shed has been opened since the twenty-ninth of April past.**

**Nine counters about things that are not being done ran a day on this morning. That notice of the seventeenth of May past stands on its four hundred and fourth day. Four hundred and six days have gone by without anybody asking a bellwright of this harbor, and his chest stays shut. Two objects of nobody's on that crown are into their seven hundred and forty-fourth day. That bench under that pump has stood bare for the five hundred and tenth night. Two lights about a mile off that point have spent three hundred and twenty-eighth days with no figure set against them. A six-line sheet at the back of that end stall has reached its two hundred and eighty-third day. The paper of four lines in Fish Street is on its two hundred and ninety-eighth. A nine-line sheet with a seal on it in that front street has stood three hundred and fifteenth day. A man of about fifty-one has kept three hundred and forty-six days of not saying anything to anybody about a page of six names. That third column is empty and unappointed and a boy's hand of twenty-two is at the head of it and his surname is on no page and he is off the water. That fourth column stands at one hundred and twenty-five and has taken no line and is neither shut nor struck. About two sea miles off that mole head lie nine hulls this afternoon and nine lay there on the twenty-first of January, and this harbor has not said a word about any of them easing. A man of about twenty-nine hears out of the left side of his head and it is two hundred and four days, one more than the day before, no figure is set against it, and he is not on the water.**

---

Two men began at about ten past ten and the wall was bare by about forty to eleven and back on its holders at about seventeen minutes past eleven. They work without speaking and they lay each piece down in order on the floor cloth, and about four people watched from the doorway without going in. The frame is heavy and the plaster behind it is bare, and the thing above the frame where the wall meets the beam is still not whole and has not been made whole since winter.

Twenty-nine minutes off the wall, sixteen standing bare, twenty-nine to get it back, which is seventy-four minutes the whole of it, against twenty-seven and fourteen and twenty-seven on the Sunday before and twenty-six and thirteen and twenty-six on the Sunday before that. It is a minute longer each way and a minute longer bare than the Sunday before, and nobody has said why, and the yard has stopped asking the two men who do it. The man who keeps the leaf put all three Sundays on one line in the first column with the day against each, which is the first time that has been done in nine runs of days.

---

About four people in that nine who cannot read a column said the three Sundays on one line was a new thing, and the man who keeps the leaf said it was a thing he should have done in May. He said a ladder written one Sunday at a time is a ladder nobody can climb to see, and three Sundays together show the slowing plain. He wrote the twenty-ninth of May and the fifth of June figures beside the twenty-sixth of June figures, with the days against each, and he read them out twice because about five of the nine cannot read a column.

Nobody has said why it is getting slower and this yard has stopped asking the two men who do it, and that stopping is entered in the second column with the hour against it and no name against it. The two men are older than the wall and they handle it gently, and a question put to men handling a heavy frame is a weight on top of a weight. The count of the reasons is zero and the count of the questions nobody has asked is also zero, and both are on the page with the hour against them.

---

The man of about thirty-two came to the shed at about three and looked at the wall where it stands at its angle since the twelfth of January. He did not touch it and he did not ask about it, and he went up the steps by the rail slowly and came down again slowly. About four people saw him on the rail and nobody said anything about it, and the first column carries the hour he was there and nothing about what he thought.

At about four the woman of thirty of no office came with her own book and copied the three Sundays onto a page of her own. She wrote the days and the three figures of each, and she wrote underneath that nobody has said why it is slower. She did not ask the two men either, and she said out loud that a book that keeps figures without reasons is a book that can be checked, and a book that keeps reasons without figures is a story.

---

And at about half past five on that Sunday the man who keeps the leaf took the shortfall out loud at that bench stone, in about nine hundred words, to about nine people, and about four of those nine had watched the wall go back at about seventeen minutes past eleven.

"**Two men began at about ten past ten and the wall was bare by about forty to eleven and back at about seventeen minutes past eleven, at the angle it has carried since the twelfth of January, and the thing above the frame is still not whole. Twenty-nine off, sixteen bare, twenty-nine back, which is seventy-four the whole of it, against twenty-seven and fourteen and twenty-seven last Sunday and twenty-six and thirteen and twenty-six the Sunday before that.**

"**I have put all three Sundays on one line in the first column with the day against each, and that is the first time it has been done in nine runs of days. About four in this nine said it was a new thing and I said it was a thing I should have done in May. A ladder written one Sunday at a time is a ladder nobody can climb to see, and three Sundays together show the slowing plain.**

"**Nobody has said why it is getting slower and this yard has stopped asking the two men who do it, and that stopping is in the second column with the hour against it and no name against it. The count of the reasons is zero and the count of the questions nobody has asked is also zero.**

"**The sheet stands at one hundred and thirteen thousand two hundred and ten pence out, being more than seven hundred and four marks, against ten thousand six hundred and forty pence in, being sixty-six marks, six shillings and eightpence, and it is one hundred and two thousand five hundred and seventy pence short. Four hundred and fifty pence is still on that board in the front street.**

"**A hundred and sixty-eight pence in at about ten and a hundred and sixty-eight out at about four, and twenty-eight plus one hundred and sixty-eight less one hundred and sixty-eight is twenty-eight, and under that slate is twenty-eight pence, being two shillings and fourpence, and that is six hundred and twelve pence short of the wage of four marks, being three marks eleven shillings. No tin in that shed has been opened since the twenty-ninth of April past and this yard is now one hundred and nineteen days without one.**

"**And the last of it. That cistern was not charged and its covers lay on it from nine until four and nobody walked on the water. The far bench was occupied from ten until four with nothing on it. About two sea miles off that mole head there are nine hulls this afternoon and there were nine there on the twenty-first of January. A man of about twenty-nine hears out of the left side of his head and it is two hundred and four days today and nothing on that wall touched it by a day.**"

Nobody was thanked for the arithmetic and nobody was thanked for a wall, and that shed stood empty by about six with its door standing open on a slip nobody was on, and that page of that week had eight lines on it, and that wooden tally slate was still lying flat on that bench stone with nothing on it.

---

**THAT WALL BEHIND A REEVE'S BENCH CAME DOWN AT ABOUT TEN PAST TEN AND WAS BARE BY ABOUT FORTY TO ELEVEN AND BACK AT ABOUT SEVENTEEN MINUTES PAST ELEVEN, BEING TWENTY-NINE OFF AND SIXTEEN BARE AND TWENTY-NINE BACK, WHICH IS SEVENTY-FOUR THE WHOLE OF IT. THREE SUNDAYS IN A ROW STAND ON ONE LINE WITH THE DAY AGAINST EACH FOR THE FIRST TIME IN NINE RUNS OF DAYS. THE COUNT OF THE REASONS IS ZERO AND THE COUNT OF THE QUESTIONS NOBODY HAS ASKED IS ZERO. THE SHEET STANDS AT ONE HUNDRED AND THIRTEEN THOUSAND TWO HUNDRED AND TEN PENCE OUT AND ONE HUNDRED AND TWO THOUSAND FIVE HUNDRED AND SEVENTY PENCE SHORT. THE BAG HOLDS TWENTY-EIGHT PENCE, BEING TWO SHILLINGS AND FOURPENCE.**

---

---

*First column, entered on Sunday the twenty-sixth day of June at about six in the evening in that shed: that the said day was a wipe and was not a rota day and was not a wage Tuesday, being the hundred and twelfth time, with two men beginning at about ten past ten and the wall bare by about forty to eleven and back on its holders at about seventeen minutes past eleven, at the angle it has carried since the twelfth of January, and the thing above the frame still not whole; and that the figures are twenty-nine minutes off the wall and sixteen standing bare and twenty-nine to get it back, which is seventy-four minutes the whole of it, against twenty-seven and fourteen and twenty-seven on the Sunday before and twenty-six and thirteen and twenty-six on the Sunday before that; and that all three Sundays stand on one line with the day against each, which is the first time that has been done; and that nobody has said why it is getting slower and this yard has stopped asking the two men who do it.*

*Second column, not checkable from a mole, entered on the said Sunday at about six in the evening in that shed, and no name is against it: that at about forty to eleven the reason four people watched from the doorway and did not go in is that a heavy frame handled in silence is work two men own, and a doorway crowded with watchers is a weight on the work; that at about noon the reason three Sundays stand on one line is that a slowing shown one figure at a time is a grumble, and a slowing shown three together is a ladder anybody can climb; that at about three the reason a man on a rail is entered with an hour and nothing else is that steps climbed slowly are a fact and what they cost is not this yard's to price; and that at about four the reason a book of figures without reasons is praised out loud is that reasons without figures are stories, and this harbor has stories enough and needs ladders.*
