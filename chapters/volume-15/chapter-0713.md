# Chapter 713

*Sunday the Third Day of July, a Wipe and the Hundred and Thirteenth Time, Thirty Minutes Off the Wall and Seventeen Standing Bare and Thirty to Get It Back, Being Seventy-Seven the Whole of It, a Page Down the Road From the Lowfen Reed With a Time on It and Beside the Time a Second Mark That Is Not a Hand, the Bag at Twenty-Eight Pence, Being Two Shillings and Fourpence, the First Column at Four Hundred and Forty-Six and the Second at Two Thousand One Hundred and Four*

Sunday the third day of July, with that market shut all day and the office on the west basin shut with it, a yard below that market with a shed standing in it, and about nine people in that shed at half past five that afternoon.

**A wipe and not a rota day and not a wage Tuesday, and that wall behind a reeve's bench taken down at about ten past ten and bare by about forty to eleven and back on its holders at about twenty-seven minutes past eleven, at the angle it has carried since the twelfth of January, and the thing above the frame still not whole. Thirty minutes off the wall, seventeen standing bare, thirty to get it back, which is seventy-seven minutes the whole of it. No charge put on that cistern and its covers lying on it from nine in the morning until four in the afternoon. A hundred and sixty-eight pence came into that yard at about ten in the morning and a hundred and sixty-eight went out of it at about four in the afternoon, and under that slate at the close of the day is twenty-eight pence, being two shillings and fourpence, and no tin in that shed has been opened since the twenty-ninth of April past.**

**Nine counters about things that are not being done ran a day on this morning. That notice of the seventeenth of May past stands on its four hundred and eleventh day. Four hundred and thirteen days have gone by without anybody asking a bellwright of this harbor, and his chest stays shut. Two objects of nobody's on that crown are into their seven hundred and fifty-first day. That bench under that pump has stood bare for the five hundred and seventeenth night. Two lights about a mile off that point have spent three hundred and thirty-fifth days with no figure set against them. A six-line sheet at the back of that end stall has reached its two hundred and ninetieth day. The paper of four lines in Fish Street is on its three hundred and fifth. A nine-line sheet with a seal on it in that front street has stood three hundred and twenty-second day. A man of about fifty-one has kept three hundred and fifty-three days of not saying anything to anybody about a page of six names. That third column is empty and unappointed and a boy's hand of twenty-two is at the head of it and his surname is on no page and he is off the water. That fourth column stands at one hundred and twenty-five and has taken no line and is neither shut nor struck. About two sea miles off that mole head lie nine hulls this afternoon and nine lay there on the twenty-first of January, and this harbor has not said a word about any of them easing. A man of about twenty-nine hears out of the left side of his head and it is two hundred and eleven days, one more than the day before, no figure is set against it, and he is not on the water.**

---

Two men began at about ten past ten and the wall was bare by about forty to eleven and back on its holders at about twenty-seven minutes past eleven. They work without speaking and they lay each piece down in order on the floor cloth, and about four people watched from the doorway without going in. The frame is heavy and the plaster behind it is bare, and the thing above the frame where the wall meets the beam is still not whole.

Thirty minutes off the wall, seventeen standing bare, thirty to get it back, which is seventy-seven the whole of it, against twenty-nine and sixteen and twenty-nine on the Sunday before. It is a minute longer each way and a minute longer bare than the Sunday before, and nobody has said why, and the yard has stopped asking the two men who do it. The man who keeps the leaf put this Sunday beside the last one on one line with the day against each, which he began last week.

---

The page came down the coast road at about two in the afternoon in the hand of a woman of about fifty-eight, and it was damp at the fold and it had been carried inside somebody's coat. It came from the Lowfen Reed and it is that community's own page, and on it there is a time written in the plain hand of a man who fishes that water, being the hour at which that route could be in contact.

Beside the time on that page there is a second mark. It is not a hand. It is a short stroke laid on with a flat brush and dried dark, and it sits in the middle of the page where a hand would go, and nobody who came down that road can say who put it there.

The keeper asked the woman of about fifty-eight what the second mark is, and she said that it is how her community says a thing is settled, and that the man whose hand the time is in did not put it there. She did not say who did and she was not asked twice, and about four people in that nine stood and looked at the two marks side by side and nobody spoke.

---

He wrote the page down in the yard's own book with the day and the hour against it and he wrote beside the time that it is that community's time and given in that community's hand, and he wrote beside the second mark that it is not a hand and that this yard does not know whose it is and is not going to find out. He did not write the mark under the time. He did not write it beside the time either, and the second of those two is the one that took a minute of his time.

The man of about thirty-two was not in the shed when the page came and came at about four. He read the book with the two marks in it and he said one thing, which was that a mark is not a time and a time is not a consent, and then he went up the steps by the rail and did not say anything else about it. About four people heard him and about four did not.

The woman of thirty of no office copied the page into her own book in her own hand, and she copied the second mark as it stands, being a short stroke with a flat brush, and she wrote under it that the copy is of the mark and says nothing about what the mark is.

---

At about half past five on that Sunday the man who keeps the leaf stood at the flat of that bench stone and took the shortfall out loud, to about nine people, four of whom cannot read a column, and about four of those nine had seen the wall go back at about twenty-seven minutes past eleven.

"**Thirty minutes off that wall this morning, seventeen standing bare, thirty to get it back, which is seventy-seven the whole of it, and it is a minute longer each way than last Sunday. Nobody has said why it is getting slower and I have stopped asking the two men who do it.**

"**A page came down that road at about two in the hand of a woman of about fifty-eight, and it is the Lowfen Reed's own page, and it carries a time in the plain hand of a man who fishes that water. Beside the time there is a second mark which is not a hand, being a short stroke laid on with a flat brush, and it sits where a hand would go, and the woman said the man whose hand the time is in did not put it there and she was not asked twice.**

"**I have written both marks down with the day and the hour against them, and I have written that the second is not a hand and that this yard does not know whose it is and is not going to find out. I did not write it under the time. The man of about thirty-two read the book at about four and said that a mark is not a time and a time is not a consent, and then he went up the steps.**

"**The sheet stands at one hundred and fourteen thousand six hundred and sixty-six pence out and one hundred and four thousand and twenty-six pence short. Four hundred and fifty pence is still on that board in the front street. A hundred and sixty-eight pence in at about ten and a hundred and sixty-eight out at about four, and twenty-eight plus one hundred and sixty-eight less one hundred and sixty-eight is twenty-eight, and under that slate is twenty-eight pence, being two shillings and fourpence, and that is six hundred and twelve pence short of the wage of four marks, being three marks eleven shillings. No tin in that shed has been opened since the twenty-ninth of April past and this yard is now one hundred and twenty-six days without one.**

"**And the last of it. That cistern was not charged and its covers lay on it from nine until four and nobody walked on the water. The far bench was occupied from ten until four with nothing put on it. About two sea miles off that mole head there are nine hulls this afternoon and there were nine there on the twenty-first of January. A man of about twenty-nine hears out of the left side of his head and it is two hundred and eleven days today and nothing about a brush mark touched it by a day.**"

Nobody was thanked for the arithmetic and nobody was thanked for carrying a page inside a coat, and that shed stood empty by about six with its door standing open on a slip nobody was on, and that page of that week had fifteen lines on it, and that wooden tally slate was still lying flat on that bench stone with nothing on it.

---

**THAT WALL CAME DOWN AT ABOUT TEN PAST TEN AND WAS BARE BY ABOUT FORTY TO ELEVEN AND BACK ON ITS HOLDERS AT ABOUT TWENTY-SEVEN MINUTES PAST ELEVEN, BEING THIRTY OFF AND SEVENTEEN BARE AND THIRTY BACK, WHICH IS SEVENTY-SEVEN THE WHOLE OF IT. A PAGE DOWN THE COAST ROAD FROM THE LOWFEN REED CARRIES A TIME IN ONE HAND AND A FLAT STROKE BESIDE IT THAT IS NOT A HAND, AND BOTH MARKS ARE WRITTEN DOWN WITH THE HOUR AGAINST THEM AND THE SECOND IS NOT PUT UNDER THE FIRST. THE SHEET STANDS AT ONE HUNDRED AND FOURTEEN THOUSAND SIX HUNDRED AND SIXTY-SIX PENCE OUT AND ONE HUNDRED AND FOUR THOUSAND AND TWENTY-SIX PENCE SHORT. THE BAG HOLDS TWENTY-EIGHT PENCE, BEING TWO SHILLINGS AND FOURPENCE.**

---

---

*First column, entered on Sunday the third day of July at about six in the evening in that shed: that the said day was a wipe and was not a rota day and was not a wage Tuesday, being the hundred and thirteenth time, with two men beginning at about ten past ten and the wall bare by about forty to eleven and back on its holders at about twenty-seven minutes past eleven, at the angle it has carried since the twelfth of January, and the thing above the frame still not whole; and that the figures are thirty minutes off the wall and seventeen standing bare and thirty to get it back, which is seventy-seven minutes the whole of it, against twenty-nine and sixteen and twenty-nine on the Sunday before, and that nobody has said why it is getting slower and this yard has stopped asking; and that a page came down that coast road at about two in the afternoon in the hand of a woman of about fifty-eight, being that community's own page and carrying a time in the plain hand of a man who fishes that water; and that beside the time there is a second mark which is not a hand, being a short stroke laid on with a flat brush and dried dark, sitting where a hand would go, and that the woman said the man whose hand the time is in did not put it there, and she was not asked twice; and that both marks were written into the yard's book with the hour against each and that the second was not written under the first.*

*Second column, not checkable from a mole, entered on the said Sunday at about six in the evening in that shed, and no name is against it: that at about two in the afternoon the reason the time was entered as that community's own and given in its own hand is that a time on a page is the only thing nine communities have been asked for and taking it in any other hand would take the asking away; that at about half past three the reason the flat stroke is written down as a mark and not as a refusal is that a mark can show that two states were different and cannot show which of them was true, and this yard does not know enough about that page to call it anything; that at about four the reason the stroke is not written under the time is that a yard that prints one mark beneath another has made its own order out of two things and that order will be read back as fact; and that at about half past four the reason one page of that week carries one line is that a road closed on a Sunday can only carry what a person could hold on a walk.*
