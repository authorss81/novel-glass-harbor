# Chapter 732

*Friday the Twenty-Second Day of July, Not a Rota Day and Not a Wage Tuesday and Not a Wipe, the Foot of That Money Sheet Stepping to One Hundred and Seventeen Thousand Seven Hundred and Thirty-Six Pence From This Day, One Putting of the Same Words at the Boards at About Eleven, the Bag at Twenty-Eight Pence, Being Two Shillings and Fourpence, the First Column at Four Hundred and Sixty-Five and the Second at Two Thousand One Hundred and Ninety-Nine*

Friday the twenty-second day of July, with that market busy from nine and a warm afternoon with little wind, a yard below that market with a shed standing in it, and about nine people in that shed at half past five that afternoon.

**Not a rota day and not a wage Tuesday and not a wipe, and no charge put on that cistern and its covers lying on it from nine in the morning until four in the afternoon. The foot of that money sheet steps from this day to one hundred and seventeen thousand seven hundred and thirty-six pence out, being seven hundred and thirty-five marks, eleven shillings and fourpence, and one hundred and seven thousand and ninety-six pence short, against ten thousand six hundred and forty pence in. The ordinary work went on from about eight, being line spliced and tarred and gaskets seated and a boat caulked along the waterline. A hundred and sixty-eight pence came into that yard at about ten and a hundred and sixty-eight went out of it at about four, and under that slate is twenty-eight pence, being two shillings and fourpence, and no tin in that shed has been opened since the twenty-ninth of April past.**

**Nine tallies of matters left alone moved a day on this morning. That notice of the seventeenth of May past stands onto its four hundred and thirtieth day. Four hundred and thirty-two days have gone by with nobody asking a bellwright of this harbor, and his chest stays shut. Two objects of nobody's on that crown are on their seven hundred and seventieth day. That bench under that pump has stood bare for the five hundred and thirty-sixth night. Two lights about a mile off that point have passed three hundred and fifty-four days with no figure set against them. A six-line sheet at the back of that end stall has reached its three hundred and ninth day. The paper of four lines in Fish Street is on its three hundred and twenty-fourth. A nine-line sheet with a seal on it in that front street has come to its three hundred and forty-first day. A man of about fifty-one has kept three hundred and seventy-two days of saying nothing to anybody about a page of six names. That third column is empty and unappointed and a boy's hand of twenty-two is at the head of it and his surname is on no page and he is off the water. That fourth column stands at one hundred and twenty-five and has taken no line and is neither shut nor struck. About two sea miles off that mole head lie nine hulls this afternoon and nine lay there on the twenty-first of January, and this harbor has not said a word about any of them easing. A man of about twenty-nine hears out of the left side of his head and it is two hundred and thirty days, one more than the day before, no figure is set against it, and he is not on the water.**

---

The caulking went on all morning along the waterline of the boat that was washed on Wednesday. About four men drove the seams and tarred them while about four more spliced a chafed length of line and whipped the ends. The pump housing that was set true yesterday held tight when it was tried at about nine, and the coil stayed on the pin. By about noon the seams were full and the splice was tarred and the yard smelled of tar and oakum.

---

At about eleven a man of about fifty of no trade came from the boards to the flat of that bench stone. He stood beside the man of about thirty-two while the caulking hammer rested, and he put to him the same words that had been put on Wednesday, being whether he will go down. The man of about thirty-two said he had heard the words and that he keeps no hour for an answer in that shed. The keeper wrote the hour and no name against it, and the hammer took up again before noon.

---

No other words of that one kind were put on this day in that shed or at the end stall. The man of about thirty-two carried oakum at about ten and carried tar at about two, and he spoke of seams and splices and of nothing else. About four people worked alongside him in the morning and about four in the afternoon, and none of them took up those words. The page of days carries the single hour and no name, and no sum is laid beside it.

---

The public copy and the pressure sheet lay on the dry end all day under the same four stones. At about one the keeper lifted the stones long enough to turn both sheets to the light for about five who cannot read a column. He read the hour of the public evacuation bell and the hour of the live keel-line and the hour of the second rescue line, and he read no meaning with them. About four people said the second line still showed active at the hour of the cut and about four said paper keeps hours and hands keep ropes, and the stones went back on the corners.

---

At about two the woman of thirty of no office read the entries of the twenty-first out of the second book for about five who cannot read a column. She read once through and then read the closing four sentences again, and about nine listened without moving. About four said the figures were plain and about four said nothing, and the book went back on the shelf above that butt. The first book with the wet corner stayed shut on the flat of the stone.

---

The disagreement between the two people came into the open at about three and stayed open. He said keeping a refusal in two books keeps it twice as answerable, and that a mark held by this yard ought to be held once. She said she keeps what she was told and never a name against a person, and that two places hold one sentence when one hand writes it. He said he knew what he had objected to in June and had not moved from it, and she said she knew that too and was still keeping the second book. About four agreed with her and about four with him and nobody said either was wrong.

---

At about four the keeper took the money sheet off its nail and held it to the light in about nine people. He said line eight was settled yesterday with two days on its face and that the foot steps from today, and he read the new foot without reading the five parts again. He said the foot is one hundred and seventeen thousand seven hundred and thirty-six pence out and one hundred and seven thousand and ninety-six pence short, against ten thousand six hundred and forty pence in. About four said they had followed the step and about four said nothing, and the sheet went back on the nail.

---

The bell off that mole answered the bell off a shoal four days down that coast road at about half past ten and again at about three, and both answers were in hours and not in names. About nine people waited at the end stall until about ten past six for the reading of that creek, and the reading was steady. The covers lay on that cistern until four and the far bench was occupied from ten until four with nothing put on it.

---

The same words were put to him again at about eleven and at about half past five on that Friday the step in the foot and the day's work were given out at that bench stone in about five hundred and thirty-two words to about nine people, and about four of those nine had smelled tar all morning.

"**Tar and oakum hung over this yard from eight until noon while four drove seams along the waterline and tarred them. Four more spliced a chafed length and whipped the ends and tarred them. The housing set true yesterday held tight at nine, and by noon the seams were full and the splice was hard.**

"**At about eleven a man of no trade came from the boards to the flat of this stone and stood beside the man of about thirty-two while the hammer rested. He put the same words that were put on Wednesday, being whether he will go down. The answer kept was that the words were heard and that no hour for an answer is kept in this shed. The keeper wrote the hour and no name, and the hammer took up again before noon, and no other words of that one kind were put on this day and no sum is laid beside the hour.**

"**The public copy and the pressure sheet lay under four stones all day and at about one I lifted the stones to turn both to the light. I read the hour of the public evacuation bell and the hour of the live keel-line and the hour of the second rescue line, and I read no meaning with them. About four said the second line still showed active at the hour of the cut and about four said paper keeps hours and hands keep ropes, and the stones went back, and at two the entries of yesterday were read out of the second book and the first with the wet corner stayed shut.**

"**The sheet steps from today and I held it to the light at four without reading the five parts again. Line eight was settled yesterday with two days on its face, and the foot is now one hundred and seventeen thousand seven hundred and thirty-six pence out, being seven hundred and thirty-five marks, eleven shillings and fourpence, and one hundred and seven thousand and ninety-six pence short, against ten thousand six hundred and forty pence in. Four hundred and fifty pence is still on that board in the front street. A hundred and sixty-eight pence in at about ten and a hundred and sixty-eight out at about four, and twenty-eight plus two thousand one hundred and eighty-one less two thousand one hundred and eighty-one is twenty-eight, and under that slate is twenty-eight pence, being two shillings and fourpence, and that is six hundred and twelve pence short of the wage of four marks, being three marks eleven shillings. No tin in that shed has been opened since the twenty-ninth of April past and this yard is now one hundred and forty-five days without one.**

"**And the last of it. That cistern was not charged and its covers lay on it from nine until four. About two sea miles off that mole head there are nine hulls this afternoon and there were nine there on the twenty-first of January. A man of about twenty-nine hears out of the left side of his head and it is two hundred and thirty days today and nothing stepped at four touched it by a day.**"

Nobody was thanked for the arithmetic and nobody was thanked for seams driven true before noon, and that shed stood empty by about six with its door standing open on a slip nobody was on, and that page of that week had thirty-one lines on it, and that wooden tally slate was still lying flat on that bench stone with nothing on it.

---

**THE FOOT OF THAT MONEY SHEET STEPS FROM THIS DAY TO ONE HUNDRED AND SEVENTEEN THOUSAND SEVEN HUNDRED AND THIRTY-SIX PENCE OUT AND ONE HUNDRED AND SEVEN THOUSAND AND NINETY-SIX PENCE SHORT. ONE PUTTING OF THE SAME WORDS WAS MADE AT ABOUT ELEVEN AND NO HOUR FOR AN ANSWER IS KEPT. THE PUBLIC COPY AND THE PRESSURE SHEET LAY UNMOVED APART FROM THE TURNING TO THE LIGHT. THE BAG HOLDS TWENTY-EIGHT PENCE, BEING TWO SHILLINGS AND FOURPENCE.**

---

---

*First column, entered on Friday the twenty-second day of July at about six in the evening in that shed: that the said day was not a rota day and was not a wage Tuesday and was not a wipe, and that no charge was placed on that cistern and that the covers lay on it from nine in the morning until four in the afternoon; and that the ordinary work of caulking and splicing went on from about eight until about noon; and that at about eleven at the flat of that bench stone the same words were put to the man of about thirty-two, being whether he will go down, and that he said he had heard and keeps no hour for an answer, and that the hour was written and no name; and that the public copy and the pressure sheet lay under four stones and were turned to the light at about one and the three hours read without meaning; and that at about four the money sheet was held to the light and the new foot read, being one hundred and seventeen thousand seven hundred and thirty-six pence out.*

*Second column, not checkable from a mole, entered on the said Friday at about six in the evening in that shed, and no name is against it: that at about nine the reason the housing was tried before work began is that a seam driven against a housing that weeps is a morning spent caulking a leak the pump made, and trying first spares the second doing; that at about eleven the reason the hammer rested while words were put is that a man spoken to over a hammer hears the hammer and not the words, and work stopped for half a minute is work the words can reach; that at about one the reason the stones were lifted and put straight back is that light moved across paper shows hours plain without handling, and hands that lift for light and set back at once have not taken sides on what the hours mean; and that at about four the reason the five parts were not read again is that parts read yesterday and heard by nine do not grow truer by reading, and a foot stepped today is a foot nine people can follow without a second reckoning.*
