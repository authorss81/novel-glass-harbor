# Chapter 758

*Wednesday the Seventeenth Day of August, a Man of About Sixty-One With His Hands Black to the Second Joint at the Nails Coming Down to That Shed at About Ten and Putting the Original Repair Log on the Dry End of That Stone and Saying Four Sentences in About Nine People and Not a Fifth, the Second and the Third of Those Four Being the Ones That Cost Him, and the Log Not Read Out and No Word of It Entered in a Column and the Keeper Writing the Hour and the Four Sentences and Writing No Name of Any Body and No Figure of Money, the Far Bench Empty All Day and Nothing Put on It, the Man of About Thirty-Two Not Going Over to It, No Line on That Sheet, the Bag at Twenty-Eight Pence, the First Column at Four Hundred and Ninety-One and the Second at Two Thousand Three Hundred and Twenty-Nine*

Wednesday the seventeenth day of August, with that market open from about nine and a dirty morning with the rain coming in off the water in short pushes and stopping, a yard below that market with a shed standing in it, and about nine people in that shed at half past five that afternoon.

**Not a rota day and not a wage Tuesday and not a wipe, and no charge put on that cistern and its covers lying on it from nine in the morning until four in the afternoon. The far bench was empty all day and nothing was put on it, and the man of about thirty-two did not go over to it at any hour of that day and was seen standing at the far end of that yard at about two looking at the rain and about four of that nine noticed and about four did not. A man of about sixty-one came down to that shed at about ten and put the original repair log on the dry end of that stone and said four sentences in about nine people and did not say a fifth. No line went onto that money sheet and the keeper said out loud at about four that the day carries no line and is not one of any form. The sheet stands at one hundred and twenty-two thousand five hundred and forty-eight pence out and one hundred and eleven thousand nine hundred and eight pence short, against ten thousand six hundred and forty pence in. A hundred and sixty-eight pence came into that yard at about ten and a hundred and sixty-eight went out of it at about four, and under that slate is twenty-eight pence, being two shillings and fourpence, and no tin in that shed has been opened since the twenty-ninth of April past.**

**Nine standing things went on in that yard this morning whatever else was happening in it. That notice of the seventeenth of May past is on its four hundred and fifty-sixth day. Four hundred and fifty-eight days have gone by without anybody asking that bellwright a question and his chest is shut and stays shut. Those two objects belonging to nobody on that crown are into the seven hundred and ninety-sixth day of themselves. That bench under that pump has now stood bare for the five hundred and sixty-second night running. Two lights about a mile off that point have gone three hundred and eightieth days with nothing set against them. The six-line sheet at the back of that end stall has reached the three hundred and thirty-fifth day of its standing. The paper of four lines in Fish Street is on the three hundred and fiftieth day. The nine-line sheet with a seal on it in that front street has stood three hundred and sixty-seventh day. And a man of about fifty-one has kept three hundred and ninety-eighth days of saying nothing to anybody about a page of six names. That third column is empty and unappointed with a boy's hand of twenty-two at the head of it, his surname is on no page, and he is off the water. That fourth column stands at one hundred and twenty-five and has taken no line and is neither shut nor struck. About two sea miles off that mole head there are nine hulls this afternoon and nine lay there on the twenty-first of January, and this harbor has not said a word about any of them easing. A man of about twenty-nine hears out of the left side of his head and it is two hundred and fifty-six days today, one more than the day before, no figure is set against it, and he is not on the water.**

---

That man came down that lane at about ten with the log under his arm in a piece of sailcloth and he did not come to the far bench and he came to that shed instead, and nobody sent for him and nobody said that anybody had sent for him.

He put it on the dry end of that stone at about ten and took his hands off it, and his hands were black to the second joint at the nails and the black had been in the creases of them for years and not since that morning.

---

He said four sentences, and he said them in that order, and he did not say a fifth at any hour of that day or of any day after it.

The first was that he signed that log after the cut and not before it, and that the hand on the entries after the cut is his and the hand on the entries before it is his too.

The second was that he knew the second of those things when he signed and knew that the water was already moving under that ground and signed anyway because the yard had a berth to keep and his part of it was the signing.

The third was that he has never once in any of the years since said so out loud to anybody at that table, including the two of them, and that this is the first time he has said it and he is not going to be asked anything else about it.

The fourth was that he is not asking for anything and is not giving anything and that whoever wants that book can take it and read it or not read it as he likes.

---

Nobody in that shed said the second and the third of those four back to him and nobody wrote them on a board and nobody read that log out in that shed or any other.

About four of that nine said nothing at all while he said them and about four of that nine looked at the log on the stone and not at him, and about four of that nine went out of that shed and stood in the yard while the rest of it was said, and no one of that nine said he had been thanked and no one of that nine said he had been forgiven.

---

At about half past ten the man of about thirty-two said out loud in about nine people that he had known about that log since he was seventeen and had asked for it four times in his life and been refused four times, and that the asking had been the wrong thing to do four times and that he was not going to say so in a way that lets anybody off.

The woman of thirty of no office said out loud that a man who has been refused a thing four times and has asked for it four times has been doing one thing for four years and not four, and that she had not known that until this morning either.

---

At about one the keeper wrote the hour and the four sentences on the face of that page of that week with the day against them, and he wrote no name of a body against any hour of it and no figure of money against any hour of it, and he wrote that the log was not read out and was not entered in a column and was not put on the boards at the back of that market.

He said out loud that the log stayed on the stone at the head of that bench and that it was still there at half past five and that nobody had taken it and that it was not this yard's and had not been asked to be this yard's.

---

At about two a woman of about fifty-eight who is the man's wife came down that lane and stood at the door of that shed and did not come in, and she was in that yard for about four minutes and then she went back up the lane.

Nobody stopped what they were doing for her and nobody said anything to her, and after she had gone the man of about sixty-one said one more thing that was not one of the four, being that his wife had not spoken to him since a Tuesday in November and had not spoken to him this morning either and that this was not on the four and he was not going to make it a fifth.

---

At about half past three the man of about thirty-two said out loud in about nine people that he was not going to forgive his father and that nobody in that shed was to say that he had or had not, and that a man who has been silent for years has not bought anything with the silence and has not lost anything by stopping.

The keeper wrote that at about half past three and wrote it with the hour against it and wrote no reason against it, and about four of that nine said nothing and nobody said he was right and nobody said he was wrong.

---

The ordinary work of that Wednesday went on and was not stopped by any of it, being the pump run clean from about nine and two boats hauled up on the north slip in the rain and the second lay taking a plank off a stem in the wet.

The sheet with the fourteen gaps at the end stall stood all day with no figure moved by one, and that wooden tally slate was still lying flat on that bench stone with nothing on it all day and nothing was put on that bench at any hour of that day.

---

At about half past five on that Wednesday the whole of that day was put out loud at that stone, in about one thousand two hundred and thirty-two words, to about nine people, and about four of those nine had stood at that flat stone at about ten and heard four sentences and had not moved while they were said.

The woman of thirty of no office said out loud that a man who has been refused a thing four times and has asked for it four times has been doing one thing for four years and not four, and that she had not known that until this morning either.**"

"**And the rest of it. The pump ran clean from about nine and two boats were hauled up on that north slip in the rain and the second lay was taking a plank off a stem in the wet, and nobody in this shed stopped work because of what was said at about ten. The sheet stands at one hundred and twenty-two thousand five hundred and forty-eight pence out and one hundred and eleven thousand nine hundred and eight pence short, against ten thousand six hundred and forty pence in. No tin in this shed has been opened since the twenty-ninth of April past and this yard is now one hundred and seventy-one days without one. Twenty-eight plus two thousand one hundred and eighty-one plus one thousand eight hundred and forty-eight less four thousand and twenty-nine is twenty-eight, and under that slate is twenty-eight pence, being two shillings and fourpence, and that is six hundred and twelve pence short of the wage of four marks, being three marks eleven shillings, with a hundred and sixty-eight pence in at about ten and a hundred and sixty-eight out at about four. About two sea miles off that mole head there are nine hulls this evening and there were nine there on the twenty-first of January. A man of about twenty-nine hears out of the left side of his head and it is two hundred and fifty-six days today and nothing said at about ten today touched that figure by a day.**"

Nobody was thanked for four sentences and nobody was thanked for a book that was not read out, and that shed stood empty by about six with its door standing open on a slip nobody was on, and that page of that week had twenty-nine lines on it, and that wooden tally slate was still lying flat on that bench stone with nothing on it.

**A MAN OF ABOUT SIXTY-ONE CAME DOWN TO THAT SHED AT ABOUT TEN AND PUT THE ORIGINAL REPAIR LOG ON THE DRY END OF THAT STONE AND SAID FOUR SENTENCES IN ABOUT NINE PEOPLE AND DID NOT SAY A FIFTH. THE SECOND AND THE THIRD OF THOSE FOUR ARE THE ONES THAT COST HIM. NOBODY WAS THANKED AND NOBODY WAS FORGIVEN AND THE MAN OF ABOUT THIRTY-TWO SAID OUT LOUD AT ABOUT HALF PAST THREE THAT HE WAS NOT GOING TO FORGIVE HIS FATHER. THE FAR BENCH WAS EMPTY ALL DAY AND NOTHING WAS PUT ON IT. NO LINE WENT ONTO THAT MONEY SHEET. THE BAG HOLDS TWENTY-EIGHT PENCE, BEING TWO SHILLINGS AND FOURPENCE.**

*Second column, not checkable from a mole, entered on the said Wednesday at about six in the evening in that shed, and no name is against it: that at about ten the reason he came to that shed and not to the far bench is that a bench a man has sat on for years is a place where anything said is said to the person who left it empty, and a shed with nine people in it is a place where what is said is said to whoever is standing there; that at about one the reason those four sentences were written with the hour and the day and not read out is that a thing said out loud in nine people becomes a story about the man who said it within a week, and four sentences written down in a yard's own hand stay four sentences for as long as the hand lasts; that at about two the reason his wife was let to stand at the door and go again is that a family that has used not speaking as a way of caring has not learned any other way yet, and the first hour of learning it is the hour in which everybody still goes back; and that at about half past three the reason the man of about thirty-two said he was not going to forgive him and said nobody was to say whether he had or had not is that forgiveness given in a yard of nine people is a thing nine people now hold, and a thing nine people hold is not a thing between two men.*
