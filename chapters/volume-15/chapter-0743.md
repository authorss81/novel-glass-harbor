# Chapter 743

*Tuesday the Second Day of August, the Ninety-Second Wage Tuesday With the Column Unmoved at Twenty-Six Lines and One Hundred and Four Marks, Line Ten on That Sheet at About Two With the Second of August on Its Face for the Work and the Fourth for the Day It Settles and Not Settled Today, the Boat Away at About Four, the Bag at Twenty-Eight Pence, the First Column at Four Hundred and Seventy-Six and the Second at Two Thousand Two Hundred and Fifty-Four*

Tuesday the second day of August, with that market open from about nine and a hot morning that lay flat on the water without a breath on it, a yard below that market with a shed standing in it, and about nine people in that shed at half past five that afternoon.

**The ninety-second wage Tuesday and not a rota day and not a wipe, and the column on the lid of that step read out at about ten in the morning in four minutes by a man of about thirty-one of the second lay and standing at twenty-six lines and one hundred and four marks and not moved on that day, and the chest with the twenty envelopes in it shut from ten until four, and a further week standing fallen due at the close of it. Line ten went onto that money sheet at about two in the afternoon carrying two days on its face, being Tuesday the second day of August for the work and Thursday the fourth for the day it settles, and it is not settled today and the foot of that sheet stands where it stood yesterday. The sheet stands at one hundred and eighteen thousand seven hundred and eighty-four pence out and one hundred and eight thousand one hundred and forty-four pence short, against ten thousand six hundred and forty pence in, being sixty-six marks, six shillings and eightpence. A hundred and sixty-eight pence came into that yard at about ten and a hundred and sixty-eight went out of it at about four, and under that slate is twenty-eight pence, being two shillings and fourpence, and no tin in that shed has been opened since the twenty-ninth of April past.**

**Nine days of things that are not being done went on a day in the morning, and this is what they stood at. That notice of the seventeenth of May past is on its four hundred and forty-first day. Four hundred and forty-three days have gone by without anybody asking a bellwright of this harbor a question, and his chest is shut and stays shut. Two objects belonging to nobody on that crown are into the seven hundred and eighty-first day of themselves. That bench under that pump has now stood bare for the five hundred and forty-seventh night running. Two lights about a mile off that point have gone three hundred and sixty-fifth days with nothing set against them. A six-line sheet at the back of that end stall has reached the three hundred and twentieth day of its standing. The paper of four lines in Fish Street is on the three hundred and thirty-fifth. A nine-line sheet with a seal on it in that front street has stood three hundred and fifty-second day. And a man of about fifty-one has now kept three hundred and eighty-third days of saying nothing to anybody about a page of six names. That third column is empty and unappointed with a boy's hand of twenty-two at the head of it, his surname is on no page, and he is off the water. That fourth column stands at one hundred and twenty-five and has taken no line and is neither shut nor struck. About two sea miles off that mole head lie nine hulls this afternoon and nine lay there on the twenty-first of January, and this harbor has not said a word about any of them easing. A man of about twenty-nine hears out of the left side of his head and it is two hundred and forty-one days, one more than the day before, no figure is set against it, and he is not on the water.**

---

The column was read at about ten in four minutes and the chest stayed shut as it stays shut on every wage Tuesday. The man of about thirty-one of the second lay read the twenty-six lines and the one hundred and four marks and shut the lid again, and a further week stood fallen due at the close of the reading, and nobody asked for the reading a second time.

---

Six bell seats had been cut and faced in the six days of work that began on the thirtieth, and the seals to go with them were cut and faced on Monday, and about four of that party cannot read a column and stood at the flat of that bench stone all the while the figures were read. The six seats are for six boats of six places and the six places are on the face of the line by the name of a place and not by the name of a route.

---

At about two the keeper took the money sheet off its nail and laid it flat on the dry end of that stone in front of about nine people. He said out loud that the work was done on Tuesday the second day of August and that the day it settles is Thursday the fourth, and that both days are on the face of the line, and that the second day is the day it settles and not the day the work was done.

---

What went on the face in parts is six days of a boat hired at the figure in chalk at six times one hundred and sixty-eight for one thousand and eight pence and coal and oil for six days at one hundred and twenty pence and ninety yards of rope at ninety times three for two hundred and seventy pence and six seats and seals at one hundred and eight pence and nine men at six days at sixteen pence a man-day at nine times six times sixteen for eight hundred and sixty-four pence, and one thousand and eight plus one hundred and twenty plus two hundred and seventy plus one hundred and eight plus eight hundred and sixty-four is two thousand three hundred and seventy pence, being fourteen marks, ten shillings and tenpence.

---

The keeper read the five parts with the multiplications and then the sum, and the sum stayed on the face of the line and did not go into the foot, and the foot stands as it stood yesterday at one hundred and eighteen thousand seven hundred and eighty-four pence out and one hundred and eight thousand one hundred and forty-four pence short, and no figure was written into it today.

---

The disagreement between those two about what a page prints was in that shed at about three in about nine people, and it is not settled. He said a line that carries the day it settles on its face is a line a stranger can check, and that the same courtesy is owed to a claim, and she said a line is a line and a claim is a person standing behind it, and that a claim carrying two days on its face is a claim waiting to be told it was late.

---

He said he had not moved from June and she said she knew that and that she was still keeping the second book on the shelf above that butt because one wet road takes one book, and about four agreed with her and about four agreed with him and nobody said either was wrong, and the matter is open and no page in that yard carries a resolution of it.

---

The boat went out of that landing at about four in the afternoon with nine men aboard, and the man of about thirty-two and the woman of about thirty-three who stands in the tender and the man who keeps the leaf were in her, and the pump hand of about forty-four was in her because he asked to be. The tender gear was made fast amidships and the independent line was coiled on the pin and the spare glass lay in its wrap beside it, and the keeper wrote the hour she cleared the bar and wrote that the boat was going to a place whose name his book had not carried until this week.

---

Nobody put the same words to the man of about thirty-two this day about going down, and nobody put them to him at the end stall, and the page of days carries no hour against his name. He read the reason of the day at about one, in his own hand, with the hour against it, and about nine people saw it go down the page and none of them spoke the words again, and the keeper wrote no hour for an asking because no asking was made.

---

What was written at about one was that one person would go down on the second morning at Orison, held by the woman of about thirty-three with a watch, against twenty-five minutes worth of capacity, with a reason on the page and the hour against it, and that one person at a time and nobody twice in a day and nobody at the top of a flood and an ascent asked for not argued with. The man of about thirty-two read it and said it was the same reason he had written in June and that he was not going to write it better than he wrote it then.

---

The register copy of the reason went to a table at the west basin office with the hour against it, and the office listed it and entered it and paid a day at the figure in chalk and bound nobody. The far bench was occupied from ten until four with nothing put on it and the sheet with the fourteen gaps stood at the end stall with no figure moved by one.

---

At about half past five on that Tuesday the shortfall went out loud at that bench stone in about nine hundred words to about nine people, and about four of those nine had stood at the flat of that stone at about two and heard five parts read with their multiplications.

"**The lid on that step was read at about ten this morning in four minutes by a man of about thirty-one of the second lay, and it came to twenty-six lines and one hundred and four marks, and it did not move, and the chest stayed shut from ten until four, and a further week stood fallen due at the close of it. That is the ninety-second of those Tuesdays and the column has not moved on any of them and nobody has asked for the reading twice.**"

"**Six bell seats were cut and faced in the six days of work that began on the thirtieth, and the seals to go with them were cut and faced yesterday, and the six seats are for six boats of six places and those six places are on the face of the line by the name of a place and not by the name of a route. At about two I took the money sheet off its nail and laid it flat on the dry end of this stone in front of about nine, and I said out loud that the work was done on the second of August and that the day it settles is the fourth, and that both days are on the face of the line.**"

"**The five parts are six days of a boat hired at the figure in chalk at six times one hundred and sixty-eight for one thousand and eight pence, coal and oil for six days at one hundred and twenty pence, ninety yards of rope at ninety times three for two hundred and seventy pence, six seats and seals at one hundred and eight pence, and nine men at six days at sixteen pence a man-day at nine times six times sixteen for eight hundred and sixty-four pence. One thousand and eight plus one hundred and twenty plus two hundred and seventy plus one hundred and eight plus eight hundred and sixty-four is two thousand three hundred and seventy pence, being fourteen marks, ten shillings and tenpence. I read the five parts with the multiplications and then the sum, and the sum stayed on the face of the line and did not go into the foot, and the foot stands as it stood yesterday.**"

"**At about three the disagreement was in this shed. He said a line that carries the day it settles on its face is a line a stranger can check, and that the same courtesy is owed to a claim, and she said a line is a line and a claim is a person standing behind it, and that a claim carrying two days on its face is a claim waiting to be told it was late. He said he had not moved from June and she was still keeping the second book on the shelf, and about four agreed with her and about four with him and nobody said either was wrong, and this is not settled and no page here settles it.**"

"**The boat went out at about four this afternoon with nine men in her, and the man of about thirty-two and the woman of about thirty-three who stands in the tender and the man who keeps the leaf were in her, and the pump hand of about forty-four was in her because he asked to be. At about one today the reason was written down in the diver's own hand with the hour against it, being one person on the second morning at Orison, held by the woman of about thirty-three with a watch, against twenty-five minutes worth of capacity, one person at a time and nobody twice in a day and nobody at the top of a flood and an ascent asked for not argued with. He read it and said it was the reason he wrote in June and that he was not going to write it better than he wrote it then. Nobody put words to him about going down today and nobody put them to him at the end stall, and the page of days carries no hour and no name for this Tuesday.**"

"**And the last of it. The sheet stands at one hundred and eighteen thousand seven hundred and eighty-four pence out and one hundred and eight thousand one hundred and forty-four pence short, against ten thousand six hundred and forty pence in, being sixty-six marks, six shillings and eightpence. A hundred and sixty-eight pence in at about ten and a hundred and sixty-eight out at about four, and twenty-eight plus two thousand one hundred and eighty-one plus one thousand eight hundred and forty-eight less four thousand and twenty-nine is twenty-eight, and under that slate is twenty-eight pence, being two shillings and fourpence, and that is six hundred and twelve pence short of the wage of four marks, being three marks eleven shillings. No tin in that shed has been opened since the twenty-ninth of April past and this yard is now one hundred and fifty-six days without one. About two sea miles off that mole head there are nine hulls this afternoon and there were nine there on the twenty-first of January. A man of about twenty-nine hears out of the left side of his head and it is two hundred and forty-one days today and nothing read or written this morning touched it by a day.**"

Nobody was thanked for the arithmetic and nobody was thanked for a reason written in a man's own hand, and that shed stood empty by about six with its door standing open on a slip nobody was on, and that page of that week had thirty-one lines on it, and that wooden tally slate was still lying flat on that bench stone with nothing on it.

---

**LINE TEN WENT ONTO THAT SHEET AT ABOUT TWO CARRYING TWO DAYS ON ITS FACE, BEING THE SECOND FOR THE WORK AND THE FOURTH FOR THE DAY IT SETTLES, BEING TWO THOUSAND THREE HUNDRED AND SEVENTY PENCE, BEING FOURTEEN MARKS, TEN SHILLINGS AND TENPENCE, IN FIVE PARTS, AND IT IS NOT SETTLED TODAY AND THE FOOT OF THAT SHEET HAS NOT MOVED. THE COLUMN ON THE LID OF THAT STEP DID NOT MOVE ON THE NINETY-SECOND OF THOSE TUESDAYS. THE BAG HOLDS TWENTY-EIGHT PENCE, BEING TWO SHILLINGS AND FOURPENCE.**

*First column, entered on Tuesday the second day of August at about six in the evening in that shed: that the said day was the ninety-second wage Tuesday and was not a rota day and was not a wipe, and that the column on the lid of that step was read at about ten in four minutes at twenty-six lines and one hundred and four marks and was not moved, with the chest shut from ten until four and a further week fallen due; and that six bell seats and six seals were cut and faced in the six days of work from the thirtieth, the six seats being for six boats of six places named on the face of the line by the name of a place; and that line ten went onto the money sheet at about two carrying two days on its face, being the second for the work and the fourth for the day it settles, the second day named out loud before any figure was written, being six times one hundred and sixty-eight for one thousand and eight pence and one hundred and twenty pence for coal and oil and ninety times three for two hundred and seventy pence of rope and one hundred and eight pence for six seats and seals and nine times six times sixteen for eight hundred and sixty-four pence, being two thousand three hundred and seventy pence; and that the sum stayed on the face of the line and the foot of that sheet was not written into and stands at one hundred and eighteen thousand seven hundred and eighty-four pence out; and that at about one the reason for one descent at Orison on the morrow was written down in the diver's own hand with the hour against it, being held by the woman of about thirty-three with a watch against twenty-five minutes worth of capacity, one person at a time and nobody twice in a day and nobody at the top of a flood and an ascent not argued with; and that the boat went out at about four with nine men in her; and that nobody put the same words to the man of about thirty-two on that day about going down, and no hour and no name went on the page of days for it.*

*Second column, not checkable from a mole, entered on the said Tuesday at about six in the evening in that shed, and no name is against it: that at about ten the reason the twenty envelopes were not opened is that a chest opened on a wage Tuesday becomes a payment, and this harbor has not paid, and a payment made out of a chest that has been standing shut since the winter would be a payment nobody could check against anything; that at about two the reason the sum stayed on the face of the line and not in the foot is that a foot is a total and a total has no day on it, and a day a stranger cannot find is a day the day cannot be argued with; that at about one the reason the reason was written in the diver's own hand and not in the keeper's is that a reason written by the man who will answer for it can be read back to him, and one written by a keeper is a keeper's account of a man; and that at about four the reason the boat went out on the same tide she was wanted on is that a day asked for by name is paid at the figure in chalk whether anybody is on the water or not, and a boat held back a day is a boat paid for a day it was not in.*
