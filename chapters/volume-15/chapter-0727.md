# Chapter 727

*Sunday the Seventeenth Day of July, a Wipe and the Hundred and Fifteenth Time That Wall Came Down Bare at About Half Past Ten, Thirty-Two Off and Nineteen Bare and Thirty-Two Back Being Eighty-Three in Sum, the Second of the Two Books Dry on the Shelf Above the Butt, the Bag at Twenty-Eight Pence, Being Two Shillings and Fourpence, the First Column at Four Hundred and Sixty and the Second at Two Thousand One Hundred and Seventy-Four*

Sunday the seventeenth day of July, with that market shut fast until nine and a pale heat lying over the boards by noon, a yard below that market with a shed standing in it, and about nine people in that shed at half past five that afternoon.

**A wipe and not a rota day and not a wage Tuesday, and that wall behind a bench in the room over that market coming down bare at about half past ten for the hundred and fifteenth time, being thirty-two off and nineteen bare and thirty-two back and eighty-three in sum of the ladder, and going up again unrepaired and not whole and at the angle it has held since the twelfth of January. No charge was put on that cistern and its covers lay on it from nine in the morning until four in the afternoon, and about four people came for water after four and were served without waiting. The sheet stands at one hundred and sixteen thousand seven hundred and forty-six pence out and one hundred and six thousand one hundred and six pence short, against ten thousand six hundred and forty pence in. A hundred and sixty-eight pence came into that yard at about ten and a hundred and sixty-eight went out of it at about four, and two thousand one hundred and eighty-one pence came into that yard in the running of the thirteen days and two thousand one hundred and eighty-one went out of it, and twenty-eight plus two thousand one hundred and eighty-one less two thousand one hundred and eighty-one is twenty-eight, and under that slate is twenty-eight pence, being two shillings and fourpence, and no tin in that shed has been opened since the twenty-ninth of April past, this yard now one hundred and forty days without one.**

**Nine counters about things that are not being done ran a day on this morning. That notice of the seventeenth of May past stands on its four hundred and twenty-fifth day. Four hundred and twenty-seven days have gone by without anybody asking a bellwright of this harbor, and his chest stays shut. Two objects of nobody's on that crown are into their seven hundred and sixty-fifth day. That bench under that pump has stood bare for the five hundred and thirty-first night. Two lights about a mile off that point have spent three hundred and forty-ninth days with no figure set against them. A six-line sheet at the back of that end stall has reached its three hundred and fourth day. The paper of four lines in Fish Street is on its three hundred and nineteenth. A nine-line sheet with a seal on it in that front street has stood three hundred and thirty-sixth day. A man of about fifty-one has kept three hundred and sixty-seventh days of not saying anything to anybody about a page of six names. That third column is empty and unappointed and a boy's hand of twenty-two is at the head of it and his surname is on no page and he is off the water. That fourth column stands at one hundred and twenty-five and has taken no line and is neither shut nor struck. About two sea miles off that mole head lie nine hulls this afternoon and nine lay there on the twenty-first of January, and this harbor has not said a word about any of them easing. A man of about twenty-nine hears out of the left side of his head and it is two hundred and twenty-five days, one more than the day before, no figure is set against it, and he is not on the water.**

---

The Sunday work began slow and stayed slow through the heat, with the pump housing closed up after the week's opening and the boat's bottom washed down with two buckets and a stiff brush. About four people worked the rail coil from Wednesday and found one chafed length near the pin and cut it out and whipped the ends, and the man of about forty looked at the whipping twice and said it would do for harbor work.

---

At about half past ten the wall came down bare in the room over that market for the hundred and fifteenth time, with about nine people of this party on the stairs and in the doorway and none of them touching a lath. The man of about thirty-one of the second lay called the minutes off his watch in a low voice, and the boy of twenty-two wrote them on the back of a spare leaf with a short pencil. Thirty-two went to the lifting and nineteen stood bare and thirty-two went to the getting back, and eighty-three was the sum of the ladder, and the plaster showed the same dark edge along the lower course as it had shown since the winter.

---

The wall went up again at the angle it has held since the twelfth of January, unrepaired and not whole, and the dust was swept off the bench and out through the doorway with a short broom. About four people said the bare minutes felt longer in the heat and about four said the minutes were the same and only the room was hotter, and the keeper wrote the three figures and the sum and the hour and nothing about what anybody felt. The room was locked at about eleven and the key went back on its nail in that shed, and nobody stayed behind to look at the bare place twice.

---

At about noon the woman of thirty of no office took both books off the shelf above that butt and opened them side by side on the dry end of that bench stone. The first showed the cockled corner where the cart wheel had gone over it on Friday, with the two leaves of the twelfth and the thirteenth still rippled and hard to turn, and the second lay flat and dry beside it with the same figures in the same hand. She turned the four leaves over twice for about nine people to see, and she said the wet corner had dried stiff and would stay stiff, and that the figures under it could still be read against the dry copy.

---

About four people of that nine can read a column and about five cannot, and she read the two days out once more before one so that nobody would have to take the cockled leaves on trust. When the reading was done the second book went back on the shelf above that butt and the first went beside it, and the stone was wiped dry with a rag and left empty for the afternoon.

---

The Lowfen Reed lay dormant through the whole of the day and nobody came down that coast road with a new time and nobody sent one up by a cart. The end stall stood quiet from about one until about three, with about four people sitting in its shade and mending a net with brown twine, and the keeper sat with them for a while and wrote nothing. At about three a man of about fifty of no harbor came down for water and drank from the dipper and went back up the lane, and his going was not written down because he asked for nothing and withdrew from nothing and gave no time for anybody.

---

The disagreement between the two of them was in the shed again without being spoken of at length, the way a tool left on a bench is in a room without being used. He said at about two that a second book kept beside a first would double every quarrel that ever went into it, and she said that what goes into both is what she was told and never a name against a person. He did not answer that beyond saying he knew what he had objected to in June, and she said she knew that too and was keeping the second book all the same. About four people looked at the stone while this passed and about four looked at the door, and the matter stayed open between them and no page of these days carries a resolution of it.

---

At about four the covers were still lying on that cistern and the yard smelled of hot dust and rope and the butt water. About four people came for water after four and were served from the butt with the dipper, and the keeper marked the hours on a scrap and put the scrap under the slate. The boy of twenty-two swept the shed floor and stacked the buckets by the door and did not go near the water beyond carrying.

---

The bell off that mole answered the bell off a shoal four days down that coast road at about half past ten in the morning and again at about three in the afternoon, and both answers were in hours and not in names. About two sea miles off that mole head the nine hulls lay in the haze through the afternoon, and the keeper glassed them once at about four and wrote nine against the hour and nothing beside the figure.

---

At about half past five on that Sunday the shortfall went out loud at that bench stone in about nine hundred words to about nine people, four of whom had been on the stairs at about half past ten in the morning.

"**The Sunday began with the pump closed up and the gaskets left to set and the bottom washed down with two buckets, and the rail coil gave up one chafed length near the pin that was cut out and whipped. At about half past ten that wall came down bare for the hundred and fifteenth time, with thirty-two off and nineteen bare and thirty-two back, being eighty-three in sum of the ladder, and it went up again unrepaired and not whole at the angle it has held since the twelfth of January. The room was locked at about eleven and the key is back on its nail in this shed.**

"**At about noon both books were opened side by side on the dry end of this stone, and the first shows the cockled corner where the wheel went over it on Friday and the second lies flat and dry beside it with the same figures in the same hand. The two days of the twelfth and the thirteenth were read out once more before one so that nobody takes a cockled leaf on trust, and both books are back on the shelf above that butt and this stone was wiped dry.**

"**The Lowfen Reed lay dormant through the whole of the day and nobody came down that coast road with a new time and nobody sent one up by a cart, and the end stall stood quiet from one until three with a net mending in its shade. The disagreement between the two of them was in this shed again at about two without being pressed to an end, and he said a second book doubles every quarrel and she said what goes in both is what she was told and never a name, and the matter stays open and no page of these days settles it.**

"**The sheet stands at one hundred and sixteen thousand seven hundred and forty-six pence out and one hundred and six thousand one hundred and six pence short, against ten thousand six hundred and forty pence in, being sixty-six marks, six shillings and eightpence. Four hundred and fifty pence is still on that board in the front street. Two thousand one hundred and eighty-one pence in and two thousand one hundred and eighty-one out in the running of these thirteen days, and twenty-eight plus two thousand one hundred and eighty-one less two thousand one hundred and eighty-one is twenty-eight, and under that slate is twenty-eight pence, being two shillings and fourpence, and that is six hundred and twelve pence short of the wage of four marks, being three marks eleven shillings. No tin in that shed has been opened since the twenty-ninth of April past and this yard is now one hundred and forty days without one.**

"**And the last of it. That cistern was not charged and its covers lay on it from nine until four, and about four people came for water after four and were served without waiting. About two sea miles off that mole head there are nine hulls this afternoon and there were nine there on the twenty-first of January. A man of about twenty-nine hears out of the left side of his head and it is two hundred and twenty-five days today and nothing read out of two books at noon touched it by a day.**"

Nobody was thanked for the arithmetic and nobody was thanked for a dry copy on a shelf, and that shed stood empty by about six with its door standing open on a lane nobody was on, and that page of that week had twenty-nine lines on it, and that wooden tally slate was still lying flat on that bench stone with nothing on it.

---

**A WIPE AND THE HUNDRED AND FIFTEENTH TIME THAT WALL CAME DOWN BARE AT ABOUT HALF PAST TEN, BEING THIRTY-TWO OFF AND NINETEEN BARE AND THIRTY-TWO BACK AND EIGHTY-THREE IN SUM, AND IT WENT UP AGAIN UNREPAIRED AND NOT WHOLE. BOTH BOOKS WERE OPENED SIDE BY SIDE AT NOON AND THE SECOND IS DRY AND CARRIES THE SAME FIGURES. THE LOWFEN REED LAY DORMANT AND NOBODY CAME DOWN THAT COAST ROAD WITH A NEW TIME. THE SHEET STANDS AT ONE HUNDRED AND SIXTEEN THOUSAND SEVEN HUNDRED AND FORTY-SIX PENCE OUT AND ONE HUNDRED AND SIX THOUSAND ONE HUNDRED AND SIX PENCE SHORT. THE BAG HOLDS TWENTY-EIGHT PENCE, BEING TWO SHILLINGS AND FOURPENCE.**

---

---

*First column, entered on Sunday the seventeenth day of July at about six in the evening in that shed: that the said day was a wipe and was not a rota day and was not a wage Tuesday, and that that wall behind a bench in the room over that market came down bare at about half past ten for the hundred and fifteenth time, being thirty-two off and nineteen bare and thirty-two back and eighty-three in sum, and went up again unrepaired and not whole at the angle it has held since the twelfth of January; and that no charge was placed on that cistern and the covers lay on it from nine until four; and that both books were opened side by side at noon and the first shows a cockled corner and the second is dry and carries the same figures; and that the Lowfen Reed lay dormant through the whole of the day and nobody came down that coast road with a new time; and that the disagreement between the two of them was in that shed at about two and stays open and no page carries a resolution of it.*

*Second column, not checkable from a mole, entered on the said Sunday at about six in the evening in that shed, and no name is against it: that at about half past ten the reason nobody touched a lath while the wall stood bare is that a wipe is a thing looked at and not a thing helped, and hands on the work would have made the minutes a different thing from the hundred and fourteen before it; that at about noon the reason both books were opened in nine people is that a cockled corner left undescribed becomes a story by evening, and paper shown side by side stays paper; that at about two the reason the disagreement was left to lie like a tool on a bench is that pressing it on a Sunday would have made nine people into judges, and nine people are witnesses to what was said and not judges of it; and that at about four the reason the quiet of that creek road was written with no reason against it is that a road not walked on a Sunday after a fortnight's counting is ordinary rest and not a falling away, and writing a reason for rest would teach the page to ask for reasons on every quiet day.*
