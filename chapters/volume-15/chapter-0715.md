# Chapter 715

*Tuesday the Fifth Day of July, the Eighty-Eighth Wage Tuesday, the Lid Read Out at About Ten in Four Minutes and Standing at Twenty-Six Lines and One Hundred and Four Marks and Not Moved, Line Six on That Sheet With the Day the Work Was Done Against It, Being One Thousand Three Hundred and Forty-Eight Pence in Five Parts, Two Books Begun This Afternoon and Kept Apart, the Bag at Twenty-Eight Pence, Being Two Shillings and Fourpence, the First Column at Four Hundred and Forty-Eight and the Second at Two Thousand One Hundred and Fourteen*

Tuesday the fifth day of July, with that market open from about nine and the lid of that step uncovered by ten, a yard below that market with a shed standing in it, and about nine people in that shed at half past five that afternoon.

**The eighty-eighth wage Tuesday and not a rota day and not a wipe, and the lid read out at about ten in four minutes by a man of about thirty-one of the second lay and standing at twenty-six lines and one hundred and four marks and not moved, and the chest with the twenty envelopes in it shut from ten until four, and the eighty-eighth week standing fallen due at the close of that day behind the eighty-seventh. Line six went onto that money sheet this afternoon with the day the work was done against it, being one thousand three hundred and forty-eight pence in five parts, and it is settled into the foot of the sheet on Thursday. Two books were begun at about half past three by the woman of thirty of no office and the two of them are kept apart, and one man took his page up and went down the road at about five. A hundred and sixty-eight pence came into that yard at about ten and a hundred and sixty-eight went out of it at about four, and under that slate is twenty-eight pence, being two shillings and fourpence, and no tin in that shed has been opened since the twenty-ninth of April past.**

**Nine counters about things that are not being done ran a day on this morning. That notice of the seventeenth of May past stands on its four hundred and thirteenth day. Four hundred and fifteen days have gone by without anybody asking a bellwright of this harbor, and his chest stays shut. Two objects of nobody's on that crown are into their seven hundred and fifty-third day. That bench under that pump has stood bare for the five hundred and nineteenth night. Two lights about a mile off that point have spent three hundred and thirty-seventh days with no figure set against them. A six-line sheet at the back of that end stall has reached its two hundred and ninety-second day. The paper of four lines in Fish Street is on its three hundred and seventh. A nine-line sheet with a seal on it in that front street has stood three hundred and twenty-fourth day. A man of about fifty-one has kept three hundred and fifty-five days of not saying anything to anybody about a page of six names. That third column is empty and unappointed and a boy's hand of twenty-two is at the head of it and his surname is on no page and he is off the water. That fourth column stands at one hundred and twenty-five and has taken no line and is neither shut nor struck. About two sea miles off that mole head lie nine hulls this afternoon and nine lay there on the twenty-first of January, and this harbor has not said a word about any of them easing. A man of about twenty-nine hears out of the left side of his head and it is two hundred and thirteen days, one more than the day before, no figure is set against it, and he is not on the water.**

---

The lid was read out at about ten in four minutes and did not move for the eighty-eighth time. The man of about thirty-one stood on that step with the lid open and read the twenty-six lines steady, and about nine people listened without writing anything down. The chest with the twenty envelopes stood shut from ten until four, and the eighty-eighth week stands fallen due at the close of the day behind the eighty-seventh, and the column has not moved on any of the eighty-eight.

---

The work for line six was done this morning off the Point and this yard did it. Four days of a boat hired at the figure in chalk came out of the chalk and not out of a page, and coal and oil for four days, and sixty yards of rope, and tin and sheet copper and gaskets for the pump, and five men at four days.

The keeper wrote the line onto that sheet at about two in the afternoon with the fifth day of July against it as the day the work was done, and it is settled into the foot of that sheet on Thursday the seventh. It is four times one hundred and sixty-eight for six hundred and seventy-two pence, and eighty pence for the coal and the oil, and sixty times three for one hundred and eighty pence of rope, and ninety-six pence for the tin and the copper and the gaskets, and five times four times sixteen for three hundred and twenty pence of men. Those five are one thousand three hundred and forty-eight pence, being eight marks, five shillings and eightpence, and the multiplication is printed at every one of the five parts.

---

At about half past three in the afternoon the woman of thirty of no office began a second book. She put the first of the two on the bench stone beside her own and stood the second on the shelf above the water butt, and she wrote on the cover of each what it was, being that one carries what she was told and the other carries what she was told by somebody else.

She gave her reason in about nine people without raising her voice. She said that nine communities have put nine pages on nine tables this summer and that every one of those pages has been carried by somebody, and that a page carried by somebody can be lost on a wet road or come back with a line gone off it, and that a claim on a page that is the only page is a claim that can be taken off that coast with the page.

About four people in that nine said that was right. The man of about thirty-two said the thing he said in June, which is that a page which prints who did not agree turns a refusal into a private thing, and that a second copy of that page prints it twice. She said she had heard him say it and that she was not stopping, and about four people said nothing at all, and the two of them are still married and the matter is open and no page carries a resolution of it.

---

At about five one man of one community took his page off the bench stone, read it, folded it and went down the road. He said nothing to anybody in the shed about why and nobody asked him, and about four people saw him go and about four did not notice him. The keeper wrote the hour he went at and wrote no reason against it, because no reason was given.

The two books stood on that bench stone and on that shelf at the hour of that day, and they were not on one another, and neither of them carries the other's figures.

---

At half past five on that Tuesday he took the shortfall out loud at the flat of that bench stone, in about five hundred and eighty-three words, to about nine people, and about four of those nine had stood in front of two books at about half past three.

"**For the eighty-eighth time a man of about thirty-one of the second lay stood on that step at about ten with the lid open and read out twenty-six lines in four minutes, and the figure at the foot of that lid is the same figure it carried at seven in the morning, being twenty-six lines and one hundred and four marks. The chest with the twenty envelopes in it stands shut from ten until four, and the eighty-eighth week stands fallen due at the close of today behind the eighty-seventh.**

"**Line six went on this afternoon with the fifth of July against it as the day the work was done, and it is settled on Thursday. Four times one hundred and sixty-eight for six hundred and seventy-two, and eighty for coal and oil, and sixty times three for one hundred and eighty of rope, and ninety-six for tin and copper and gaskets, and five times four times sixteen for three hundred and twenty of men, and those five are one thousand three hundred and forty-eight pence, being eight marks, five shillings and eightpence, with the multiplication at every part.**

"**The woman of thirty of no office began a second book at about half past three and put the first on the bench stone and the second on the shelf above the butt and wrote on each what it was. Her reason is that a page carried by somebody can be lost on a wet road, and a claim on a page that is the only page is a claim that can be taken off this coast with the page. About four said that was right. I said the thing I said in June, that a page which prints who did not agree turns a refusal into a private thing, and a second copy of that page prints it twice. She said she had heard me say it and she was not stopping. The two of us are married and this is not settled and no page will settle it.**

"**The sheet stands at one hundred and fourteen thousand six hundred and sixty-six pence out until Thursday and one hundred and four thousand and twenty-six pence short, and what goes into it is ten thousand six hundred and forty pence on every day. Four hundred and fifty pence is still on that board in the front street. A hundred and sixty-eight pence in at about ten and a hundred and sixty-eight out at about four, and twenty-eight plus one hundred and sixty-eight less one hundred and sixty-eight is twenty-eight, and under that slate is twenty-eight pence, being two shillings and fourpence, and that is six hundred and twelve pence short of the wage of four marks, being three marks eleven shillings. No tin in that shed has been opened since the twenty-ninth of April past and this yard is now one hundred and twenty-eight days without one.**

"**And the last of it. That cistern was not charged and its covers lay on it from nine until four and nobody walked on the water. The far bench was occupied from ten until four with nothing put on it. About two sea miles off that mole head there are nine hulls this afternoon and there were nine there on the twenty-first of January. A man of about twenty-nine hears out of the left side of his head and it is two hundred and thirteen days today and nothing begun this afternoon touched it by a day.**"

Nobody was thanked for the arithmetic and nobody was thanked for a second book, and that shed stood empty by about six with its door standing open on a slip nobody was on, and that page of that week had seventeen lines on it, and that wooden tally slate was still lying flat on that bench stone with nothing on it.

---

**THE LID DID NOT MOVE FOR THE EIGHTY-EIGHTH TIME, BEING TWENTY-SIX LINES AND ONE HUNDRED AND FOUR MARKS, WITH THE CHEST SHUT FROM TEN UNTIL FOUR. LINE SIX IS ON THAT SHEET WITH THE FIFTH OF JULY AGAINST IT AS THE DAY THE WORK WAS DONE AND IS SETTLED ON THURSDAY, BEING ONE THOUSAND THREE HUNDRED AND FORTY-EIGHT PENCE, BEING EIGHT MARKS, FIVE SHILLINGS AND EIGHTPENCE, IN FIVE PARTS WITH THE MULTIPLICATION AT EVERY ONE. TWO BOOKS WERE BEGUN THIS AFTERNOON AND KEPT APART AND THEY ARE NOT ON ONE ANOTHER. THE SHEET STANDS AT ONE HUNDRED AND FOURTEEN THOUSAND SIX HUNDRED AND SIXTY-SIX PENCE OUT AND ONE HUNDRED AND FOUR THOUSAND AND TWENTY-SIX PENCE SHORT. THE BAG HOLDS TWENTY-EIGHT PENCE, BEING TWO SHILLINGS AND FOURPENCE.**

*First column, entered on Tuesday the fifth day of July at about six in the evening in that shed: that the said day was the eighty-eighth wage Tuesday and was not a rota day and was not a wipe, and that the lid was read out at about ten in four minutes by a man of about thirty-one of the second lay and stands at twenty-six lines and one hundred and four marks and did not move, with the chest with the twenty envelopes in it shut from ten until four, and the eighty-eighth week standing fallen due at the close of the day behind the eighty-seventh; and that line six went onto that sheet at about two in the afternoon with the fifth day of July against it as the day the work was done, being four times one hundred and sixty-eight pence for six hundred and seventy-two pence and eighty pence for coal and oil and sixty times three pence for one hundred and eighty pence of rope and ninety-six pence for tin and copper and gaskets and five times four times sixteen pence for three hundred and twenty pence of men, being one thousand three hundred and forty-eight pence, being eight marks, five shillings and eightpence, and it is settled on the seventh; and that the woman of thirty of no office began a second book at about half past three and put the first on that bench stone and the second on the shelf above the butt, and that the disagreement between them is open and is not settled; and that one man took his page at about five and went down the road and said nothing about why and nobody asked him.*

*Second column, not checkable from a mole, entered on the said Tuesday at about six in the evening in that shed, and no name is against it: that at about two in the afternoon the reason the day of the work and not the day of the entry is against the line is that a line a stranger cannot date is a line a stranger has to take on trust, and this sheet is checkable by anybody with a pencil; that at about half past three the reason the two books are put on two pieces of wood and not on one is that one wet road takes one book and does not take both, and the whole of the reason for a second book is defeated if the second book sits under the first; that at about four the reason the same objection is entered again and is not answered is that an objection put once is an objection this yard has had, and an objection put twice with nothing said back is an objection the two of them have, and no page of this movement will say otherwise; and that at about five the reason the hour a man went at is written with no reason against it is that the reason belongs to him and the hour is all that happened in this shed.*
