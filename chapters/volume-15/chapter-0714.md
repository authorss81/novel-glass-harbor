# Chapter 714

*Monday the Fourth Day of July, a Rota Day and the Hundred and Sixty-Third Charge on That Cistern, Eleven Trips by Hand and Walked on by None of It, A Man of About Fifty-Six Come Down the Road to Look at Two Marks on One Page and Saying That a Seam Shows Two Things Were Different and Never Which of Them Was True, the Bag at Twenty-Eight Pence, Being Two Shillings and Fourpence, the First Column at Four Hundred and Forty-Seven and the Second at Two Thousand One Hundred and Nine*

Monday the fourth day of July, with that market open from about nine and the cistern at the back of it empty to the stones by about eight, a yard below that market with a shed standing in it, and about nine people in that shed at half past five that afternoon.

**A rota day and not a wage Tuesday and not a wipe, and that cistern charged in eleven trips by hand between about eight in the morning and about eleven and walked on by none of it, being the hundred and sixty-third charge, and the covers left lying on it from about eleven until about four in the afternoon, and that butt behind the market covered from eight in the morning until four in the afternoon with no charge on it either. A man of about fifty-six came down that coast road at about eleven in the morning to look at two marks on one page and said that a seam will show a woman that two things were different and will never show her which of them was true. A hundred and sixty-eight pence came into that yard at about ten and a hundred and sixty-eight went out of it at about four, and under that slate is twenty-eight pence, being two shillings and fourpence, and no tin in that shed has been opened since the twenty-ninth of April past.**

**Nine counters about things that are not being done ran a day on this morning. That notice of the seventeenth of May past stands on its four hundred and twelfth day. Four hundred and fourteen days have gone by without anybody asking a bellwright of this harbor, and his chest stays shut. Two objects of nobody's on that crown are into their seven hundred and fifty-second day. That bench under that pump has stood bare for the five hundred and eighteenth night. Two lights about a mile off that point have spent three hundred and thirty-sixth days with no figure set against them. A six-line sheet at the back of that end stall has reached its two hundred and ninety-first day. The paper of four lines in Fish Street is on its three hundred and sixth. A nine-line sheet with a seal on it in that front street has stood three hundred and twenty-third day. A man of about fifty-one has kept three hundred and fifty-four days of not saying anything to anybody about a page of six names. That third column is empty and unappointed and a boy's hand of twenty-two is at the head of it and his surname is on no page and he is off the water. That fourth column stands at one hundred and twenty-five and has taken no line and is neither shut nor struck. About two sea miles off that mole head lie nine hulls this afternoon and nine lay there on the twenty-first of January, and this harbor has not said a word about any of them easing. A man of about twenty-nine hears out of the left side of his head and it is two hundred and twelve days, one more than the day before, no figure is set against it, and he is not on the water.**

---

That cistern went its eleventh trip at about eleven for the hundred and sixty-third time and the covers went on at the same hour as they went on last week. The men who carry it worked steady and did not stop to look at the water, and about four people who came for water after four were served without waiting. The man who keeps the leaf wrote the charge number and the hours on the page of that week, and he wrote that the rota days of this movement are four and that two of them are behind.

The man of about fifty-six came at about eleven with a bundle of reed under his arm and he stood in the shed door for a while before he came in. He has kept the marks of that reed for about thirty years and he has never kept one for this yard and he was not asked to.

---

He looked at the page for a long time and then he turned it over and showed the back, where there is a seam. It runs across the sheet about three inches from the fold and it is not a tear and it is not a fold, being a place where the damp has come through two sheets at once, and where the ink of the time has darkened across the seam and the ink of the flat stroke has not.

He said that is what a seam is. Two sheets lay together while something wet was in them, and the mark that came through is the mark of two sheets having been there, and it cannot tell a woman what was written on the one underneath and was taken off again. He said the water takes a line off a sheet without any harm coming to it and leaves the seam behind, and the seam is the honest part.

About four people in that nine wanted the seam to say who had taken the mark off. He said he was not going to be the one who says that, and he gave his reason in one sentence, being that a man who has kept marks for thirty years and has never kept one for this yard is the only man in that shed who cannot be moved to say what he saw, and he would rather stay that way.

---

The second thing he said was about the water and not about the paper. He said that reed channel has cut a new bed about a boat's breadth east of the one it used, and that it cut it in the winter, and that the reed has grown back over both and is the same reed on both and stands about the same depth in both, and a man standing in that channel cannot tell by looking down which bed the water is in.

He said the same of the ground under it, being that the old bed is still there under the silt and will carry a boat for a while yet, and that both beds are true water and one of them is where the fish are. Then he said the thing that went onto the page, which is that a page can carry a time that is a half of the width of that channel out of true and the man who wrote it cannot know it and did not know it.

He was not asked to say more about the page. He was asked one thing only, being whether the time his community gave would hold if the channel went on cutting, and he said it would hold for about a week and then it would want looking at, and that this yard would have to ask them again and could ask them as often as it liked.

---

The keeper wrote all three things down with the hour against each, being the seam, the new bed and the week. He wrote that no figure of money stands against the bed and no figure of sea miles is set against the seam, and that a page with a seam in it is entered whole and not cut about. The woman of thirty of no office copied the three lines into her own book and read them out twice because about five of that nine cannot read a column.

Nobody in that shed called that page a bad page and nobody in that shed called it a good page, and the man of about thirty-two came in at about three and read the three lines and went out again and did not say that it settled anything, because it did not settle anything.

---

The man who keeps the leaf read the shortfall out loud at that bench stone at about half past five on that Monday, in about nine hundred words, to about nine people, and about four of those nine had carried reed into the shed at about eleven.

"**Four men carried eleven trips of water up from that butt this morning between about eight and about eleven and filled it, and that was the hundred and sixty-third time this yard has filled it, and the covers went on at the hour they went on last week. A man of about fifty-six came down that road at about eleven with a bundle of reed under his arm and he has kept the marks of that channel for about thirty years and has never kept one for this yard.**

"**He turned the page over and showed the back. There is a seam across it about three inches from the fold, where the damp came through two sheets at once, and the ink of the time is darkened across it and the ink of the flat stroke is not. He said a seam will show a woman that two things were different and will never show her which of them was true, and that the water takes a line off a sheet without harm and leaves the seam behind, and the seam is the honest part of it.**

"**He said the channel has cut a new bed about a boat's breadth east of the old one this winter and the reed has grown over both and is the same reed standing the same depth on both, so a man standing in there cannot tell by looking down which bed the water is in. He said the old bed is still under the silt and will carry a boat for a while yet, and both beds are true water and one of them is where the fish are. He said a page can carry a time a half breadth out of true and the man who wrote it cannot know it and did not know it. He was asked one thing, being whether the time would hold, and he said about a week and then it would want looking at.**

"**Nobody in this shed has called that page good or bad. The sheet stands at one hundred and fourteen thousand six hundred and sixty-six pence out and one hundred and four thousand and twenty-six pence short. Four hundred and fifty pence is still on that board in the front street. A hundred and sixty-eight pence in at about ten and a hundred and sixty-eight out at about four, and twenty-eight plus one hundred and sixty-eight less one hundred and sixty-eight is twenty-eight, and under that slate is twenty-eight pence, being two shillings and fourpence, and that is six hundred and twelve pence short of the wage of four marks, being three marks eleven shillings. No tin in that shed has been opened since the twenty-ninth of April past and this yard is now one hundred and twenty-seven days without one.**

"**And the last of it. The wall in that shed is still two lines and about eleven inches of empty plaster. About two sea miles off that mole head there are nine hulls this afternoon and there were nine there on the twenty-first of January. A man of about twenty-nine hears out of the left side of his head and it is two hundred and twelve days today and nothing said about a seam touched it by a day.**"

Nobody was thanked for the arithmetic and nobody was thanked for thirty years of reed, and that shed stood empty by about six with its door standing open on a slip nobody was on, and that page of that week had sixteen lines on it, and that wooden tally slate was still lying flat on that bench stone with nothing on it.

---

**THAT CISTERN WAS CHARGED IN ELEVEN TRIPS BY HAND AND WAS THE HUNDRED AND SIXTY-THIRD CHARGE, AND IT WAS WALKED ON BY NONE OF IT. A SEAM ACROSS THE BACK OF A PAGE SHOWS THAT TWO SHEETS WERE LAID TOGETHER AND CANNOT SHOW WHAT WAS ON THE ONE UNDERNEATH, AND A CHANNEL THAT HAS CUT A NEW BED A BOAT'S BREADTH EAST OF THE OLD ONE IS THE SAME REED OVER BOTH. THE SHEET STANDS AT ONE HUNDRED AND FOURTEEN THOUSAND SIX HUNDRED AND SIXTY-SIX PENCE OUT AND ONE HUNDRED AND FOUR THOUSAND AND TWENTY-SIX PENCE SHORT. THE BAG HOLDS TWENTY-EIGHT PENCE, BEING TWO SHILLINGS AND FOURPENCE.**

---

---

*First column, entered on Monday the fourth day of July at about six in the evening in that shed: that the said day was a rota day and was not a wage Tuesday and was not a wipe, and that that cistern was charged in eleven trips by hand between about eight and about eleven and was walked on by none of it, being the hundred and sixty-third charge, with the covers lying on it from about eleven until about four; and that a man of about fifty-six came down that coast road at about eleven with a bundle of reed under his arm, and that he has kept the marks of that channel for about thirty years and has never kept one for this yard and was not asked to; and that he showed the back of that page, where there is a seam across it about three inches from the fold, the ink of the time being darkened across it and the ink of the flat stroke not, and that he said a seam shows that two things were different and never which of them was true; and that he said the channel has cut a new bed about a boat's breadth east of the old one and the reed is the same reed over both, and that both beds are true water and one of them is where the fish are; and that he said the time his community gave would hold about a week and then would want looking at.*

*Second column, not checkable from a mole, entered on the said Monday at about six in the evening in that shed, and no name is against it: that at about eleven in the morning the reason no figure of sea miles is set against the seam is that the two are a length on a page and a distance a boat lies, and a number that means a length and a number that means a distance are two kinds of number and cannot be laid one against the other; that at about half past eleven the reason no figure of money stands against the new bed is that the bed cost that community nothing to cut and would cost them everything to be wrong about, and a figure of pence printed beside it would be read as a price on a mistake; that at about one the reason a man of thirty years is not asked to say what he saw is that he is the only man in that shed who cannot be moved, and a yard that uses that man is a yard that has spent the only thing it has; and that at about half past three the reason no page calls that page good or bad is that a seam settles the question of what was different and leaves open the question of what was true, and a page that answers both has answered a question it cannot reach.*
