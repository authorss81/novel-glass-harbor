# Chapter 757

*Tuesday the Sixteenth Day of August, the Ninety-Fourth Wage Tuesday and the Last of Those Eight, the Column on the Lid of That Step Read at About Ten in Four Minutes at Twenty-Six Lines and One Hundred and Four Marks and Not Moved, the Chest With the Twenty Envelopes in It Shut From Ten Until Four, and a Further Week Standing Fallen Due at the Close of That Reading, No Line on That Sheet and No Figure Written Into Its Foot, the Bag at Twenty-Eight Pence, the First Column at Four Hundred and Ninety and the Second at Two Thousand Three Hundred and Twenty-Four*

Tuesday the sixteenth day of August, with that market open from about nine and a low cloud with the wind in the east and no swell at all on that bar, a yard below that market with a shed standing in it, and about nine people in that shed at half past five that afternoon.

**The ninety-fourth wage Tuesday and the last of those eight, and not a rota day and not a wipe. The column on the lid of that step was read out at about ten in the morning in four minutes by a man of about thirty-one of the second lay and came to twenty-six lines and one hundred and four marks and did not move, and the chest with the twenty envelopes in it was shut from ten until four as it is shut on every one of those eight Tuesdays, and a further week stood fallen due at the close of that reading. No line went onto that money sheet and the keeper said out loud at about four that the day carries no line and is not one of any form. The sheet stands at one hundred and twenty-two thousand five hundred and forty-eight pence out and one hundred and eleven thousand nine hundred and eight pence short, against ten thousand six hundred and forty pence in. A hundred and sixty-eight pence came into that yard at about ten and a hundred and sixty-eight went out of it at about four, and under that slate is twenty-eight pence, being two shillings and fourpence, and no tin in that shed has been opened since the twenty-ninth of April past.**

**Nine of the standing things went into that Tuesday and this is where they stood at noon. That notice of the seventeenth of May past is on its four hundred and fifty-fifth day. That bellwright of this harbor has now gone four hundred and fifty-seven days with nobody asking him anything and his chest is shut and stays shut. Those two objects belonging to nobody on that crown are into the seven hundred and ninety-fifth day of themselves. That bench under that pump has now stood bare for the five hundred and sixty-first night running. Two lights about a mile off that point have gone three hundred and seventy-ninth days with nothing set against them. The six-line sheet at the back of that end stall has reached the three hundred and thirty-fourth day of its standing. That paper of four lines in Fish Street is on the three hundred and forty-ninth day. The nine-line sheet carrying a seal in that front street has stood three hundred and sixty-sixth day. And a man of about fifty-one has kept three hundred and ninety-seventh days of saying nothing to anybody about a page of six names. That third column is empty and unappointed with a boy's hand of twenty-two at the head of it, his surname is on no page, and he is off the water. That fourth column stands at one hundred and twenty-five and has taken no line and is neither shut nor struck. About two sea miles off that mole head there are nine hulls this afternoon and nine lay there on the twenty-first of January, and this harbor has not said a word about any of them easing. A man of about twenty-nine hears out of the left side of his head and it is two hundred and fifty-five days today, one more than the day before, no figure is set against it, and he is not on the water.**

---

That column was read at about ten in four minutes by a man of about thirty-one of the second lay and it came to twenty-six lines and one hundred and four marks and it did not move, and that is the reading of the ninety-fourth of those eight Tuesdays and the last of them.

The further week stood fallen due at the close of that reading and the reading was not asked for a second time, and the chest with the twenty envelopes in it was shut from ten until four and nobody put a hand on that lid at any hour of that day.

---

At about eleven the keeper said out loud at the flat of that stone, in about nine people, that eight Tuesdays was a run and that a run is not an ending, and that the week that has just fallen due is the ninth and that it would fall due on the twenty-third and that nothing about that Tuesday had changed since the twenty-eighth of June.

About four of that nine agreed with him and about four said nothing and nobody said the run had been a good one and nobody said it had been a bad one.

---

At about one the man of about thirty-two said out loud in about nine people that a wage Tuesday is the day a harbor finds out what it owes and that finding out is not paying, and that this harbor had now found out eight times in a run of weeks and had paid none of them, and that he was not going to pretend the ninth would be different because nobody in this shed had said it would be.

The woman of thirty of no office said out loud that she had a figure of forty marks first after food for a person of no office that had been on her own page since the spring and that it had not gone down either, and that she was not going to say that out loud in a yard where nothing was paid so that hers sounded like a complaint.

---

At about two a man of about forty-one of that landing came in over the bar with a sheet in the hand of the man of about thirty-eight and the hours on it were read at the door of that shed and written with the day against them.

The hours say that the ninth section of that bell was cut into two at that landing on the Friday with a saw and a man of about fifty holding it and that the two pieces were set on two beds and that the ninth has not been rung since and that nobody at that landing has asked for it to be rung.

---

At about half past three the keeper read the hours out again at that stone and wrote them with the day and the hour against them and said out loud that the hours came down that road in somebody else's hand and that this yard had not asked for them and had not sent anybody to fetch them.

The man of about thirty-two said out loud that a page a person brings a yard is a page the yard did not ask for and that it is still a page, and that the whole of the argument in this shed since June has been about what a yard does with a page it did not ask for, and about four of that nine agreed with him and about four said nothing.

---

The ordinary work of that Tuesday went on and was not stopped by any of it, being the pump run clean from about nine and a boat of about six tons hauled out on the east slip and washed down with a bucket and the fourth lay tied twice in that flat water.

The sheet with the fourteen gaps at the end stall stood all day with no figure moved by one, and the far bench was occupied from ten until four with nothing put on it, and that man of about sixty-one was seen at the end of that lane at about four going away from this yard and not coming in.

---

A hundred and four marks stood still on the lid of that step and at about half past five on that Tuesday the whole of that day was put out loud at that stone, in about nine hundred and thirty-six words, to about nine people, and about four of those nine had stood at the lid of that step at about ten and heard twenty-six lines read out and not moved.

"**That column was read at about ten in four minutes by a man of about thirty-one of the second lay and it came to twenty-six lines and one hundred and four marks and it did not move, and that is the reading of the ninety-fourth of those eight Tuesdays and the last of them. The further week stood fallen due at the close of that reading and the reading was not asked for a second time, and the chest with the twenty envelopes in it was shut from ten until four and nobody put a hand on that lid at any hour of this day.**"

"**At about eleven I said out loud at the flat of this stone, in about nine of us, that eight Tuesdays was a run and that a run is not an ending, and that the week that has just fallen due is the ninth and that it would fall due on the twenty-third, and that nothing about that Tuesday had changed since the twenty-eighth of June. About four of that nine agreed with me and about four said nothing, and nobody said the run had been a good one and nobody said it had been a bad one.**"

"**At about one the man of about thirty-two said out loud in about nine of us that a wage Tuesday is the day a harbor finds out what it owes and that finding out is not paying, and that this harbor had now found out eight times in a run of weeks and had paid none of them, and that he was not going to pretend the ninth would be different because nobody in this shed had said it would be. The woman of thirty of no office said out loud that she had a figure of forty marks first after food for a person of no office that had been on her own page since the spring and that it had not gone down either, and that she was not going to say that out loud in a yard where nothing was paid so that hers sounded like a complaint.**"

"**At about two a man of about forty-one of that landing came in over the bar with a sheet in the hand of the man of about thirty-eight and the hours on it were read at the door of this shed and written with the day against them. The hours say that the ninth section of that bell was cut into two at that landing on the Friday with a saw and a man of about fifty holding it, and that the two pieces were set on two beds, and that the ninth has not been rung since, and that nobody at that landing has asked for it to be rung.**"

"**At about half past three I read the hours out again at that stone and wrote them with the day and the hour against them and said out loud that the hours came down that road in somebody else's hand and that this yard had not asked for them and had not sent anybody to fetch them. The man of about thirty-two said out loud that a page a person brings a yard is a page the yard did not ask for and that it is still a page, and that the whole of the argument in this shed since June has been about what a yard does with a page it did not ask for, and about four of that nine agreed with him and about four said nothing.**"

"**And the rest of it. The pump ran clean from about nine and a boat of about six tons was hauled out on that east slip and washed down with a bucket and the fourth lay was tied twice in that flat water, and nobody in this shed stopped work because of what was said at about one. The sheet with the fourteen gaps stood at that end stall all day with no figure moved by one, and the far bench was occupied from ten until four with nothing put on it, and that man of about sixty-one was seen at the end of that lane at about four going away from this yard and not coming in. No line went onto that money sheet and I said out loud at about four that the day carries no line and is not one of any form. The sheet stands at one hundred and twenty-two thousand five hundred and forty-eight pence out and one hundred and eleven thousand nine hundred and eight pence short, against ten thousand six hundred and forty pence in. No tin in this shed has been opened since the twenty-ninth of April past and this yard is now one hundred and seventy days without one. Twenty-eight plus two thousand one hundred and eighty-one plus one thousand eight hundred and forty-eight less four thousand and twenty-nine is twenty-eight, and under that slate is twenty-eight pence, being two shillings and fourpence, and that is six hundred and twelve pence short of the wage of four marks, being three marks eleven shillings, with a hundred and sixty-eight pence in at about ten and a hundred and sixty-eight out at about four. About two sea miles off that mole head there are nine hulls this evening and there were nine there on the twenty-first of January. A man of about twenty-nine hears out of the left side of his head and it is two hundred and fifty-five days today and nothing read at about two today touched that figure by a day.**"

Nobody was thanked for the finding out of what a harbor owes and nobody was thanked for the eight weeks it has not paid, and that shed stood empty by about six with its door standing open on a slip nobody was on, and that page of that week had thirty-one lines on it, and that wooden tally slate was still lying flat on that bench stone with nothing on it.

**THE NINETY-FOURTH WAGE TUESDAY AND THE LAST OF THOSE EIGHT. THE COLUMN ON THE LID OF THAT STEP WAS READ AT ABOUT TEN IN FOUR MINUTES AT TWENTY-SIX LINES AND ONE HUNDRED AND FOUR MARKS AND DID NOT MOVE. THE CHEST WITH THE TWENTY ENVELOPES IN IT WAS SHUT FROM TEN UNTIL FOUR AND A FURTHER WEEK STOOD FALLEN DUE AT THE CLOSE OF THAT READING. NO LINE WENT ONTO THAT MONEY SHEET. THE BAG HOLDS TWENTY-EIGHT PENCE, BEING TWO SHILLINGS AND FOURPENCE.**

*First column, entered on Tuesday the sixteenth day of August at about six in the evening in that shed: that the said day was the ninety-fourth wage Tuesday and the last of those eight and was not a rota day and was not a wipe; and that the column on the lid of that step was read at about ten in four minutes by a man of about thirty-one of the second lay and came to twenty-six lines and one hundred and four marks and did not move, and that the chest with the twenty envelopes in it was shut from ten until four and nobody put a hand on that lid at any hour of that day, and that a further week stood fallen due at the close of that reading and the reading was not asked for a second time; and that at about eleven the keeper said out loud in about nine that eight Tuesdays was a run and not an ending and that the ninth would fall due on the twenty-third and that nothing about that Tuesday had changed since the twenty-eighth of June; and that at about one the man of about thirty-two said out loud in about nine that a wage Tuesday is the day a harbor finds out what it owes and that finding out is not paying, and that this harbor had found out eight times and paid none of them; and that at the same hour the woman of thirty of no office said out loud that she had a figure of forty marks first after food for a person of no office on her own page since the spring that had not gone down either, and that she was not going to say that out loud in a yard where nothing was paid so that hers sounded like a complaint; and that at about two a man of about forty-one of that landing came in over the bar with a sheet in the hand of the man of about thirty-eight, the hours being that the ninth section of that bell was cut into two at that landing on the Friday with a saw and a man of about fifty holding it, and that the two pieces were set on two beds, and that the ninth has not been rung since and that nobody at that landing has asked for it to be rung; and that at about half past three the keeper read those hours out again and wrote them with the day and hour and said the hours had come down that road in somebody else's hand and that this yard had not asked for them and had not sent anybody to fetch them, and that the man of about thirty-two said a page a person brings a yard is a page the yard did not ask for and is still a page; and that no line went onto that money sheet and the keeper said out loud at about four that the day carries no line and is not one of any form.*

*Second column, not checkable from a mole, entered on the said Tuesday at about six in the evening in that shed, and no name is against it: that at about eleven the reason the keeper said eight Tuesdays was a run and not an ending is that a run of eight ends on a day somebody can name and a run of nine does not, and a harbor that has been owed for eight weeks will be owed for a ninth and the ninth is the one that decides whether the eighth mattered; that at about one the reason the woman of thirty of no office did not say her figure in a yard where nothing was paid is that a figure said beside other unpaid figures stops being a debt and becomes a grievance, and a debt is a thing a person can be given and a grievance is a thing a person can only keep; that at about half past three the reason the keeper wrote that the hours came in somebody else's hand is that a record delivered is not a record owned, and the difference between the two is the only thing on this coast that has ever been worth writing on a page; and that at about four the reason the day carries no line is that a settlement on a day a yard is shut is a settlement nobody watched, and the whole of the third form of that sheet is that a stranger can find the day a figure landed on.*
