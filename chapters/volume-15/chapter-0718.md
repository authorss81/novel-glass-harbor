# Chapter 718

*Friday the Eighth Day of July, Not a Rota Day and Not a Wage Tuesday and Not a Wipe, a Line Closing in Its Own Hand in About Nine People Because a Seam Cannot Show Which of Two Things Was True, Put Into the Third Column of That Page on Its Own Saying and Not Counted and Not Said, the Sheet at One Hundred and Sixteen Thousand and Fourteen Pence Out and One Hundred and Five Thousand Three Hundred and Seventy-Four Pence Short, the Bag at Twenty-Eight Pence, Being Two Shillings and Fourpence, the First Column at Four Hundred and Fifty-One and the Second at Two Thousand One Hundred and Twenty-Nine*

Friday the eighth day of July, with that market busy by ten and the first of a warm wind off the land in the afternoon, a yard below that market with a shed standing in it, and about nine people in that shed at half past five that afternoon.

**Not a rota day and not a wage Tuesday and not a wipe, and no charge put on that cistern and its covers lying on it from nine in the morning until four in the afternoon. A route closed in its own hand at about two in the afternoon in about nine people, and the closing was written on that community's own page by a woman of about fifty-eight in her own hand, and the three columns of that page were not counted and the count was not said in that shed and is not in this slip. The fitting-out sheet stands at one hundred and sixteen thousand and fourteen pence out and one hundred and five thousand three hundred and seventy-four pence short. A hundred and sixty-eight pence came into that yard at about ten and a hundred and sixty-eight went out of it at about four, and under that slate is twenty-eight pence, being two shillings and fourpence, and no tin in that shed has been opened since the twenty-ninth of April past.**

**Nine counters about things that are not being done ran a day on this morning. That notice of the seventeenth of May past stands on its four hundred and sixteenth day. Four hundred and eighteen days have gone by without anybody asking a bellwright of this harbor, and his chest stays shut. Two objects of nobody's on that crown are into their seven hundred and fifty-sixth day. That bench under that pump has stood bare for the five hundred and twenty-second night. Two lights about a mile off that point have spent three hundred and fortieth days with no figure set against them. A six-line sheet at the back of that end stall has reached its two hundred and ninety-fifth day. The paper of four lines in Fish Street is on its three hundred and tenth. A nine-line sheet with a seal on it in that front street has stood three hundred and twenty-seventh day. A man of about fifty-one has kept three hundred and fifty-eight days of not saying anything to anybody about a page of six names. That third column is empty and unappointed and a boy's hand of twenty-two is at the head of it and his surname is on no page and he is off the water. That fourth column stands at one hundred and twenty-five and has taken no line and is neither shut nor struck. About two sea miles off that mole head lie nine hulls this afternoon and nine lay there on the twenty-first of January, and this harbor has not said a word about any of them easing. A man of about twenty-nine hears out of the left side of his head and it is two hundred and sixteen days, one more than the day before, no figure is set against it, and he is not on the water.**

---

The line went onto the seat in the ground at about one in the afternoon and then came back up wet, and the pump was run for an hour on the tank that was left. Four men were on the boat and about nine people in that shed. Nothing else was done that morning except the ordinary work of a yard that has a boat of its own to keep.

---

At about two the woman of about fifty-eight stood up at the flat of that bench stone with the Lowfen Reed page in her hand and read the closing out loud in about nine people. The closing was four sentences long and it was in her own hand and it had been written at that creek that morning and come down the road in her apron.

The four sentences said that the time given for that route was given by a man who was not free to refuse it, and that a page so given is not that community's answer. They said that the seam across the back of that page shows that a mark was taken off and cannot show which mark it was, and that a thing which cannot show the difference between a willing hand and a pressed hand cannot be used to show that the rest of that coast's pages are good either. They said that the route is closed from this morning and that the closing is in their hand and not this yard's.

Nobody in that shed called that route anything. The word that would have been used was not used by anybody, and the man of about thirty-two stood with his hands on the edge of that bench stone and did not use it either.

---

About four people in that nine asked what happens to the four stones of that creek now, and the woman of about fifty-eight said that they are theirs and will be looked at in the spring by their own people. The keeper wrote that down with the hour and did not offer a boat and did not send a hand, and the reason is in the second column.

The Lowfen Reed put itself into the third column of the page of routes on its own saying, being the column of the dormant, and the keeper wrote on the page of that week that the Lowfen Reed has placed itself there and that the three columns of the page of routes were not counted today and are not counted in a room on any day of this movement. That is the second of three times they are written and the third is on the last of the sixty days, and neither of them is in a shed's mouth.

---

The yard did not argue with the four sentences. The man of about thirty-two was asked at about three whether he thought the coast's other pages were any better on the strength of that one, and he said that a seam shows that two things were different and a man who has been shown one seam has learned nothing at all about any other page, and about four people agreed with that and about four thought the seam showed rather more than that.

He was right that it shows less. Nobody wrote on any page in that shed that the other pages on that coast were good, and the woman of thirty of no office wrote in both of her books, at about four, that a closed route is not evidence about an open one, and she read that out twice.

---

At about half past five on that Friday the shortfall was taken out loud at that bench stone in about four hundred and ninety-three words to about nine people, and about four of those nine had heard four sentences read at about two.

"**A route closed at about two this afternoon in about nine people, and the closing was four sentences long in the hand of a woman of about fifty-eight, written at that creek this morning and brought down in her apron. The first sentence is that the time was given by a man who was not free to refuse it and that a page so given is not that community's answer. The second is that the seam across the back of that page shows a mark was taken off and cannot show which mark, and that a thing which cannot tell a willing hand from a pressed hand cannot show the rest of that coast's pages are good. The third is that the route is closed from this morning and the closing is in their hand and not ours. The fourth is that they will not be asked why.**

"**Nobody in this shed called that route anything. The four stones are theirs and will be looked at in the spring by their own people, and I have written that down and offered no boat and sent no hand, and about four people asked me who would look at them and I said their own people would.**

"**The Lowfen Reed has put itself into the third column of the page of routes on its own saying, being the column of the dormant, and the three columns of that page were not counted today and are not counted in a room on any day of this fortnight.**

"**The sheet stands at one hundred and sixteen thousand and fourteen pence out and one hundred and five thousand three hundred and seventy-four pence short, against ten thousand six hundred and forty pence in, being sixty-six marks, six shillings and eightpence. Four hundred and fifty pence is still on that board in the front street. A hundred and sixty-eight pence in at about ten and a hundred and sixty-eight out at about four, and twenty-eight plus one hundred and sixty-eight less one hundred and sixty-eight is twenty-eight, and under that slate is twenty-eight pence, being two shillings and fourpence, and that is six hundred and twelve pence short of the wage of four marks, being three marks eleven shillings. No tin in that shed has been opened since the twenty-ninth of April past and this yard is now one hundred and thirty-one days without one.**

"**And the last of it. That cistern was not charged and its covers lay on it from nine until four. The far bench was occupied from ten until four with nothing put on it. About two sea miles off that mole head there are nine hulls this afternoon and there were nine there on the twenty-first of January. A man of about twenty-nine hears out of the left side of his head and it is two hundred and sixteen days today and nothing said at about two this afternoon touched it by a day.**"

Nobody was thanked for the arithmetic and nobody was thanked for four sentences, and that shed stood empty by about six with its door standing open on a slip nobody was on, and that page of that week had twenty lines on it, and that wooden tally slate was still lying flat on that bench stone with nothing on it.

---

**THE LOWFEN REED CLOSED ITSELF IN ITS OWN HAND AT ABOUT TWO IN THE AFTERNOON, BEING FOUR SENTENCES, AND THE THIRD COLUMN OF THE PAGE OF ROUTES CARRIES IT ON ITS OWN SAYING. THE SEAM SHOWS THAT A MARK WAS TAKEN OFF AND CANNOT SHOW WHICH MARK, AND A CLOSED ROUTE IS NOT EVIDENCE ABOUT AN OPEN ONE. THE THREE COLUMNS OF THE PAGE OF ROUTES WERE NOT COUNTED TODAY. THE SHEET STANDS AT ONE HUNDRED AND SIXTEEN THOUSAND AND FOURTEEN PENCE OUT AND ONE HUNDRED AND FIVE THOUSAND THREE HUNDRED AND SEVENTY-FOUR PENCE SHORT. THE BAG HOLDS TWENTY-EIGHT PENCE, BEING TWO SHILLINGS AND FOURPENCE.**

---

---

*First column, entered on Friday the eighth day of July at about six in the evening in that shed: that the said day was not a rota day and was not a wage Tuesday and was not a wipe, and that no charge was placed on that cistern and that the covers lay on it from nine in the morning until four in the afternoon; and that the Lowfen Reed put itself into the third column of the page of routes on its own saying at about two in the afternoon, in about nine people, after the closing had been written in that community's own hand on that community's own page, and that the closing was four sentences in the hand of a woman of about fifty-eight, written at that creek that morning and brought down in her apron; and that the four sentences are that the time was given by a man who was not free to refuse it and a page so given is not that community's answer, and that the seam shows a mark was taken off and cannot show which, and that a thing which cannot tell a willing hand from a pressed hand cannot show the rest of that coast's pages are good, and that the route is closed from that morning in their hand and not this yard's, and that they will not be asked why; and that the three columns of the page of routes were not counted on that day and were not said in a room.*

*Second column, not checkable from a mole, entered on the said Friday at about six in the evening in that shed, and no name is against it: that at about two in the afternoon the reason the four sentences were read in a shed and not entered in this yard's book alone is that a community closing its own line is doing a thing this yard has no standing to hold, and the page in front of me can only carry that it happened; that at about half past two the reason the third column was not counted is that a count taken today would put a figure in this yard's mouth against a thing nine people decided in a room at a creek four days away, and a number printed beside that is a number this party made; that at about three the reason no boat was offered for the four stones is that offering is a way of keeping, and a yard that offers to look after a line's own stones will be asked to look after the line; and that at about four the reason both books carry that a closed route is not evidence about an open one is that the tidiest lie available to a coast in this position is the one that takes one community's failure and spends it as proof for the rest.*
