# Chapter 756

*Monday the Fifteenth Day of August, a Rota Day and the Hundred and Seventy-Fifth Charge on That Cistern, Line Twelve Settled Into the Foot of That Money Sheet at About Four With Its Four Parts Read and Their Multiplications Before the Sum Went Down and the Foot Carrying the Thirteenth of August on the Face of the Line for the Work and the Fifteenth for the Settlement, the Foot of That Sheet Stepping From Tomorrow to One Hundred and Twenty-Two Thousand Five Hundred and Forty-Eight Pence Out and One Hundred and Eleven Thousand Nine Hundred and Eight Pence Short, the Bag at Twenty-Eight Pence, the First Column at Four Hundred and Eighty-Nine and the Second at Two Thousand Three Hundred and Nineteen*

Monday the fifteenth day of August, with that market open from about nine and a hard clear morning with the wind in the north-east and the swell dropping away off that bar from about ten, a yard below that market with a shed standing in it, and about nine people in that shed at half past five that afternoon.

**A rota day and not a wage Tuesday and not a wipe, and that cistern charged in eleven trips by hand between about eight in the morning and about eleven and walked on by none of it, being the hundred and seventy-fifth charge, and the covers left lying on it from about eleven until about four in the afternoon. Line twelve settled into the foot of that money sheet at about four and the four parts were read with their multiplications before the sum went down, the line carrying Saturday the thirteenth of August on its face for the day the work was done and this fifteenth for the day it settles. The foot of that sheet is read at the close of this day as it stood before the sum went down and stands at one hundred and twenty-one thousand eight hundred and eighty-two pence out and one hundred and eleven thousand two hundred and forty-two pence short, and from tomorrow it stands at one hundred and twenty-two thousand five hundred and forty-eight pence out and one hundred and eleven thousand nine hundred and eight pence short, against ten thousand six hundred and forty pence in. A hundred and sixty-eight pence came into that yard at about ten and a hundred and sixty-eight went out of it at about four, and under that slate is twenty-eight pence, being two shillings and fourpence, and no tin in that shed has been opened since the twenty-ninth of April past.**

**The nine counters ran in that shed this morning while that cistern was being filled and this is what they stood at. That notice of the seventeenth of May past is on its four hundred and fifty-fourth day. That bellwright of this harbor has now gone four hundred and fifty-six days with nobody asking him a question and his chest stays shut. Those two objects belonging to nobody on that crown are into the seven hundred and ninety-fourth day of themselves. That bench beneath that pump has stood bare for the five hundred and sixtieth night running. Two lights about a mile off that point have gone three hundred and seventy-eighth days and nothing is set against them. The six-line sheet at the back of that end stall has reached the three hundred and thirty-third day of its standing. That paper of four lines in Fish Street is on the three hundred and forty-eighth day. The nine-line sheet with a seal on it in that front street has stood three hundred and sixty-fifth day. And a man of about fifty-one has kept three hundred and ninety-sixth days of saying nothing to anybody about a page of six names. That third column is empty and unappointed with a boy's hand of twenty-two at the head of it, his surname is on no page, and he is off the water. That fourth column stands at one hundred and twenty-five and has taken no line and is neither shut nor struck. About two sea miles off that mole head there are nine hulls this afternoon and nine lay there on the twenty-first of January, and this harbor has not said a word about any of them easing. A man of about twenty-nine hears out of the left side of his head and it is two hundred and fifty-four days today, one more than the day before, no figure is set against it, and he is not on the water.**

---

That cistern went its eleventh trip at about eleven for the hundred and seventy-fifth time and the covers went on at the same hour and lay there until about four, and about five people came for water after four and were served without waiting.

The keeper wrote the charge number and the hours and wrote beside them that a settlement did go into the foot of that sheet today and which settlement.

---

At about eleven the man of about thirty-eight of that landing came in over the bar at about eleven with a sheet in his own hand and it was read at the door of that shed and the hours were written with the day against them.

The hours say that the two sections of that bell which came down on Wednesday were put on a bed at that landing on the Friday afternoon, and that the frame is down to two of nine and that both of those are hung, and that the ninth section is laid on dunnage in a room where nine people work and is not rung and has not been rung since about half past two in the morning of the ninth.

---

At about one the keeper said out loud at the flat of that stone, in about nine people, that a sum which is read out loud after it has gone down is a sum that cannot be stopped, and that this yard reads the parts and the multiplications first so that anybody standing there can say whether the total is the total of the parts.

He said he had learned that in June and not before June, and that a yard which adds a column and shows nobody the column is a yard that has asked to be trusted, and about four of that nine agreed with him and about four said nothing.

---

At about two the woman of thirty of no office said out loud in about nine people that the disagreement between two people in that yard about what a page prints had not gone anywhere in four days and that she was not going to make a thing of it because a thing made of it is how it starts to be settled by whoever is loudest.

The man of about thirty-two said that was fair and that he was not going to say anything else about it today, and about four of that nine said nothing and nobody said either was wrong, and the matter is open and no page in that shed carries a resolution of it.

---

At about four the keeper took that sheet off its nail and laid it flat on the dry end of that stone in front of about nine people and read the four parts with the multiplications and then read the sum, and the thirteenth and the fifteenth were both on the face of that line and had been on it since Saturday.

Two hundred and forty pence of glass and frames. Fifty pence of two seats and a bench. One hundred and twenty pence of a stove and its pipe. Four times four times sixteen for two hundred and fifty-six pence of four men at four days. Two hundred and forty plus fifty plus one hundred and twenty plus two hundred and fifty-six is six hundred and sixty-six pence, being four marks, two shillings and twopence, and the sum went into the foot of that sheet and the foot was read again at the close of the day.

---

The ordinary work of that Monday went on and was not stopped by any of it, being the pump run clean from about nine and the glass front of that case going into its frame at about two and a lighter going out of that quay at about eleven with four men in her.

The sheet with the fourteen gaps at the end stall stood all day with no figure moved by one, the far bench was occupied from ten until four and nothing was put on it, and the man of about sixty-one was not in that yard and was not sent for.

---

Line twelve was settled into that foot at about four and at about half past five on that Monday the whole of that day was put out loud at that stone, in about nine hundred and thirty-one words, to about nine people, and about four of those nine had stood at that flat stone at about four and watched a sum go down into the foot of a sheet.

"**That cistern went its eleventh trip at about eleven for the hundred and seventy-fifth time and the covers went on at the same hour and lay there until about four, and about five people came for water after four and were served without waiting. I wrote the charge number and the hours and wrote beside them that a settlement did go into the foot of that sheet today and which settlement.**"

"**At about eleven the man of about thirty-eight of that landing came in over the bar with a sheet in his own hand and it was read at the door of this shed and the hours were written with the day against them. The hours say that the two sections of that bell which came down on Wednesday were put on a bed at that landing on the Friday afternoon, and that the frame is down to two of nine and that both of those are hung, and that the ninth section is laid on dunnage in a room where nine people work and is not rung and has not been rung since about half past two in the morning of the ninth.**"

"**At about one I said out loud at the flat of this stone, in about nine of us, that a sum which is read out loud after it has gone down is a sum that cannot be stopped, and that this yard reads the parts and the multiplications first so that anybody standing there can say whether the total is the total of the parts. I said I had learned that in June and not before June, and that a yard which adds a column and shows nobody the column is a yard that has asked to be trusted, and about four of that nine agreed with me and about four said nothing.**"

"**At about two the woman of thirty of no office said out loud in about nine of us that the disagreement between two people in this yard about what a page prints had not gone anywhere in four days and that she was not going to make a thing of it because a thing made of it is how it starts to be settled by whoever is loudest. The man of about thirty-two said that was fair and that he was not going to say anything else about it today, and about four of that nine said nothing and nobody said either was wrong, and this is not settled and no page in this shed settles it.**"

"**At about four I took that sheet off its nail and laid it flat on the dry end of this stone in front of about nine of us and read the four parts with the multiplications and then read the sum, and the thirteenth and the fifteenth were both on the face of that line and had been on it since Saturday. Two hundred and forty pence of glass and frames. Fifty pence of two seats and a bench. One hundred and twenty pence of a stove and its pipe. Four times four times sixteen for two hundred and fifty-six pence of four men at four days. Two hundred and forty plus fifty plus one hundred and twenty plus two hundred and fifty-six is six hundred and sixty-six pence, being four marks, two shillings and twopence, and the sum went into the foot of that sheet and the foot was read again at the close of the day.**"

"**And the rest of it. The pump ran clean from about nine and the glass front of that case went into its frame at about two and a lighter went out of this quay at about eleven with four men in her, and nobody in this shed stopped work because of what was said at about one. The sheet with the fourteen gaps stood at that end stall all day with no figure moved by one, and the far bench was occupied from ten until four with nothing put on it, and that man of about sixty-one did not come down to this shed today. The foot of that sheet is read at the close of this day as it stood before the sum went down and stands at one hundred and twenty-one thousand eight hundred and eighty-two pence out and one hundred and eleven thousand two hundred and forty-two pence short, and from tomorrow it stands at one hundred and twenty-two thousand five hundred and forty-eight pence out and one hundred and eleven thousand nine hundred and eight pence short, against ten thousand six hundred and forty pence in. No tin in this shed has been opened since the twenty-ninth of April past and this yard is now one hundred and sixty-nine days without one. Twenty-eight plus two thousand one hundred and eighty-one plus one thousand eight hundred and forty-eight less four thousand and twenty-nine is twenty-eight, and under that slate is twenty-eight pence, being two shillings and fourpence, and that is six hundred and twelve pence short of the wage of four marks, being three marks eleven shillings, with a hundred and sixty-eight pence in at about ten and a hundred and sixty-eight out at about four. About two sea miles off that mole head there are nine hulls this evening and there were nine there on the twenty-first of January. A man of about twenty-nine hears out of the left side of his head and it is two hundred and fifty-four days today and nothing said at about four today touched that figure by a day.**"

Nobody was thanked for the arithmetic and nobody was thanked for a cistern, and that shed stood empty by about six with its door standing open on a slip nobody was on, and that page of that week had thirty lines on it, and that wooden tally slate was still lying flat on that bench stone with nothing on it.

---

**LINE TWELVE SETTLED INTO THE FOOT OF THAT MONEY SHEET AT ABOUT FOUR, BEING SIX HUNDRED AND SIXTY-SIX PENCE IN FOUR PARTS, WITH THE THIRTEENTH AND THE FIFTEENTH BOTH ON THE FACE OF THE LINE. THE FOOT OF THAT SHEET WAS READ AT THE CLOSE OF THIS DAY AS IT STOOD BEFORE THE SUM WENT DOWN AND STEPS FROM TOMORROW. THIS WAS THE HUNDRED AND SEVENTY-FIFTH CHARGE. THE BAG HOLDS TWENTY-EIGHT PENCE, BEING TWO SHILLINGS AND FOURPENCE.**

*First column, entered on Monday the fifteenth day of August at about six in the evening in that shed: that the said day was a rota day and was the hundred and seventy-fifth charge on that cistern and was not a wage Tuesday and was not a wipe, that cistern being charged in eleven trips by hand between about eight and about eleven and walked on by none of it, with the covers lying on it from about eleven until about four and about five people served after four without waiting; and that at about eleven a man of about thirty-eight of that landing came in over the bar with a sheet in his own hand which was read at the door of that shed, the hours being that the two sections of that bell which came down on Wednesday were put on a bed at that landing on the Friday afternoon, that the frame is down to two of nine and both of those are hung, and that the ninth section is laid on dunnage in a room where nine people work and is not rung and has not been rung since about half past two in the morning of the ninth; and that at about one the keeper said out loud in about nine that a sum read out loud after it has gone down is a sum that cannot be stopped, and that this yard reads the parts and the multiplications first so that anybody standing there can say whether the total is the total of the parts; and that at about two the woman of thirty of no office said out loud in about nine that the disagreement between two people in that yard about what a page prints had not gone anywhere in four days and that she was not going to make a thing of it, and that the man of about thirty-two said that was fair and that he was not going to say anything else about it today; and that at about four line twelve settled into the foot of that money sheet with its four parts read and their multiplications read before the sum went down, the face of that line carrying the thirteenth for the day the work was done and the fifteenth for the day it settles, being two hundred and forty pence of glass and frames, fifty pence of two seats and a bench, one hundred and twenty pence of a stove and its pipe and four times four times sixteen for two hundred and fifty-six pence of four men at four days, being six hundred and sixty-six pence; and that the foot of that sheet was read at the close of that day as it stood before the sum went down and steps from the sixteenth.*

*Second column, not checkable from a mole, entered on the said Monday at about six in the evening in that shed, and no name is against it: that at about one the reason the parts are read before the sum is that a sum read first and parts afterwards is a notice, and a notice is a thing a person has to trust, and a yard that has spent sixty days refusing to be trusted should not start on the day it is easiest; that at about two the reason she said she was not going to make a thing of the disagreement is that a disagreement is a working arrangement and not an injury, and the day the two people in a marriage begin treating it as an injury is the day one of them has stopped working on it in public; that at about four the reason the thirteenth and the fifteenth were both read out loud again on the day of the settlement is that a line whose two days are written on it and read on the day the second one lands cannot be moved afterwards by anybody, including the man who wrote it; and that at about four the reason the foot was read again as it stood before the sum went down is that a yard which shows a figure only after it has changed it has nothing on the page for a stranger to compare the change against.*
