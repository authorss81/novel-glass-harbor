# Chapter 723

*Wednesday the Thirteenth Day of July, Not a Rota Day and Not a Wage Tuesday and Not a Wipe, a Sheet of Tin Fixed Under the Seat of That Bell Off the Mole Carrying the Own Figures of a Seizure in the Winter and a Column Headed With the Reason Given at the Time, Read Out Once on the Boards at About Two in Front of About Twenty People of Whom About Eleven Cannot Read It, Eight Bells at the Morning Reading Where Nine Were Due, the Bag at Twenty-Eight Pence, Being Two Shillings and Fourpence, the First Column at Four Hundred and Fifty-Six and the Second at Two Thousand One Hundred and Fifty-Four*

Wednesday the thirteenth day of July, with that market open from about nine and a flat grey morning, a yard below that market with a shed standing in it, and about nine people in that shed at half past five that afternoon.

**Not a rota day and not a wage Tuesday and not a wipe, and no charge put on that cistern and its covers lying on it from nine in the morning until four in the afternoon. A sheet of tin was fixed under the seat of that bell off the mole this morning, being the working bell that answers a bell off a shoal four days down that coast road, and it carries that office's own figures for a seizure in the winter and a column headed with the reason given at the time. It was read out once this afternoon on the boards at the back of that market by a man of about thirty-three who hires hands, in front of about twenty people of whom about eleven cannot read it, and it was not read out a second time. The sheet stands at one hundred and sixteen thousand seven hundred and forty-six pence out and one hundred and six thousand one hundred and six pence short. A hundred and sixty-eight pence came into that yard at about ten and a hundred and sixty-eight went out of it at about four, and under that slate is twenty-eight pence, being two shillings and fourpence, and no tin in that shed has been opened since the twenty-ninth of April past.**

**Nine counters about things that are not being done ran a day on this morning. That notice of the seventeenth of May past stands on its four hundred and twenty-first day. Four hundred and twenty-three days have gone by without anybody asking a bellwright of this harbor, and his chest stays shut. Two objects of nobody's on that crown are into their seven hundred and sixty-first day. That bench under that pump has stood bare for the five hundred and twenty-seventh night. Two lights about a mile off that point have spent three hundred and forty-fifth days with no figure set against them. A six-line sheet at the back of that end stall has reached its three hundredth day. The paper of four lines in Fish Street is on its three hundred and fifteenth. A nine-line sheet with a seal on it in that front street has stood three hundred and thirty-second day. A man of about fifty-one has kept three hundred and sixty-third days of not saying anything to anybody about a page of six names. That third column is empty and unappointed and a boy's hand of twenty-two is at the head of it and his surname is on no page and he is off the water. That fourth column stands at one hundred and twenty-five and has taken no line and is neither shut nor struck. About two sea miles off that mole head lie nine hulls this afternoon and nine lay there on the twenty-first of January, and this harbor has not said a word about any of them easing. A man of about twenty-nine hears out of the left side of his head and it is two hundred and twenty-one days, one more than the day before, no figure is set against it, and he is not on the water.**

---

At the morning reading at about ten on that mole eight bells came in where nine were due, and the ninth came in about eleven minutes late and then came in. The keeper wrote the hours in a column with no names and did not write against the late one, and about four people who were standing there said nothing about it and about four said they had heard it.

That was the first time since this yard began keeping a page of days that a bell on that coast had come in late and this yard had not written a reason against it, and the reason is in the second column.

---

The tin was fixed under the seat of that bell at about eight in the morning by two men of that yard at the third lay, and it is about the width of two hands and it is riveted and it will be there until somebody takes it down. It carries figures in a clean hand and no seal and no name, and beside the figures there is a column headed with the words reason given at the time.

The figures are eleven boats taken off that coast on one day in the winter, being three from a creek, four from a shoal and four from a mole, and the eleven are given as eleven and not as a total of anything else, and the day is given as a day and a month.

---

At about two in the afternoon the man of about thirty-three read it out once on the boards at the back of that market. About twenty people stood there and about eleven of them cannot read it and he read it out in the plain way and did not explain it and did not read it twice.

The figures were eleven boats off that coast, three from that creek, four off a shoal and four off a mole, on one day in the winter. The reason given at the time was that the bells of those three places could not be answered from the seaward side, and that the Keel required one voice at each of them, and that eleven boats were the smallest number that could be held under one voice for one winter.

When he had read that he stopped, and about four people said nothing and about four said the same thing at the same moment, which was that the reason was not wrong, and nobody argued with it and nobody said it was wrong.

---

The three boats off that creek are the Lowfen Reed's own water. The keeper wrote that on the page of that week at about half past two, being the creek's name and the figure of three and the day and the hour, and he wrote it plainly and without a word against anybody.

The woman of thirty of no office had it in both of her books by four o'clock and read it out twice in that room over the market, once from the board and once from her own copy, and the two copies were the same figure and the same words and neither of them is in the other.

About four people said that the man of about forty-seven who laid the flat stroke was in the right of it. About four said that a man who laid a stroke for that reason was still a man who laid a stroke. Nobody said that a stroke was the same thing as the eleven boats and nobody said it was a different thing either.

---

The woman of thirty of no office had it in both of her books and at about half past five on that Wednesday the man who keeps the leaf took the shortfall out loud at that bench stone, in about six hundred and thirty-two words, to about nine people, and about four of those nine had stood on those boards at about two.

"**Eight bells came in at the reading on that mole at about ten this morning where nine were due, and the ninth came in about eleven minutes late and then came in, and I wrote the hours in a column with no names and wrote no reason against it. That is the first time since this yard began keeping a page of days that a bell on this coast has come in late and this yard has written nothing about why, and I did not write which one it was.**

"**A sheet of tin is riveted under the seat of that bell off the mole since about eight this morning and carries that office's own figures for a seizure in the winter and a column headed with the reason given at the time. At about two the man of about thirty-three read it out once on the boards at the back of that market in front of about twenty people of whom about eleven cannot read it. The figures are eleven boats off this coast on one day in the winter, being three off a creek and four off a shoal and four off a mole. The reason given at the time is that the bells of those three places could not be answered from the seaward side and that the Keel required one voice at each of them and that eleven boats was the smallest number that could be held under one voice for one winter. When he had read that he stopped, and about four of those twenty said the reason was not wrong, and nobody argued with it and nobody said it was wrong.**

"**Those three boats are off the Lowfen Reed's own water and I have written the creek's name and the figure of three and the hour on the page of that week without a word against anybody. The woman of thirty of no office had it in both of her books by four o'clock and read it out twice in that room, once from the board and once from her own copy. About four said the man of about forty-seven who laid the stroke was in the right of it and about four said that a man who laid a stroke for that reason is still a man who laid a stroke, and nobody said the two things are the same.**

"**The sheet stands at one hundred and sixteen thousand seven hundred and forty-six pence out and one hundred and six thousand one hundred and six pence short, against ten thousand six hundred and forty pence in. Four hundred and fifty pence is still on that board in the front street. A hundred and sixty-eight pence in at about ten and a hundred and sixty-eight out at about four, and twenty-eight plus one hundred and sixty-eight less one hundred and sixty-eight is twenty-eight, and under that slate is twenty-eight pence, being two shillings and fourpence, and that is six hundred and twelve pence short of the wage of four marks, being three marks eleven shillings. No tin in that shed has been opened since the twenty-ninth of April past and this yard is now one hundred and thirty-six days without one.**

"**And the last of it. That cistern was not charged and its covers lay on it from nine until four. The far bench was occupied from ten until four with nothing put on it. About two sea miles off that mole head there are nine hulls this afternoon and there were nine there on the twenty-first of January. A man of about twenty-nine hears out of the left side of his head and it is two hundred and twenty-one days today and nothing riveted under a bell seat this morning touched it by a day.**"

Nobody was thanked for the arithmetic and nobody was thanked for eleven boats in a winter, and that shed stood empty by about six with its door standing open on a slip nobody was on, and that page of that week had twenty-five lines on it, and that wooden tally slate was still lying flat on that bench stone with nothing on it.

---

**A SHEET OF TIN WAS RIVETED UNDER THE SEAT OF THAT BELL OFF THE MOLE AND ITS FIGURES WERE READ OUT ONCE ON THE BOARDS AT ABOUT TWO IN FRONT OF ABOUT TWENTY PEOPLE, AND THE REASON GIVEN AT THE TIME FOR ELEVEN BOATS TAKEN OFF THIS COAST IN THE WINTER WAS THAT THREE PLACES COULD NOT BE ANSWERED FROM THE SEWARD SIDE. THREE OF THE ELEVEN WERE OFF THE LOWFEN REED'S OWN WATER. NOBODY SAID THE REASON WAS WRONG AND NOBODY ARGUED WITH IT. THE SHEET STANDS AT ONE HUNDRED AND SIXTEEN THOUSAND SEVEN HUNDRED AND FORTY-SIX PENCE OUT AND ONE HUNDRED AND SIX THOUSAND ONE HUNDRED AND SIX PENCE SHORT. THE BAG HOLDS TWENTY-EIGHT PENCE, BEING TWO SHILLINGS AND FOURPENCE.**

---

*First column, entered on Wednesday the thirteenth day of July at about six in the evening in that shed: that the said day was not a rota day and was not a wage Tuesday and was not a wipe, and that no charge was placed on that cistern and that the covers lay on it from nine in the morning until four in the afternoon; and that at the reading on that mole at about ten eight bells came in where nine were due and the ninth came in about eleven minutes late and then came in, with the hours in a column with no names and no reason written against the late one, which is the first time since this yard began keeping a page of days that a bell on this coast has come in late and this yard has written nothing about why; and that a sheet of tin was riveted under the seat of that bell off the mole at about eight, carrying that office's own figures for a seizure in the winter and a column headed with the reason given at the time; and that it was read out once at about two on the boards at the back of that market by a man of about thirty-three who hires hands in front of about twenty people of whom about eleven cannot read it, the figures being eleven boats off that coast on one day in the winter, three off a creek and four off a shoal and four off a mole, and the reason given at the time being that the bells of those three places could not be answered from the seaward side and that the Keel required one voice at each of them; and that three of the eleven were off the Lowfen Reed's own water, and that the creek's name and the figure of three and the hour stand on that page without a word against anybody.*

*Second column, not checkable from a mole, entered on the said Wednesday at about six in the evening in that shed, and no name is against it: that at about ten in the morning the reason no reason is written against a bell that came in late is that the first time this yard writes why a bell was late is the first time this yard has decided what a bell should be doing, and a page of times that this yard grades is a page of times this yard owns; that at about two in the afternoon the reason the reason given at the time was read out with the figures and not after them is that a figure on a board with no reason on it is a complaint, and a reason read after a complaint is an answer, and about twenty people would have heard the answer before they heard the complaint; that at about half past three the reason this yard did not argue with that reason is that the reason is true about the sea and is on the tin of the office that took the boats and was written before anybody on this coast had anything to do with it; and that at about four the reason both books carry the creek's name and the figure of three is that a figure which exists in one book on this coast exists once, and once is a number anybody can arrive at by different roads.*
