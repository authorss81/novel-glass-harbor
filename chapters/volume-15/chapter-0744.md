# Chapter 744

*Wednesday the Third Day of August, the Room at Orison Given Its Name in That Yard's Book for the First Time This Week, a Man of About Fifty-Six Put Into the Ninth Bell on a Line in About Nineteen Fathoms Without Being Asked and Without Anybody in That Room Being Able to Stop It, a Fixed Air Limit of Twenty-Five Minutes' Worth of Capacity Written on a Page and Not Met and No Figure of Money Against Any Part of It, the Sheet at One Hundred and Eighteen Thousand Seven Hundred and Eighty-Four Pence Out, the Bag at Twenty-Eight Pence, the First Column at Four Hundred and Seventy-Seven and the Second at Two Thousand Two Hundred and Fifty-Nine*

Wednesday the third day of August, with that market opening late in that town and a hard bright morning on the water off Orison, the yard's boat coming to the Orison landing at about ten in the morning with nine men in her, and about nine people in the room beside that bell at half past five that evening.

**Not a rota day and not a wage Tuesday and not a wipe, and no charge put on that cistern in that harbor, whose covers lay on it from nine in the morning until four in the afternoon, and that cistern is two days of water and a night from this landing. A man of about fifty-six, thin, who dries his hands before he touches any glass, was put into the ninth bell on a line in about nineteen fathoms off that landing at about eleven in the morning without being asked, and nobody in that room was able to stop it, and it was a forced held line. The reason was written down in the hand of the man who keeps the leaf at about half past ten on the face of the landing with the hour against it and it carries no consent on its face because none was given. A fixed air limit of twenty-five minutes' worth of capacity stood on the page from the moment the line went out and was not met, and no figure of money stands against any hour of it. The sheet stands at one hundred and eighteen thousand seven hundred and eighty-four pence out and one hundred and eight thousand one hundred and forty-four pence short, against ten thousand six hundred and forty pence in. A hundred and sixty-eight pence came into that yard at about ten and a hundred and sixty-eight went out of it at about four, and under that slate is twenty-eight pence, being two shillings and fourpence, and no tin in that shed has been opened since the twenty-ninth of April past.**

**Nine days of things that are not being done went on a day in the morning, and this is what they stood at. That notice of the seventeenth of May past is on its four hundred and forty-second day. Four hundred and forty-four days have gone by without anybody asking a bellwright of that harbor a question, and his chest is shut and stays shut. Two objects belonging to nobody on that crown are into the seven hundred and eighty-second day of themselves. That bench under that pump has now stood bare for the five hundred and forty-eighth night running. Two lights about a mile off that point have gone three hundred and sixty-sixth days with nothing set against them. A six-line sheet at the back of that end stall has reached the three hundred and twenty-first day of its standing. The paper of four lines in Fish Street is on the three hundred and thirty-sixth. A nine-line sheet with a seal on it in that front street has stood three hundred and fifty-third day. And a man of about fifty-one has now kept three hundred and eighty-fourth days of saying nothing to anybody about a page of six names. That third column is empty and unappointed with a boy's hand of twenty-two at the head of it, his surname is on no page, and he is off the water. That fourth column stands at one hundred and twenty-five and has taken no line and is neither shut nor struck. About two sea miles off that mole head in that other harbor lie nine hulls this afternoon and nine lay there on the twenty-first of January, and that harbor has not said a word about any of them easing. A man of about twenty-nine hears out of the left side of his head and it is two hundred and forty-two days, one more than the day before, no figure is set against it, and he is not on the water.**

---

The boat lay against the Orison landing at about ten with her warp on the bollard and the tender gear still made fast amidships, and the man of about thirty-eight who keeps the line of that bell met the nine of them on the stones and took the warp himself without being asked.

---

The bell stands on a frame of iron on the headland above the landing and it is a bell and not a building and there is a room beside it with a stove in it and a bench and a window facing the water. About nine people standing in that room can see the frame through the window and can touch the bell rope where it comes through the wall, and a man who works there can tell them the rest of it.

---

The keeper wrote the name in that yard's book at about ten in the morning with the hour against it, being that the room beside the bell on that headland is the Ninth Bell, and that the name is the name at the head of the sheet that came down that coast road on Monday, and that this yard's book had not carried it before this week and had carried nothing else for that place either.

---

The bell had been run in three days out of the last four and the man of about thirty-eight said the answers under it had been going wrong at the same hour twice, and that when the answers went wrong at the same hour twice the whole of the chain above it began to come apart in about a day, and that this had happened on the eleventh of May and on the second of April and on nothing else anybody can remember.

---

The man of about fifty-six was in the water off that landing when the boat came in, and he had been in the water since the Saturday. He is thin and about fifty-six and he dried his hands on his own coat before he touched the frame of that bell, and he had a line of about forty yards of new line through his harness and the other end of it on the ring at the top of the frame, and he said out loud, to nobody in particular, that it was the only arrangement of its kind that had ever held for him, in the past tense, and that it would hold again in the past tense.

---

At about half past ten the keeper wrote the reason down with the hour against it on the face of that landing in his own hand, being one person already on the line, held by the woman of about thirty-three with a watch on the surface against twenty-five minutes worth of capacity with a cutoff she can pull without asking, one person at a time and nobody twice in a day and nobody at the top of a flood and an ascent asked for not argued with, and he wrote on the same page that no consent had been asked for and none had been given and that the page was a record of what was being done and not a permission.

---

At about eleven the man of about fifty-six said out loud from the water, through the bell and not into the room, that if the ninth bell was answered by nine things it would be nine disagreements, and that a disagreement in the chain above it was a fracture, and that a fracture in that chain is not a thing anybody standing in that room has seen the end of, and that no figure of one is kept on this coast. About nine people in that room heard him and none of them was asked anything and none of them answered him.

---

He put his memory into the bell at about ten past eleven and nobody in that room was able to stop it, because the room is above the water and the man was in the water and the thing that was done is not done to a room. The chain of the mechanism came steady within about a quarter of an hour, and the man of about thirty-eight said in that room that in three days of answering wrongly the chain had not gone one step toward being whole, and that it had stopped going wrong instead, and that those are not the same and he had no figure for the difference.

---

The woman of about thirty-three stood on the surface deck of the boat with the independent line in her own hand and her other hand on the cutoff and the watch where she could see it, and the limit of twenty-five minutes worth of capacity came and went twice on the page. She did not pull the cutoff, and the keeper wrote that she did not pull it, and wrote that the reason was that a hand on a cutoff is a hand on a cutoff and that a cutoff pulled at a moment nobody asked for is a decision made in place of the man on the line.

---

The man of about thirty-two said at about half past twelve in that room, to about nine people, that the whole of the argument he had been having with his wife since June went the other way on a page at Orison than it did at a market, and that here the page is the dangerous thing and there the page is the only thing anybody has, and that he was not going to pretend to know which of those two statements was the true one, and nobody said it was.

---

At about one the man of about thirty-eight was asked, by him, one thing, being whether the ninth bell could be run for a day with nothing on the line behind it, and the man of about thirty-eight said that in about four months of keeping that line it had never been done, and that nobody knew what the bell did with the hours, and that he was not going to find out on a working day with nine people standing in his room.

---

The cost came in the same hour in the same place and not one hour later than it did. The man of about fifty-six was asked by the man of about thirty-eight, in that room, what the bell had answered at the fourth hour, and he gave an answer, and then he said that he could not tell whether he had remembered that or had been given it, and the man of about thirty-eight wrote the words out in full and did not shorten them, and no figure of money stands against the writing of them.

---

At about two the man of about thirty-two wrote one question up that coast road in his own hand with the hour against it, addressed to the woman of thirty of no office, being whether a person who has put himself into a thing without being asked may be taken out of it by the people standing round it, and he put nothing else on the page and no reason and no name of the man on the line. He gave the page to the man of about thirty-eight and the man of about thirty-eight said it would be walked up that road in the morning.

---

Nobody put the same words to the man of about thirty-two this day about going down, and nobody put them to him on that landing, and the page of days carries no hour against his name and no sum is set against it on any sheet. The column headed with the asking was summed on the Friday the twenty-ninth of July and is not summed again on any day that follows it, and no figure is set against that column on this day.

---

The disagreement between the two of them was not in that room, because one of them was two days of water and a night away with one wet road between, keeping a second book on a shelf, and one wet road takes one book. What came up that coast road on that page was a disagreement in her own hand with the hour against it, and it was not settled, and no page in that yard carries a resolution of it.

---

At about half past five on that Wednesday the reading went out in that room at Orison in about eleven hundred and sixty-nine words to about nine people, and about four of those nine had been in that room since about ten, and the money sheet was not in that room and was not read in it.

The bell stands on an iron frame on the headland and it is a bell and not a building, and the room beside it has a stove and a bench and a window on the water, and about nine of us standing in it can see the frame through that window and can touch the rope where it comes through the wall, and the man who works there can tell the rest of it. I wrote the name in that yard's book at about ten with the hour against it, being that the room beside that bell is the Ninth Bell, and that the name is the name at the head of the sheet that came down that road on Monday, and that this yard's book had not carried it before this week.**"

"**The man of about thirty-two wrote one question up this coast road at about two in his own hand with the hour against it, and gave it to the man of about thirty-eight to be walked up in the morning, and the answer came back in her own hand and it is not settled and no page here settles it. Nobody put words to the man of about thirty-two about going down today and nobody put them to him on that landing, and that column was summed on the twenty-ninth of July and is not summed again on any day after it.**"

"**And the last of it, and it is read here and not in that market. The sheet stands at one hundred and eighteen thousand seven hundred and eighty-four pence out and one hundred and eight thousand one hundred and forty-four pence short, against ten thousand six hundred and forty pence in, and that sheet is not in this room and was not read in it today. A hundred and sixty-eight pence went into that yard at about ten and a hundred and sixty-eight came out of it at about four, and twenty-eight plus two thousand one hundred and eighty-one plus one thousand eight hundred and forty-eight less four thousand and twenty-nine is twenty-eight, and under that slate is twenty-eight pence, being two shillings and fourpence, and that is six hundred and twelve pence short of the wage of four marks, being three marks eleven shillings. No tin in that shed has been opened since the twenty-ninth of April past and that yard is now one hundred and fifty-seven days without one. A man of about twenty-nine hears out of the left side of his head and it is two hundred and forty-two days today and nothing that was done in this room touched it by a day.**"

Nobody was thanked for the arithmetic and nobody was thanked for a man put into a bell, and that room stood empty by about six with its window open on the frame, and that page of that week had twenty-nine lines on it, and that wooden tally slate was still lying flat on that bench stone with nothing on it.

THE ROOM BESIDE THAT BELL IS THE NINTH BELL AND THIS YARD'S BOOK DID NOT CARRY THAT NAME BEFORE THIS WEEK. THE SHEET STANDS AT ONE HUNDRED AND EIGHTEEN THOUSAND SEVEN HUNDRED AND EIGHTY-FOUR PENCE OUT AND ONE HUNDRED AND EIGHT THOUSAND ONE HUNDRED AND FORTY-FOUR PENCE SHORT. THE BAG HOLDS TWENTY-EIGHT PENCE, BEING TWO SHILLINGS AND FOURPENCE.**
