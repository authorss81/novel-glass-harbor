# Chapter 729

*Tuesday the Nineteenth Day of July, the Ninetieth Wage Tuesday With the Lid at Twenty-Six Lines and One Hundred and Four Marks Unmoved and the Chest Shut From Ten Until Four, Line Eight on That Sheet Worked This Day and Settled on the Twenty-First, Being Nine Hundred and Ninety Pence in Five Parts, the Bag at Twenty-Eight Pence, Being Two Shillings and Fourpence, the First Column at Four Hundred and Sixty-Two and the Second at Two Thousand One Hundred and Eighty-Four*

Tuesday the nineteenth day of July, with that market open from about nine and a close heat over the lane by noon, a yard below that market with a shed standing in it, and about nine people in that shed at half past five that afternoon.

**A wage Tuesday and not a rota day and not a wipe, being the ninetieth, with the lid read at about ten in four minutes by a man of about thirty-one of the second lay at twenty-six lines and one hundred and four marks unmoved, and the chest with the twenty envelopes in it shut from ten until four, and a further week standing fallen due at its close. No charge was put on that cistern and its covers lay on it from nine until four. Line eight went onto that sheet this day, worked the nineteenth of July and settled the twenty-first, being nine hundred and ninety pence in five parts, and the keeper said the day out loud before he wrote a figure. The sheet stands at one hundred and sixteen thousand seven hundred and forty-six pence out and one hundred and six thousand one hundred and six pence short, against ten thousand six hundred and forty pence in. A hundred and sixty-eight pence came into that yard at about ten and a hundred and sixty-eight went out of it at about four, and two thousand one hundred and eighty-one pence came into that yard in the running of the thirteen days and two thousand one hundred and eighty-one went out of it, and twenty-eight plus two thousand one hundred and eighty-one less two thousand one hundred and eighty-one is twenty-eight, and under that slate is twenty-eight pence, being two shillings and fourpence, and no tin in that shed has been opened since the twenty-ninth of April past, this yard now one hundred and forty-two days without one.**

**Nine counters about things that are not being done ran a day on this morning. That notice of the seventeenth of May past stands on its four hundred and twenty-seventh day. Four hundred and twenty-nine days have gone by without anybody asking a bellwright of this harbor, and his chest stays shut. Two objects of nobody's on that crown are into their seven hundred and sixty-seventh day. That bench under that pump has stood bare for the five hundred and thirty-third night. Two lights about a mile off that point have spent three hundred and fifty-first days with no figure set against them. A six-line sheet at the back of that end stall has reached its three hundred and sixth day. The paper of four lines in Fish Street is on its three hundred and twenty-first. A nine-line sheet with a seal on it in that front street has stood three hundred and thirty-eighth day. A man of about fifty-one has kept three hundred and sixty-ninth days of not saying anything to anybody about a page of six names. That third column is empty and unappointed and a boy's hand of twenty-two is at the head of it and his surname is on no page and he is off the water. That fourth column stands at one hundred and twenty-five and has taken no line and is neither shut nor struck. About two sea miles off that mole head lie nine hulls this afternoon and nine lay there on the twenty-first of January, and this harbor has not said a word about any of them easing. A man of about twenty-nine hears out of the left side of his head and it is two hundred and twenty-seven days, one more than the day before, no figure is set against it, and he is not on the water.**

---

The lid was read at about ten in four minutes by the man of about thirty-one of the second lay, with about nine people standing around that step and none of them touching the chest. He read twenty-six lines and one hundred and four marks and said the column does not move, and he shut the chest and left it shut from ten until four with the twenty envelopes inside.

---

No work was taken out of that chest and no envelope was opened between ten and four, and the far bench was occupied from ten until four with mending and nothing on paper. At about eleven the boy of twenty-two carried two buckets to the butt and set them down and did not draw, because the covers lay on that cistern and the butt water was for drinking only.

---

At about noon the keeper took the fitting-out sheet off its nail and laid it on the dry end of that bench stone for line eight. He said out loud in about nine people that the day of the work was Tuesday the nineteenth day of July, and he waited while the woman of thirty wrote the day on a scrap and held it up. He said the day of settlement would be Thursday the twenty-first, and he waited while she wrote that beside the first, and only then did he take up the pencil.

---

He wrote ninety yards of line at threepence the yard, being ninety times three and two hundred and seventy pence, and he read it out as he wrote it. About four people watched the yards run along the stone and counted them on their fingers and about four watched the pencil, and the woman with a watch nodded once when the two hundred and seventy was set down. The line was the good length coiled back on the pin yesterday after the soft place was cut out, with the red-marked cut length still hanging on the nail by the door.

---

He wrote two bell seats at eighteenpence the piece, being two times eighteen and thirty-six pence, and he read it out as he wrote it. The seats were the two iron shoes for the bell off that mole and the bell off the shoal, cleaned of rust and reseated with new bolts.

---

He wrote the watch for the tender with the independent line at sixty pence, being one watch and one line and sixty pence together, and he read it out as he wrote it. The woman of about thirty-three stood beside the stone while this figure went on, with her coil hung on its pin and the cutoff where it could be reached without stooping. She said nothing while the pencil moved, and when the sixty was set down she put her hand on the coil and took it off again.

---

He wrote nine men at two days at sixteenpence the man-day, being nine times two times sixteen and two hundred and eighty-eight pence, and he read the multiplication out as he wrote it. The nine were the hands who had gone over pump and line and boat on Monday, two days each at the harbor figure.

---

He wrote two days of boat at one hundred and sixty-eight pence the day, being two times one hundred and sixty-eight and three hundred and thirty-six pence, and he read it out as he wrote it. The boat was the one hauled alongside yesterday with the loose thwart wedged and nailed and the sail left bent, lying now off the slip with her cover on. When the five parts were down he added them aloud, being two hundred and seventy and thirty-six and sixty and two hundred and eighty-eight and three hundred and thirty-six, and nine hundred and ninety pence was the sum, being six marks two shillings and sixpence, being six times one hundred and sixty and two times twelve and six.

---

The disagreement between the two of them was in the shed at about four without being pressed. He said a line of nine hundred and ninety pence written in five parts is still a line that leans on a descent, and that two books carrying it twice will carry the lean twice. She said what goes into both is what she was told and never a name against a person, and that the same sum written twice by one hand is one sum in two places. He said he knew what he had objected to in June, and she said she knew that too and was keeping the second book all the same, and about four looked at the sheet and about four looked at the door, and the matter stayed open and no page of these days carries a resolution of it.

---

The Lowfen Reed lay dormant through the whole of the day and nobody came down that coast road with a new time and nobody sent one up by a cart. The second book stayed dry on the shelf above that butt and was opened once at about one to take the five parts before they went onto the sheet, and the first with the cockled corner was opened beside it for checking. The bell off that mole answered the bell off the shoal at about three in hours and not in names, and the nine hulls lay off that mole head in the evening haze without a word said about any of them easing.

---

At about half past five on that Tuesday the shortfall went out loud at that bench stone in about nine hundred words to about nine people, four of whom had heard the lid read at about ten in the morning.

"**The Tuesday lid was read at about ten in four minutes at twenty-six lines and one hundred and four marks unmoved, and that chest with twenty envelopes inside is shut from ten until four and no envelope was opened. A further week stands fallen due at the close of this Tuesday and the column does not move on any of the eight. That cistern was not charged and its covers lay on it from nine until four, and the butt water was for drinking only.**

"**At about noon I took that sheet off its nail for line eight and said the day out loud before I wrote a figure, being Tuesday the nineteenth day of July for the work and Thursday the twenty-first for the settlement, and the day was written on a scrap and held up first. Ninety yards of line at threepence the yard, being ninety times three and two hundred and seventy pence, and two bell seats at eighteenpence, being two times eighteen and thirty-six pence, and the watch for the tender with the independent line at sixty pence together.**

"**Nine men at two days at sixteenpence the man-day, being nine times two times sixteen and two hundred and eighty-eight pence, and two days of boat at one hundred and sixty-eight pence the day, being two times one hundred and sixty-eight and three hundred and thirty-six pence. Two hundred and seventy and thirty-six and sixty and two hundred and eighty-eight and three hundred and thirty-six is nine hundred and ninety pence, being six marks two shillings and sixpence, being six times one hundred and sixty and two times twelve and six, and line eight carries those five multiplications on its face with both days.**

"**The disagreement between the two of them was here again at about four without being pressed to an end, and the matter stays open and no page of these days settles it. The Lowfen Reed lay dormant and nobody came down that road with a new time, and the second book stayed dry and was opened once at one to take the five parts before they went on.**

"**The sheet stands at one hundred and sixteen thousand seven hundred and forty-six pence out and one hundred and six thousand one hundred and six pence short, against ten thousand six hundred and forty pence in, being sixty-six marks, six shillings and eightpence. Four hundred and fifty pence is still on that board in the front street. Two thousand one hundred and eighty-one pence in and two thousand one hundred and eighty-one out in the running of these thirteen days, and twenty-eight plus two thousand one hundred and eighty-one less two thousand one hundred and eighty-one is twenty-eight, and under that slate is twenty-eight pence, being two shillings and fourpence, and that is six hundred and twelve pence short of the wage of four marks, being three marks eleven shillings. No tin in that shed has been opened since the twenty-ninth of April past and this yard is now one hundred and forty-two days without one. About two sea miles off that mole head there are nine hulls this afternoon and there were nine there on the twenty-first of January. A man of about twenty-nine hears out of the left side of his head and it is two hundred and twenty-seven days today.**"

Nobody was thanked for the arithmetic and nobody was thanked for saying the day out loud before writing, and that shed stood empty by about six with its door standing open on a lane nobody was on, and that page of that week had thirty-one lines on it, and that wooden tally slate was still lying flat on that bench stone with nothing on it.

---

**THE NINETIETH WAGE TUESDAY WITH THE LID AT TWENTY-SIX LINES AND ONE HUNDRED AND FOUR MARKS UNMOVED AND THE CHEST SHUT FROM TEN UNTIL FOUR. LINE EIGHT ON THAT SHEET WORKED THE NINETEENTH AND SETTLED THE TWENTY-FIRST, BEING NINETY YARDS AT THREEPENCE FOR TWO HUNDRED AND SEVENTY AND TWO SEATS AT EIGHTEENPENCE FOR THIRTY-SIX AND THE WATCH AT SIXTY AND NINE MEN AT TWO DAYS AT SIXTEENPENCE FOR TWO HUNDRED AND EIGHTY-EIGHT AND TWO DAYS OF BOAT FOR THREE HUNDRED AND THIRTY-SIX, BEING NINE HUNDRED AND NINETY PENCE IN SUM. THE DISAGREEMENT STAYS OPEN AND THE LOWFEN REED LAY DORMANT. THE SHEET STANDS AT ONE HUNDRED AND SIXTEEN THOUSAND SEVEN HUNDRED AND FORTY-SIX PENCE OUT AND ONE HUNDRED AND SIX THOUSAND ONE HUNDRED AND SIX PENCE SHORT. THE BAG HOLDS TWENTY-EIGHT PENCE, BEING TWO SHILLINGS AND FOURPENCE.**

---

---

*First column, entered on Tuesday the nineteenth day of July at about six in the evening in that shed: that the said day was the ninetieth wage Tuesday and was not a rota day and was not a wipe, with the lid read at ten in four minutes at twenty-six lines and one hundred and four marks unmoved and the chest with twenty envelopes shut from ten until four; and that no charge was placed on that cistern and the covers lay on it from nine until four; and that line eight went onto that sheet, worked the nineteenth and settled the twenty-first, being ninety yards at threepence for two hundred and seventy and two seats at eighteenpence for thirty-six and the watch at sixty and nine men at two days at sixteenpence for two hundred and eighty-eight and two days of boat at three hundred and thirty-six, being nine hundred and ninety in sum; and that the keeper said the day out loud before he wrote a figure; and that the disagreement between the two of them was in that shed at four and stays open; and that the Lowfen Reed lay dormant and nobody came down that coast road with a new time.*

*Second column, not checkable from a mole, entered on the said Tuesday at about six in the evening in that shed, and no name is against it: that at about ten the reason the lid was read in four minutes and no longer is that a wage reading spun out teaches nine people to listen for tone, and tone is not a figure and this yard keeps figures; that at about noon the reason the day was said out loud before any figure is that a line carrying two days can be mislaid between them, and a day spoken in nine people holds the pencil to the right leaf; that at about four the reason the five multiplications were read as they were written is that a sum of nine hundred and ninety with no parts shown is a sum nine people must take on trust, and parts shown plain let trust rest on looking; and that at about five the reason the dormant road was written with no reason against it is that a Tuesday with a lid reading and a line going on is full enough to make quiet ordinary, and quiet on a full day is not a message for the page to carry.*
