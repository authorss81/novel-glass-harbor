# Chapter 169

*What a Refusal Became in a City*

Wednesday the twenty-seventh and Thursday the twenty-eighth of September, in a doorway, at the head of the dock, and in the shed.

**Wil Tranter came up the hill on Wednesday the twenty-seventh at about eleven in the morning with a letter in a coat pocket, and he came up the hill having not written to this party, and he said so before he said anything else.**

"**I wrote to him on Monday night. I told him what you said to me, as near as I could get it, and I told him I had come down the hill and thought about it and I was going to put his line down with the yard declining to certify and nothing else. I did not send you a copy and I am not going to apologize for that, because if I had sent you a copy you would have written him a letter and this would have been a different thing and I do not want it to be a different thing.

"**And he wrote back on Tuesday night and it came up on the Wednesday with the morning boat, and I have read it four times and I have not been able to get round one sentence of it, and I have brought it up here to be got round by two people instead of one.**"

He read it out in the doorway, standing, with the wind coming in past him.

> *Sir, —*
>
> *I have your letter of the twenty-fifth. I am obliged to act upon it and not upon the sense of it.*
>
> *You write that the yard declines to certify. I accept that. I then find that I have nothing to set at the foot of the line.*
>
> *A finding has to end somewhere. If the owner will not swear to his ground, the ground is either proved by somebody else or it is not proved, and there is no third place to leave it in, and I have looked for one for two days.*
>
> *I have therefore entered this: —*
>
> *The yard of Morrow Quay, whose sheet is set in type in this city, gives the bar of that harbor at two and three-quarter fathoms on the flood; declines to certify the figure; and will not be held to it.*
>
> *I would be obliged if you would tell me whether that last clause is the yard's own words. It is not in your letter. I have entered it because a man who will not certify a figure and will not be held to it is a man who is standing behind it and does not wish to be examined, and that is a different kind of man from a man who has nothing, and the difference between those two men is worth money to a man in an office in one direction and not in the other, and I would rather I put it down and was wrong than leave it off and be taken apart over it in a room I have not been in.*
>
> *I am, Sir, your obedient servant,*
> *Ailsa Rorke, surveyor.*

**Nobody in that yard said anything for about half a minute.**

"**He has made it up,**" said Wil Tranter. "**That is the thing I cannot get round. He has written down the words *will not be held to it* and I did not say them and you did not say them, and he has got them in his own hand in a city and it is going into a thing in October with that sentence in it, and about four hundred people are going to read that a yard of Morrow Quay stands behind a figure and does not want to be examined.

"**And you know what the answer is. It is that you have not got anybody in a city who can say *the yard of Morrow Quay declines to certify its figure and is not standing behind it and does not mind being examined.* You have got a wall and a sheet and a man who will not put his name under a number. And a wall cannot answer a question, because that is the one thing a wall is not.**"

---

**And Calder Marr said the thing that had been sitting at the back of the fortnight, and he said it slowly, and it is the first time since the second of May that he has said anything he had not written down first.**

"**Then there is a thing I have got wrong and I have got it wrong out loud, so write it down whatever you write, and it is this.

"**Since the third of May I have been telling this harbor that a party which publishes itself is a party somebody can ask a question of. And in July I said out loud that a wall worth reading is a wall somebody can be brought up in front of and made to answer. And Trewin stood on my own steps in August and said the same thing and I told him no, in the open air, and I put that on the face of the sheet of the twenty-fifth of August in about sixty boats.

"**And the truth is that a refusal is a sentence in a market, and this yard has spent five months learning how to publish one and is now on the receiving end of one, and the man it got published by is a surveyor in a city who has a street and a number and no reason on this coast to be careful with a phrase, and he has put four words of ours into a sentence and the four words are not ours.

"**And I do not get to be sorry about it, and I do not get to write to him, and I do not get to tell him what we said, because a party that publishes itself is not a party that gets to be sorry in a street and mean it, and I am not going to go up a hill in a city I have never seen and ask a man to take four words out of a page.

"**So we are going to be read wrong, and we are going to be read wrong out loud, in front of a man who crossed the water to tell us so, and I would like it noticed that he is the one who came.**"

"**Then it is a fair thing to be sent,**" said Wil Tranter. "**You may as well have the rest of it.**

"**I asked you in a doorway on Friday whether one figure on a printed sheet could be checked by anybody, and you said come back on it, and I have come back on it, and here is the rest of it, and I have not slept much and I am not sorry I came.

"**There is one kind of answer in a city that ends a sentence, and it is not a refusal, and it is not a wall, and it is a name. A man with a page cannot act on a refusal. He can act on a name, because a name is a thing that can be written down in a list of people and asked for at a later date, and a refusal is a thing he has to argue about for four years and then put in a drawer.

"**And there are three people on this coast who can put a trade at the bottom of a line and say what a thing is. There are two men in this town who do it for money, and there is one person who does it for nothing and who is owed forty marks first after food by this harbor's own work since the fifteenth of October, and she is not on your crew, and she is not of your yard, and you are not going to pay her, and you have said out loud in a market office in this town that if you are ever asked to you are going to say no.

"**I am not telling you to go and ask her. I am telling you why a city is going to go on reading this yard as a man standing behind a number it will not be examined about, and it is not because you are a liar. It is because there is nobody in your name at all.**"

---

**And Nessa Pike said one thing about the two figures, in the yard, on the Wednesday afternoon, and it is in the book and it was said once and nobody has said it since and none of them is going to.**

"**Now. Two figures on one bar.

"**Ours is in print in a city and it cannot be un-printed, and that is a fact about a press and not about water. His is two and a half, made four times in nineteen years by a man with a boat and two men and no harbor on her anywhere, and that is a fact about a man and not about a press. One of those two numbers is the depth of that bar at the middle of a spring tide at twenty past two on the flood, and I do not know which one it is and neither does anybody in this harbor and neither does anybody in the city, and both of those are true and neither of them cancels the other.

"**And I am not going to say that again in a shed for about four years, and I am not going to have either of you improve on it in a market, and if either of you says it before I do in a place where there are people I will know, and I am not going to tell you how I will know.**"

Nobody argued with her about that, which is what happens.

---

*First column, checkable by any man who can get off a mole in this harbor: that on Wednesday the twenty-seventh of September instant a letter came into this harbor in the ordinary way from a marine surveyor of a city, whose name is Ailsa Rorke and whose letterhead carries a street and a number and nothing else, and that the man of the first lay named Wil Tranter brought it up this hill and read it out in the doorway; and that this party did not write to that city, did not send that man any paper, and has entered that it is not going to, and the reason is on the page: that a party which has published itself is not a party that gets to be sorry in a street and mean it, and that a party which cannot correct a sentence in a file in a city is not going to get a man to do it for it.*

*And that the said letter records an entry in which that surveyor has set down that this yard gives the bar at two and three-quarters of a fathom on the flood, declines to certify the figure, and will not be held to it; and that this party did not use the words *will not be held to it* at any time in the month of September or at any time in the four months before it, and that the said man of the first lay states in his own hand that he did not use them in his letter of Monday the twenty-fifth instant, and that the said surveyor states in his own letter that the clause is not in the letter he received and that he entered it because a man who will not certify and will not be held to is a man standing behind a figure and does not wish to be examined; and that this party has entered that the said surveyor is a working man in his trade, that he has written down the reason he entered what he entered, that he offered to be wrong in a document rather than vague in one, and that this party is not going to put a name on a man who has put his name on a street.*

*And that this party has entered against itself, with the date and the hour, that a refusal given in a doorway in about eleven sentences on Monday the twenty-fifth instant has become in a city on the twenty-seventh a statement that this yard stands behind a figure and will not be examined about it, and that the four words that made the difference were not said by this party; and that the reason the whole of it is in the first column and not the second is that any man in this harbor may come up this hill and ask this party whether it has written to a city and been told to come back, and can go and look, and the answer is no.*

*Second column, not checkable from a mole, entered on Wednesday the twenty-seventh of September instant at about half past nine in the evening in this shed, and no name is against it: that the holder of the writ of the first of May entered on the twenty-third of August in the first column that this party has heard a man of fifty-four say that a wall worth reading is a wall somebody can be brought up in front of, and has not agreed with him, and has chosen a market, and is not going to explain itself out of that; and that on the twenty-seventh instant he entered against himself that the position is that a wall can be asked a question of and that a refusal cannot be corrected by anybody who is not its author, and that both of those are true, and that the party does not know which of them is going to cost more in October and has not asked and is not going to; and that the reason this line is in this column and not the first is that a man in a boat can check what he said in a doorway and cannot check what a stranger in a city has done with it in three days.*

**And on Thursday the twenty-eighth, which is a rota day, the cistern behind the engine house was charged at no figure by strangers for the fourth time in a fortnight and this party did not walk it, and it has held since the nineteenth of June. No money moved on the Wednesday and none on the Thursday. A mark and twelve shillings and fourpence is in the bag and is the whole of it. Thirty-one on the note, not re-written, its three counts unmoved since the fourth of January. Five marks of compressor's gland standing on it, unpaid, and of no use whatever to this yard because the thing after the gland is the hose. Nothing in a tin since the twenty-eighth of April. Eight marks and a day of a boat to this harbor's bellwright since the twenty-third of May, unargued, and the chest was not opened this month and is not going to be. Seven pence to this yard's own shipwright. Three marks to a man of Ashtide, unmoved since the eighth of August, and he is not named in that book again. Forty marks standing whole to a boy of twenty-two who cannot do the thing he was kept for, and the third column of that book has not been filled since the first of August and nobody has been appointed and a column nobody fills is a column somebody else fills. Forty marks to a person of no office, first after food, unpaid since the fifteenth of October. The light is not on. The north berth is out. The tide gang is four men short. The hard at Ashtide is unanswered. Nineteen winters are in a naval file and in a list being made up in a city by an officer this yard emptied, and nobody in Morrow Quay will ever be able to check what she writes with them.**
