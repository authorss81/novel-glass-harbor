# Chapter 161

*The Nineteenth Monday, and a Wage That Does Not Go In*

Monday the eleventh and Tuesday the twelfth of September, on the mole's steps, at the head of the dock, and in the shed.

**The wall behind the reeve's bench came down at eight on Monday morning and it came down bare, and that was nineteen Mondays since the first of May, and the clerk kept his own page and did not mention it.**

This party has not put anything on that wall in five months. It has entered twice that it is not going to and it is not going to start on the nineteenth. Up on the crown above the third seam's mark the two objects of nobody's were under their tarpaulins about a hundred and seventeen days, and one of the tarpaulins still has a corner off it since the seventeenth of June, and neither of them moved on the Monday and neither has moved in a hundred and seventeen days and this party went past them at about nine in the morning on its way to the steps and looked at both of them and touched neither.

The mole head's light was not on behind them and was not going to be, because a light costs oil and the repair account of the office that owns that light has been empty since the middle of February.

---

**And then the whole of the morning went into the shed with a slate, and the slate stayed on the bench from then until the lamp was lit and it was not put away once in between.**

A mark and twelve shillings and fourpence in a bag. That is three hundred and eight pence, and Calder Marr wrote the pence on the slate because it is the only figure of this yard that can be wrong by being written down wrong, and he wrote it twice and got it the same both times.

"**Four marks on Tuesday,**" said Nessa Pike.

"**Four marks on Tuesday.**

"**And on the second of May, when this yard made a wage of four marks a week, what did a day at a seam bring.**

"**It has brought three. It brought five twice in August. It brought seven on the twenty-fourth of June and that is the last time anything this yard has done on a face has been worth more than five. Three is the figure and five is the figure and seven is a figure from June that I would not know what to do with if it came again tomorrow.**"

"**So say the whole of it out loud, because you are going to be asked it out loud in a fortnight and I would rather you said it in here first where I can argue with you.**"

He said the whole of it out loud. It took about forty seconds and it went like this.

"**A mark and twelve shillings and fourpence in a bag on the morning of Monday the eleventh of September. Three marks owing to a man of Ashtide for a day of work on the eighth of August, with a date on it, in the first column, in no column of this book, and not a shilling of it paid, and he is not named in this book again and is not going to be asked a question and is not going to be sent for.

"**Seven pence owing to this yard's own shipwright for the postage of a letter of the twentieth of August, out of her own pocket, and not paid.

"**Five marks of compressor's gland on a creditor's page that has not been re-written since the fourth of January, unpaid since the nineteenth of May, and of no use whatever to this yard because the thing after the gland is the hose and this harbor has no delivery hose and the longest thing of hose in it is about nine fathoms.

"**Thirty-one on the chandler's note, not re-written, three counts of weeks standing at twenty-two and thirteen and eleven and none of them moved. Nothing in a tin since the twenty-eighth of April. Forty marks standing whole to a boy of twenty-two who cannot do the thing he was kept for, and a column nobody fills.

"**Eighteen lines and seventy-two marks in the wage column and the fifth of March the oldest and staying there, and it has not moved since the twenty-ninth of August.

"**Thirteen envelopes on a bench in the chest and one week fallen due this year with no envelope standing against it, which is the week of Tuesday the fifteenth of August.

"**And a wage of four marks on Tuesday the twelfth, which is tomorrow.**"

The tide made a quarter of an inch in the shed sill and neither of them looked at it.

"**Then that is a bag that is two marks and twelve pence short of a wage,**" said Nessa Pike, "**and there is no day of work booked between now and Wednesday, and I want to know what you are going to write on Tuesday.**"

"**I am going to write that it was not paid.**"

"**You are going to write that it was not paid, and it is going to be the second time in four weeks, and the second time is the one that a man in a market remembers.**"

"**Then it goes in the book and it stands there, and the reason goes against the line, and a request to strike it out of that column is refused in the same words it is refused for a line in any column. That is not a punishment. That is the only thing I have got.**"

---

**And a woman of the third lay came up the hill at about eleven on the Monday morning and booked Thursday the fourteenth for a day of her own, and said before she said anything else that she would pay twelve of seventeen at the mole the way she always had, and Calder Marr said that was the arrangement and had been since the third of May.**

That was the first money this yard had earned since the twenty-eighth of August. It was three marks, and they did not know that until the board was written on the Thursday, and the wage was on the Tuesday, and the two days are the wrong way round and no arrangement in this harbor can make them the other way round.

---

**And on Tuesday the twelfth the wage of four did not go in, and the envelope was not made, and it was entered in the first column in the holder's own left hand with the figure and the date against it and the reason against the line, and it is the second Tuesday in a row on which one has fallen due and has not gone in.**

*First column, checkable by any man who can get off a mole in this harbor: that the wage of four marks for the week ending on Tuesday the twelfth day of September instant was not paid and no envelope was made, that the bag held a mark and twelve shillings and fourpence on the morning of that day, that the column stands at eighteen lines and seventy-two marks with the fifth of March the oldest and staying there, and that this is the second Tuesday in a row on which a wage has fallen due and has not been paid, and that the last week that was paid was the week ending on the twenty-ninth of August and was paid out of a day of work that this party did on the mole's toe inside the first clause of the notice of the thirty-first of October under the ninth acknowledgement in a ledger, and that the yard has entered the connection and is not going to improve on it; and that the reason against the line is that a debt is a debt and a wage is a wage and a man is not paid a shilling of it and told it is a payment, and that this party has entered it against itself with the date and the hour rather than wait for a day it does not know is coming.*

*And that the wage for the week ending on Tuesday the fifth day of September instant was also not paid and no envelope was made for it either, and that this party entered that on the twelfth and not before, and did not enter it on the fifth, and has entered why: that no day of work was booked in that week and none came, and that the bag has not held four marks since the twenty-ninth of August, and that the party's own standing arrangement is that a wage goes in an envelope in the holder's own left hand on the Tuesday it falls due, out of the bag, and that a party which begins to enter a wage late and then enters it on a day of its own choosing is a party whose column has stopped being a column and become a diary, and this yard is not going to do that.*

*And that the count of envelopes on the bench in that chest is thirteen, and that the number of weeks fallen due this year with no envelope standing against them is two, being the week of Tuesday the fifteenth of August and the week of Tuesday the fifth of September, and that the two figures are different figures and neither of them is the other, and that both of them are entered in the same breath in this column and not one after the other because a yard which is asked how many weeks it has paid has to say both numbers at once rather than the one that is easier; and that an envelope that is not made is not a wage and this column is not the wage, and that the forty marks standing to a boy of twenty-two are not an envelope and are not reduced by anything on either line.*

---

**And at about four in the afternoon the shipwright put the slate on the bench between them, with the pence still on it, and said the thing she had been not-saying since Friday.**

"**Then that is on me.**"

"**What is.**"

"**Seven pence. I put it on the envelope out of my own pocket on the twentieth of August because the bag had nothing in it, and you said out loud on that night that the yard would owe it to me and pay it when it had it, and it has not had it, and it is going to go on not having it, and I am not asking you for it and I am not going to ask you for it and I want the asking on the record so that in four years nobody can say the woman never mentioned it.**

"**And I am mentioning it, and you are not going to pay it, and I know you are not going to pay it, and we are both going to keep saying the same thing about it every Tuesday until one of us is wrong.**"

"**You are not wrong.**"

"**No. I am going up the hill and back down it every night anyway and a penny of postage is not what is standing between me and this shed. Put it in the first column with the date against it and my name on it, because if it is in the second column it is a feeling and it is not a feeling, it is seven pence and a woman is out of pocket seven pence.**"

He put it in the first column with the date against it and her name on it, and it took about eleven seconds, and the read-back took longer because she read it back with her name in it out loud, which she has done once before and not often.

---

**And the last thing on the Tuesday was the thing they did not go and ask for, and it is the whole of the moral shape of this fortnight and it is a line in the first column with the reason against it.**

"**There is a thing this yard could go and ask for,**" said Nessa Pike. "**I am not saying it out loud as a suggestion. I am saying it out loud so that we both know I have thought about it and that neither of us is going to do it.**

"**It is in that office on the west basin, and it is nine words long, and you have got a form that was read in a doorway on a hill and a clerk's own hand on it, and if you went up those steps tomorrow morning and asked, that office would have to put a tenth thing in that ledger at its own cost and name the man who pays before it did it, and it would cost somebody a day.**

"**It has already paid twice. A man told you on a mole's steps in front of eleven people that his office has been asked twice and has paid twice and cannot pay a third time and is not going to be.**"

"**Then we are not going.**

"**And I want it written down that we are not going, and why, and I want the why to be a working one and not a noble one, because in four years somebody is going to read that this party could have gone and did not, and they are going to want to know whether it was proud or poor. It was poor. It is poor, and a poor crew does not get an inquiry's costs waived, and a writ is not money, and a hearing is not money, and there is no office in this harbor that has paid this yard a mark since the eighth of May and none is going to.**"

*First column, entered on Tuesday the twelfth of September instant at about half past four in the afternoon in this shed: that this party did not go to the harbor authority of this harbor on Monday the eleventh or on Tuesday the twelfth instant, or on any day before or since, and did not ask that office for anything, and has entered why: that the ninth acknowledgement in that office's ledger is the last entry there is in it and a tenth may not be entered and the officer of that office said out loud on a mole's steps in this harbor on Sunday the twenty-seventh of August that his office has been asked twice by this party, has paid twice, and cannot pay a third time; that an instrument of that office which puts a person, a boat or a lead inside the closure carries his acknowledgement at his own cost and it is never free, and the price of the ninth fell on a man of the first lay and has not been paid and is not going to be thanked for; and that this yard has been told in the open air that it has spent what there was to spend, and a yard that has been told that does not go back up the same steps with the same request, and that this is entered as a decision and not as a virtue and nobody is going to thank this party for it either.*

---

**And on the Monday night, and then again on the Tuesday night at about nine with the lamp lit and the far bench behind her with nobody on it after four o'clock, the day's entries went in and the ship's hand read them back from the top and the holder read them back after her, and there was nobody else in that shed.**

*Second column, not checkable from a mole, entered on Tuesday the twelfth of September instant at about nine in the evening in this shed, and no name is against it: that this party is going to be in a room on Saturday the sixteenth instant at ten in the morning at the hearing room over the tide-gang store, and is not going to be represented by anybody, and is going to answer what is asked and not what it would have chosen; and that the reason this line is in this column and not the first is that a man in a boat can check a mole and a man in a room can be checked and this one prefers to be checked and does not yet know what he will say when he is; and that the party entered on the tenth of September that it is going to do this and is not going to be thanked for doing it, and that a decision is checkable from a mole in the sense that anybody can come up this hill and ask whether it was made, and that the thing about it which is not checkable from a mole is whether it was the right one, and that nobody in this shed is able to settle that and this party is not going to pretend the two are the same question.*

Alden Marr was at the far bench from about eight until about four on the Monday and from about nine until about three on the Tuesday, with his back to the room. He was not asked for anything on either day and asked nothing, and Calder Marr did not go over to him on either day, and the yard entered that he did not and that neither brother has said why and neither brother is going to.

**No money came into this yard on the Monday and none went out. Nothing came in on the Tuesday and nothing went out, and four marks went out of the column and not out of the bag, which are two different places and a wage is paid out of the bag. The cistern behind the engine house has held since the nineteenth of June. The north berth is out. The tide gang is four men short. The fire party's hose is dead until the spring. The hard at Ashtide has been unanswered since the ninth of November, which is the better part of a year. Nineteen winters of a herring skipper's dues are in a naval file and in four lines of a letter and in a list being made up in a city by an officer this yard emptied, and nobody in Morrow Quay will ever be able to check what she writes with them. The light is not on.**

**And a day of work is booked for Thursday the fourteenth, and it is the only money this yard has between it and the wage on the nineteenth, and it is three marks, and the wage is four, and a party that has published itself is not a party that gets to be sorry in a street and mean it, and a notice does not say what a man loses, and the two of those are this yard's own sentences and neither of them has been improved on in nine months.**
