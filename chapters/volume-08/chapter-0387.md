# Chapter 387

*Tuesday the Tenth of August, the Fifty-Seventh Day of the Ninety, the Forty-Second Wage Tuesday and a Wage of Four Marks Not Paid and Forty-Two Weeks Fallen Due With No Envelope Against Any of Them, a Caulker of About Twenty-Three Named Isaac Fell Who Was There at Four in the Morning and Again at Four in the Afternoon and Was Paid One Hundred and Sixty-Eight Pence in Coin on the Mole Head Stone, and the First Fact Anybody in That Harbor Has Ever Had About a Convoy*

Tuesday the tenth of August, at that quay, on the mole head, and in that shed.

**The said Tuesday was the forty-second wage Tuesday and was not a rota day and was not a wipe, and the wage of four marks, being six hundred and forty pence, was not paid on it, and the column did not move, and the column stands at twenty-six lines and one hundred and four marks with twenty envelopes on a bench in a chest, so that forty-two weeks now stand fallen due with no envelope against any of them. The wall behind the reeve's bench came down and went up whole at the angle it has been at since the twelfth and nobody put it back. One hundred and sixty-eight pence went out of that yard on the said day, being a day of a man of about twenty-three at the mole head, paid in coin on the flat of the bench stone, and nothing came in, and there are three hundred and seventy-two pence in the bag, being two marks four shillings and fourpence, and the figure that bag is short of the wage of four marks is two hundred and sixty-eight pence, being one mark nine shillings, and it is not going to be paid and nobody is to write one word under it saying anybody is going to. Nothing has been in a tin in that shed since the twenty-eighth day of April past, and that yard paid a man of about twenty-three one hundred and sixty-eight pence, being one mark and eightpence, on a morning on which it did not pay its own wage of six hundred and forty pence, and both of those figures are in this yard's bag and neither of them is anybody's debt and neither of them is a gift.**

**And the far bench was occupied on the said Tuesday from about ten in the morning until about four in the afternoon and was not approached on it, and the holder of that writ did not go over to him, and nothing was put on that bench and read as a gift, and neither brother has said why. Mara Venn was in that harbor and going nowhere, of no office, not on the crew, not restored, not forgiven, forty marks first after food, neither reduced nor paid, and she was in that shed from about nine until about eleven and did not go up the hill. Tobias Wren was on the crown of that mole from ten until four, being sixty-one, with eleven pieces of a boat behind him and a boat's hook in his right hand, and he was not sent for and not asked and not thanked, and he is not a teacher and not a door and not a sacrifice, and he is not the man on the far bench, and neither brother went near the other at any hour of the said day. The notice of the seventeenth of May past is on its wall in a different street for eighty-five days and has not been touched.**

---

He was on the mole head at about four in the morning and there was a man of about twenty-three standing on the flat of the bench stone with a maul and a bucket of oakum and his own hands, and he had been there since about half past three.

He is a caulker. He is of the third lay and he has a place on no lay's crew and he is not on the stone and he is not one of the six men of that harbor who are ashore with no boat, and he has no boat. His name is Isaac Fell and he was entered against nothing in any column of any ledger in that yard before the said day and he is not entered against anything now except a day and a wage and an hour.

"You said on Wednesday last you were going to ask a question on this lay again on the Wednesday," he said. "I have come on the Tuesday because I heard on the Saturday at the market that the question was on somebody's board and I do not read a board, so I came and found out."

"Stand on the stone and I'll pay you and then I'll ask you."

"You pay me when it is done," Fell said. "I have been on this stone at half past three every morning I could not sleep for nine years and there has not been one single morning in nine years when anybody in this harbour was on it at half past four, and I know that because I have been here for all of them." He turned the maul over. "Say the question."

"What does this harbor want, for a day out and a day back, when the boats are in company and the boats have been asked the other four questions and four men have answered them."

Fell looked at him.

"Answered them with what."

"With a spare warp, and a man who can read a clock and not a watch, and somebody awake ashore at the other end at the hour, and a hand lead in every boat with somebody in her who can use one."

"Right," Fell said. "Then that is a list of things and it is a good list and I have had it in my head in about four boats and never once on paper, and it is worth having. And none of it works."

"Go on."

"None of it works on the day the man is not there." He set the maul down on the stone. "I have been four years in company with men off this coast. You know what goes wrong. It is never the warp. I have never in my life seen a company come apart over a warp. It is always that there was a Wednesday and a man was at his own bench on the Wednesday because his own boat wanted caulking, or he was in a pub, or his boy was ill, or he was carrying somebody's coffin, and everybody ahead of him sailed on, and everybody behind him sailed on, and the boats did not know. And the boats do not know. That is the whole of what I am telling you and it is not a discovery, I have watched it happen three times and I have been in company nine times and eight of the nine were all right."

"Go on."

"I have come here at half past three every morning for nine years and there has not been one morning when anybody in this harbor was on this stone at half past four, and that is not a fault of theirs, because there is no morning when anybody in this harbor has anything to be on this stone at half past four for." Fell took the maul up again. "So that is the fifth thing and it is not a thing. You cannot buy it, you cannot bolt it on, you cannot find it in a shop in a street, and you cannot write it on a board, and the day you do write it on a board is the day a man has a job in this harbor and every other man who was going to be here is somewhere else, and both of those are true and neither of them cancels the other, and I have watched a yard try the board about four years ago and I will not name the yard because I am not going to talk about a yard that is not this one."

**AND A CAULKER OF ABOUT TWENTY-THREE NAMED ISAAC FELL STOOD ON A MOLE HEAD STONE AT FOUR IN THE MORNING ON A TUESDAY IN AUGUST AND TOLD THIS YARD THE FIRST THING ANY PERSON IN THAT HARBOR HAS EVER BEEN ABLE TO TELL IT ABOUT A COMPANY OF BOATS, AND HE TOLD IT IN HIS OWN MOUTH ONCE AND HE WAS NOT ASKED FOR IT AGAIN AND HE WAS NOT THANKED FOR IT AND HE WAS NOT MADE TO SAY IT TWICE, AND A COMPANY OF BOATS DOES NOT STOP BECAUSE SOMEBODY DECIDED IT SHOULD STOP, IT STOPS ON A TUESDAY BECAUSE ON THAT TUESDAY THERE WAS NOBODY ELSE IN THAT HARBOR AWAKE AT FOUR IN THE MORNING, AND THE DAY THE MAN DOES NOT COME IS THE DAY IT IS OVER, AND THE BOATS DO NOT KNOW, AND HE HAS WATCHED IT HAPPEN THREE TIMES AND HE HAS BEEN IN COMPANY NINE TIMES AND EIGHT OF THE NINE WERE ALL RIGHT.**

The ship's hand priced it at about nine in the morning and priced it out loud before anybody agreed to it, which is what she does and which is the only thing in that yard she is asked to do.

"One hundred and sixty-eight pence for a day of a man," she said. "That is a mark and eightpence and it is the figure in chalk on the slate at the end stall and it is not our figure, and it is the figure for a boat and a man, and this is neither, because there is no boat in it. It is eight hours of a man who would otherwise be on a bench of his own, and I have priced the eight hours and the price is that, and there is no line and no fathom and no night's food in it, because he eats at his own house."

"Read it into the second column and not the first."

"It goes in the second column with the reason, which is that a man standing on a stone at four in the morning cannot be checked by a stranger with a leaf and a pencil, and the reason against the line is the reason he gave and it is the best reason anybody has given this shed in a month." She put the pencil down. "And I am not putting his name on a list. He is on a day and a wage and an hour. That is three things and there is no fourth and I am not going to invent one."

---

He paid it at about four in the afternoon on the flat of the bench stone on the mole head, out of the bag and in coin, a hundred and sixty-eight pence, being one mark and eightpence, and Fell counted it and put it inside his coat and said nothing about the sum and said one thing about the day.

"You did not ask me for a Tuesday after that," he said. "You asked me for a day and I have had a day. If you want a Tuesday, you ask me for a Tuesday, on a day, and you pay me for the Tuesday, and the day you do not ask me for one is the day there is not one, and I am not going to stand here and be thanked for turning up."

"I am not going to thank you," Calder said, "and I am not going to put you on a board."

"I know. I have been on a board once and I would rather be on a day."

A master of about thirty of that lay was on the quay at about half past four and had been since three, and he had been waiting for the hour and he had seen the whole of it from forty feet and had not come over.

"Are you going to take them out," he said. "Company. Out on the flood and back on the flood."

"No."

"You've been up this hill four days asking the question."

"I have been up this hill four days asking the question and I have got four things and a person, and the person is standing in front of you and he has been paid a day and not a Tuesday." Calder did not move his hands. "I will not take a company of boats out of this harbour on a day when the man whose day it is has not been asked for it by name, and I will not take one out at all until he has been asked for a Tuesday and has said yes to that Tuesday and has been paid for that Tuesday. And I will not take a company out on a flood because a flood is the only tide this harbour can make and I am not going to stand on this quay and pretend that those two things are not the same thing."

"That is a long way round."

"It is four days and a hundred and sixty-eight pence a day," Calder said, "and it is a day shorter than what it costs the other way, and I have the figure of the other way on a page in a city under glass and you are not going to be shown it."

**AND THE FIFTH THING ON A LIST IN THAT SHED IS A MAN AND IS NOT ON THE LIST, AND A DAY WAS PAID FOR HIM ON A TUESDAY OUT OF THAT YARD'S OWN FIGURES AND NOT ONE PENNY OF IT CAME OUT OF THE MAN WITH TWO PAPERS IN THE OFFICE ON THE WEST BASIN OR OUT OF THE OWNER OF ANY BOAT THAT DAY, AND NO PERSON IN THAT HARBOR IS TO BE PAID BY THE PERSON WHO WANTS THE THING LOOKED AT, AND THAT IS ONE OF SEVEN TERMS AND IT STANDS, AND A TIDE IS A TIDE AND A FLOOD IS THE ONLY TIDE THAT HARBOUR CAN MAKE AND A CONVOY IS NOT A COMPANY AND SIX MASTERS OF A LAY CAME IN ON A SATURDAY AND A LINE OF A NOTICE THAT ASKED THEM NOTHING.**

*Second column, not checkable from a mole, entered on the said Tuesday at about half past nine in the evening in this shed, and no name is against it: that the standing inventory entered in full on the fourth is not entered here again and stands; and that a company of boats does not stop because somebody decided it should stop, and that it stops on a Tuesday because on that Tuesday there was nobody else in that harbor awake at four in the morning, and that the day the man does not come is the day it is over, and that the boats do not know; and that a fifth thing which is a man and is not on the list and is paid a day and not a Tuesday is not a stage and is not named anywhere and is not a word in anybody's mouth, and that paying a man a day for turning up is not buying him and is not hiring a crew and is not a list; and that a caulker who has been on a stone at half past three every morning for nine years and has never once been paid for it and has never once asked is not a symbol of anything and is not an argument and was not used; and that a yard which pays a man one hundred and sixty-eight pence on the morning it does not pay its own wage of six hundred and forty pence has not kept a promise and has not made one and neither of those figures is a debt of anybody's and the two are not to be added together.*

*And in the fourth column of the leaf ruled on the twenty-seventh day of March past, that night, in the ship's left hand, the hundred and twelfth line:*

*What it is not: — that a man paid a day for standing on a stone is not a man bought and not a man employed and not on a board and not on a list, and that being there is not a service and is not a favour and is not an office; and that a wage paid out of a yard's own figures on the morning it does not pay its own wage is not a kindness and is not a debt paid and is not an advance and is not the beginning of anything; and that a person who is there on a Tuesday is not an institution and not an arrangement and not a fleet and is not the beginning of one, and that the fifth of five answers being the one nobody can buy is not a fault in the four that can be.*
