# Chapter 115

*The Pilot Who Keeps a Channel by Hand*

**The cistern was charged on the Thursday the fourth of June at about ten in the morning by two people of this yard and nobody else, and it is in this yard's own book with the hour, and the harbor authority's own book has nothing about it, because a thing done for nothing is not a thing an office writes down.** That is the second time inside seven days and the two of them are entered together on the same page with the reason, and the reason is the one that was entered on the Tuesday, and neither of them is in a wage column and neither of them is going to be thanked.

The boat came into the roads at about two in the afternoon on the Sunday the fourth of June instant out of a westerly that had gone into the southwest and was easing, and she had been four days in it, and there is no hard weather between here and a storm-belt island except the four days she had just had.

She was about forty feet with a leaded keel and her name in white on the quarter and no harbor on her anywhere, and she came up past the mole head at half past three with a man at her tiller who did not ask the mole head anything at all, which is the first thing a man in this harbor notices about a boat that knows where it is.

He was fifty-four or a little under it. He came up the steps at about four and did not come up to the shed; he sat down on the top of them and put the sheet out on the stone between his boots, weighted with two stones, and there were about nine people on the mole and none of them went over.

**"I am not here for work and I am not here for this office," he said. "I have no business in this harbor and I have not got one and I have not come to look for one. My name is Rowan Trewin and I keep a channel nine miles off the point on the other side of that headland, and I have kept it nineteen years, and I am the only man who keeps it, and there are two other men there who would tell you the same.**

"**And that sheet is one of yours. It came out of this harbor in a boat of the third lay on the sixth of May and I have had it in that boat three weeks, and I have read it about forty times, and I have a figure of my own beside yours and it does not agree with yours.**"

"Read that out again," Nessa Pike said, from the top of the steps.

"Nine miles off the point, nineteen years, two other men." He turned round. "You are the shipwright. I have read your sheet and I am going to be rude about one number on it. **I have not come to look at your wall and I have never been in this harbor in my life.**"

"One of the six, and not a copy of a copy, and the count of the six does not move, because the count is a count of hands and not of whereabouts," Nessa Pike said, and she was already saying it to Calder Marr in her own trade's voice. "**Write it on the page tonight with his name on it and his boat's, and put it in the first column, because a man on a mole can be asked about it tomorrow and so can the man whose boat it was.**"

And then, out loud, flat, in front of nine people on a mole, he said the rest of it.

"The bar, off the flood, at twenty past two, off a spring. Your sheet says two and three-quarters. **I make it two and a half.** On the ebb your sheet says two and a quarter and I make it two and none the less, and I have made it four times in nineteen years and I have made it in every state of moon and tide there is and I have never made it your figure and I have never made yours either, and if you want to know why, it is because I have been in that water on nineteen different days and your sheet was made by a man who was in it once on the second of May."

Nobody on that mole said anything for a while, and a gull went over.

"**I am not saying yours is wrong. I am saying that a figure a man made once is a figure anybody can check and a figure a man made four times is a figure only one man can check, and there is a difference between those two things and it is the whole of my trade.**"

"Write it down," Trewin said. "All of it. In your own hand, with my name on it, and put down that I said I would not be told which of us is right, because in about two years somebody is going to come to one of us with the other's figure and I do not want to be a man who has had it argued at him in a shed."

"**A man on a mole can be asked about that again tomorrow," Calder Marr said, "and so can you. It goes in the first column and you are not going to like the page, and I am not going to have you like the page.**"

---

They were in the shed by five and the lamp was on and Alden Marr was at the far bench with his back to them and a cough that came every forty minutes, and neither brother went over to him, and he was not asked for anything.

"Ask me the thing I came for," Trewin said, "and I will tell you that you have not asked me a single question in twenty minutes and I have not asked you one either, and I have been in a great many sheds on this coast and that is not how the afternoon goes."

"You came four days in a weather to ask a question," Calder Marr said, "and I have been four days deciding I did not know the answer to it, and I am going to answer it straight because you have earned that and because there is nothing else in this shed."

He put the sheet on the bench between them and turned it round so that it faced the shipwright.

"**Who keeps it. I do not know. That is the whole of what this party knows about it and it has been on a wall in this harbor since the third of May in a form a man can ask about.** Two and a half men, and one of them went off a stone on the fifteenth of May, and the half says out loud that she is not going in the water. We have never once been to it. Six men copied that sheet by hand in six boats on the second of May and not one of the six is mine and not one of the six was paid, and there is no copy of any of them in this shed, and that is on a wall too, and it is deliberate."

"You have never been to it."

"**Never once. And that is the answer to your question and I would rather give it to you than give you a version of it that makes this yard sound as though it had done something.**"

Trewin looked at him for a moment and then laughed, once, without much in it.

"**Right. Now I will tell you what my question was actually for, and it is not a question, it is a thing I came to say and I have said it badly.** A channel is not a set of figures. Everybody thinks a channel is a set of figures and a chart and a lead line and a man with a pole, and I have watched men do that in four countries and every one of them lost it inside five years.

"A channel is a set of figures **plus a person who will go out and look at it when the figure is wrong, twice a year, for nothing.** Plus a mark somebody can move by hand on a bad night, if they know what the mark is *for*, which is the part that is not in any book and cannot be. My two men and me, that is all, and we do it for nothing, and the harbourmaster up the coast has asked me three times in nine years to put it in a printed return and I have said no three times."

"That is three more people than we have," Nessa Pike said.

"That is the whole of what I have come to tell you and I have not said it as a reproach, because a yard that has never been to a thing does not owe anybody a visit. **You have a road. You have not got a person who will go and look at it. That is not a small difference and it is the only difference that matters and it is not on a wall.**"

---

He said the other thing at about seven, and nobody asked him for it, and Calder Marr did not ask him for it afterward and has not asked him for it since.

"**The other half is a thing I am telling you because nobody will ever ask me for it and because I would rather it were said in a shed in May than in a bar in nine years.** A road kept by three and a half people is a secret, and a secret is a thing one person loses. Not a secret somebody can take off you. One person. They die, or they go blind, or they stop going, and then there is a set of figures on a wall and nobody alive who has ever looked at them, and in about four years it is a story, and in about nine years there is a man on this coast who is quite sure it was never there at all, and he will be quite sure because there is nothing in the world to be sure *of*."

"You have watched it happen," Calder Marr said.

"**I have watched a place lose one. Twice. And I am not going to name the place, and I am asking you not to ask me, and I am asking you not to ask me in a way that lets you guess either.**"

"I am not going to ask you."

"I know that. That is why I am here and I want that on your page in those words, because I have been in this harbor once in four years and I am not coming back until the autumn and if I come back I want it to be on the record that you did not ask me."

**And then he made the offer, and he made it freely, and there was nothing in it.**

"I will come back in the autumn. Late, when the water is the best it is, and I will take my own boat and my own money and I will come out of here and go along five miles of that coast and look at it, and if your two and three-quarters is right and my two and a half is wrong I will say so on your steps in front of anybody you like, and if it is the other way round I will say that too, and I will not want paying and I will not take it and if you offer I will be annoyed."

"Why," Nessa Pike said.

**"Because somebody has to be the one who comes, and there is no office in this coast that sends anybody, and I would rather be a man from a storm belt who turned up in October than a reeve with a claim window, and that is the whole of my reason and it is not a good one and it is a real one."**

The yard said yes.

**And that is what it cost, and it is not money.** A yard with two and a half men, a column nobody is filling and no air in the harbor has just agreed in a shed to be checked, in the autumn, by a stranger, on a date, and there is no instrument in this harbor that can call a man back from a thing he has agreed to turn up for, and no instrument that can stop him, and a man in a city with a seal cannot do anything about it at all because there is no claim on it and no writ over it and no party in it.

"I will put the date in the book," Calder Marr said, "and I will put it in the book the day you say the day and not before, because I do not know the day and I am not going to invent one, and I would rather have a blank line on a page than a false one."

"That is the first honest thing anybody on this coast has said to me about a date in nine years," Trewin said, "and I have had four offices and a shop."

---

They walked him down at about eight and the wind had gone into the south and the harbor was flat, and at the top of the steps Calder Marr said the thing that had been standing in his own head since about four o'clock and had been standing there while two other people talked, and he said it once and did not improve on it.

**"You are the first man who has come into this harbor in five months and asked me a question I could not answer and then stayed for the answer. And I want to say the rest of it out loud while you are standing on them, because it is the sentence you came four days to hear and you have not had it and I have been keeping it since about four o'clock.**

"**A road nobody else can use is not a road. It is a thing one man has in a head.** And you have just told me that the thing you have kept for nineteen years is not the figures and it is not the mark and it is the man who goes out, and you have told me there are three of you, and I have two and a half and a boy who went off a stone on the fifteenth, and the half has not been in the water since February and says so out loud. So I know what I have got and I know what I have not got and you have given me the whole of that in one afternoon for nothing and I would rather be given it by you than find it out in nine years from a man who has bought a copy of a sheet."

Trewin stood on the top of the steps in the dark and did not say anything for a few seconds.

"**Then I will tell you what I came to say, and I have said it badly twice.**" He got his coat straight. "A road nobody else can use is not a road, it is a thing one man has in a head — and in about eleven years there will not be a man, and there will be your wall with four figures on it and the eleventh of April in a boy's hand that is not going to be there, and that is the whole of the future and I have thought about nothing else for four days."

Then he went down the steps to his boat and the boat went out past the mole head at ten past nine and did not put her out at the third lay and did not speak to anybody, and about eleven people on a mole watched a boat leave this harbor and none of them knew who he was.

---

The wall behind the reeve's bench came down at eight on Monday the fifth of June and it came down bare, and that was five Mondays, and there was no sitting called and nothing went up in its place, and the clerk kept his own page and did not mention it, because he had stopped mentioning it and has not started again.

**Twelve marks in a bag.** Thirty-one on the note. Nothing in a tin. Six lines and twenty-four marks in the wage column and the fifth of March the oldest and an envelope on a bench. Forty marks of claim whole. Forty marks to a conservator with no office standing, first after food, unpaid since the fifteenth of October, not reachable by the writ, and not one mark of it paid out of any money that has come into this yard since the eighth of May. Eight shillings and tenpence standing, and about four hundred feet of a woman's own warp on the bottom of this harbor. Eight marks and a day of a boat to this harbor's bellwright paid in April and the same again owing since the twenty-third of May and not paid and not argued away.

No air in this harbor and no delivery hose and a gland on a creditor's page at five marks that is fitted to the yard's own pump and is not paid for. A hand lead's line that is right. The ninth seam out, the north berth out, the third lay full, the tide gang four men short, the light not on, the fire party's hose dead until autumn, this office with no boat of its own since the evening of the twenty-eighth of April, the *Kittiwake* on the hard with no cradle, the hard at Ashtide unanswered since the ninth of November, nineteen winters disputed and on their way up a coast, the *Sea Wren* at the pile, the eleven copies and the four unreached holders not asked, and a man of the third lay, nine winters, out since the twenty-eighth of February, not found.

**A lump of green glass in a bucket in a boat on this mole's crown, with a finger's depth of harbor water in it. Two lines in a second column on two pages, written by two men, and no figure in either. Eleven logged occasions since the twenty-sixth of October and two of them with no call in them and neither moved and no occasion entered this month.**

**And a man of fifty-four out of a storm belt, who came four days in a weather and who is coming back in the autumn for nothing, and who has asked to be able to say in October that nobody asked him a question, and about four hundred feet of a woman's own warp that is on the bottom of this harbor and is going to cost a whole boat's day on Tuesday to put a figure on, and the yard owes her eight shillings and tenpence and has not used that and is not going to.**
