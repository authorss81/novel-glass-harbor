# Chapter 102

*What Is in the Bucket*

The tide was making at ten minutes past eight on Saturday the sixth of May and the wind was in the south-east and about two hours' worth of it, and the sun was out on the mole's crown and there was nowhere on four hundred yards of this harbor that a thing could be looked at in daylight except the top of a mole.

They went up the steps at seven with the bucket between two of them, and the boat came round to the mole's steps with her bucket in the stern, because the boat has to be where the bucket is and the bucket is not in a shed anymore and the boat is not in a shed either this morning. The mole's crown between the third and ninth seams is above the face and not the toe, and this office has entered six times in its own book that it treats the whole of the mole from the third to the eleventh as inside the first clause of a notice it did not write, and the seventh time was at seven in the morning on Saturday the sixth of May, on the yard's own asking, the third time in three months.

---

The clerk came up on his own feet at half past eight with the ledger on a board and did not go below the third seam's mark on the stone, and Calder Marr read the seventh of the reeve's own acknowledgements out loud on the crown at a quarter to nine so that a man on the steps below could hear it being done.

*That on Saturday the sixth of May instant the party stands on the crown of this mole between the third and ninth seams in the open air in daylight to look at an object the party has already reported, and no person of this party goes in the water and nothing is raised and nothing under the bedded material is touched; that the party is not on this office's work and is not going on it and is standing on this ground as the owner of a bucket; that the ground of the first clause of the notice of the thirty-first is the mole's toe and inner face from the third to the eleventh and this office has six times entered that it treats the whole of the mole from the third to the eleventh as inside that clause whether the toe is inside it or not, because a conservative reading of a notice is not a narrow one; and that this is the seventh of those entries, that the sixth was on the twenty-seventh of April and was asked for by a party and this seventh was asked for by a party again, that the cost of all of them is this reeve's and is never free, and that this office has no power to lift that notice and is not lifting it and has never lifted it.*

The clerk read it after him and did not shorten it and read the hour in and read it back.

"That is the seventh," he said, "and I have a page for it, and I am telling you the same thing I told you on the twenty-seventh and on the seventh of April, which is that this office cannot enter what a lump of green glass is. It can enter that a bucket came off a boat on a mole's crown at ten minutes past eight and that the boat went back down at eleven, and that is the whole of what this office has and I have said this five times this year and I am saying it a sixth time and I would like it kept."

---

They broke the rubble off it on a flat board on the crown, in the open air, in daylight, with the sun on it, and what was inside the rubble was green.

A lump about a hand's length, green at the fracture and cloudy through the body where the sun came through it, with a broken crust of the mole's own mortar on two sides of it, and where the sun came through the cloudy part it threw a soft edge on the board about a finger's width across.

Nobody put a name to it for about a quarter of an hour. Then Nessa Pike said the thing that was in her mouth for the rest of the day.

"Green glass in a bucket off a mole's toe is a piece of worked glass and worked glass has been a finding of some kind every day since the fourteenth of October and there is nothing in this harbor that is going to be different about it today. I want that in the first column, and the yard is not going to pretend it is rubble with a shine on it, and I want the second column to say that a lump in a bucket is wet glass and that wet glass is not a thing a conservator will put a name to, and that there is no conservator in this harbor with an office in it and one with none who is forty marks out of pocket and does not need to be useful to anybody." She turned the board a quarter turn. "**And the first thing anybody said out loud when I turned it over, which was the yard, and he said it was nothing. Put that in too, in his own words, and do not let anybody in four years find it in this book and think it was in a second column. It is a first-column statement made by a man who knows glass.**"

It went in in his words.

---

**The two columns, and the reading back, and the finding, and the four days starting where the first four days stopped.**

*First column, checkable by any man who can get onto this crown: the object, a lump about a hand's length, green at the fracture and cloudy through the body, with a crust of this mole's own mortar on two sides of it, and the sun through the cloudy part throwing an edge on a board about a finger's width across; a scalloped edge on one side, of which there are eleven scallops in about four inches, and which is not a fracture, because a fracture in glass runs out of a point and these do not run out of anything, and which is not a mould line, because a mould line is one line and these are eleven and they are all the same hand; the broken face, which is dull and gritty and is not the face that was worked; the place it came out of, being the mole's own rubble off the toe between the third and fourth seams at about twenty-six minutes past eleven on Tuesday the twenty-eighth of April; that it carries no claim seal, that it is in no office's book, that it has no number anywhere, that it is not a panel, and that it is not the ferry plate; and that it is not the worked panel this yard gave back in a market on the seventh of April and it is not connected with that object and nobody is to write the two of them in one line again.*

*Second column, not checkable from a mole: the mass of it, and the reason is that no man on a crown can weigh a thing in his hand and call the figure a mass; the sound of it, struck once on the board with the handle of a wrench, which was a dead note and not a ring, and the reason is that a sound made by a man in the open air on a stone crown is a sound and not a figure; the look of it, which is a thing a man can have an opinion about and a thing four men in four years will not be able to check, and the reason given is that the party is not going to describe what a lump in a bucket looks like to a man in a circuit office, because the description would be a description of this morning and not of the object; and that the party does not know how old it is, does not know who worked it, and does not know whether it was ever attached to anything, and that none of those three goes anywhere and the yard is not going to send anybody off to find out.*

The boy read it back twice. He was on the crown, and not in a boat, and the four men who could not check him were three men and a clerk.

---

**"It is worth nothing to anybody I have been able to find out about," Calder Marr said, at about ten, "and I am going to say it out loud on this crown and I am going to say it twice, once now and once on the page, and I want the page to say it before anybody asks me to say it, because a man who says it after he has been asked has been made to say it.**

"A lump of green glass in a bucket off a mole's toe in a working harbor is worth nothing to anybody I have been able to find out about. There is no seal on it, so nobody may do anything with it. It is not a plate and it is not the ferry plate and it is not the panel in Dace's boat. It is a piece of a mole's rubble with glass in it, it has been out of the water for six days in a bucket and it was in a shed for five of those six and the reason we did not look at it is a notice neither of us can lift and neither of us has argued. **It is worth nothing. And I am the holder of a writ that says I shall report every finding of worked glass whole or in part, and I am going to report it, and the report will say the thing is worth nothing, and that report is due on Wednesday the tenth at the end of the day and it is going up the coast whatever the lump is.**

"**And the fourth day is not counted and the day of the finding is not counted and the day of the finding is the sixth, so the return is due on the tenth.** That starts now. That started when the boy wrote the scallops down, and not before, and not when we broke the rubble off it, and I would like somebody in a circuit office who thinks a day can be a day to write to me about it and I will answer on the day they ask."

The clerk entered the finding and the day and the two columns and the flat statement that the object was worth nothing to anybody the party had been able to find out about, and he entered it in the first column and not the second, because it was a statement of fact made by a man who knows the trade and could be asked about it again in a market on Friday.

---

They were on the crown from eight until eleven and the boat went back down at eleven and the bucket went back into her with the object in it and the object did not go into the shed, and was put instead on the mole's crown under a tarpaulin weighted with two stones and a spare fender, **which is the harbor's own ground in the open air and is not a shed and is not a boat and is not indoors anywhere**, and the boy wrote down where it is and the hour and the two men who carried it, and the four read that back too.

**"The yard has now given two consecutive days to a thing that raised nothing," Nessa Pike said, at about eleven, with the board under her arm, "and I want that in the book in that form, because in four weeks somebody in a city is going to read two returns off this party and one of them says the thing is worth nothing and one of them says the party has not looked at it, and he is going to want to know what a crew does with a Tuesday."

"A yard that has found a thing worth nothing has bought a season. A yard that has found a thing worth something has bought a quarrel, and we have got the boy off the stone on Monday, and I would not swap what is in that bucket for anything anybody in this harbor has offered me for it, including a summer."

---

The notice of the thirty-first of October was not lifted on Saturday, and was not argued, and the yard had not asked for it to be lifted and did not ask for it on Saturday and will not. It was **six months and five days old at ten in the morning on the sixth of May**, and the second of its four clauses is the reason a lump of green glass was looked at in the open air on a mole's crown on a Saturday and not indoors anywhere in this harbor, and the third of them names Calder Marr and Nessa Pike and Bevin Rook for the harbor authority's work and none of the three has been on the harbor authority's work since the day it was served.

And the bellwright of this harbor was not on that crown and was not asked anything and is not of this party and is not of the Free Keel.

On Sunday the seventh the yard sat in the shed and drafted the second return, and the thing they argued about for two hours was not the lump. It was the line that says the age of it goes nowhere at all.

**"It cannot go in the first column, because a man on a mole cannot look at an edge and get a year out of it," said Calder Marr, "and it cannot go in the second column either, because the second column is a place for a thing this yard cannot check, and the party is not going to put *we do not know how old it is* in a column headed by the reasons we cannot check things, because we could check that one in four years by finding out, and then it would be a lie about why we did not.**

"So it goes nowhere. It goes in neither column and it goes on the page as a line of its own with the reason, and the reason is that the party declines to give a figure for it and declines to give a reason for declining, and the circuit office may write for one and the party will answer on the day it asks."

"That is a worse answer than a figure," the boy said.

"It is the only answer that is true and I would rather it was worse," Calder Marr said. "Write it."
