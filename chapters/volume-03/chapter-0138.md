# Chapter 138

*Nine Places, and a Sheet of Paper With No Price On It*

The market office in this harbor keeps four books in a press under a stone slab at the back of the counting floor, and the press is unlocked on a Tuesday and a Friday morning while the counting is going on, and it has been unlocked on a Tuesday and a Friday morning since before the woman who opens it was born, and her mother opened it before her, and there is no fee and no clerk and nobody has to ask anybody's permission, and about nine people come in on an ordinary Friday and about forty come in in a week.

The yard had never been in it. **That is entered as a fact about this party and not as anything else, and the reason is that the party has spent five months putting paper on walls and has never once gone and stood in a room where the town keeps its own accounts for anybody to read.**

---

Friday the twenty-first of July, a day at the second seam on a hirer's own account, nobody in the water, six casts of the hand lead's line and a line laid out twice along the foot of the face with two pins and a rule.

Gross on the board fifteen. The eighth two. The share two. A hired hand three. A second hired hand three. The victuals one and rope one, the two of them together two. All four of the last paid at the mole by the man who hired the day, that being twelve out of fifteen and the yard's being three.

**Four marks in a bag, and it is the last money this yard has coming in before the twenty-eighth, and nobody in this shed knows that and one of the two people in it suspects it.**

And the line came true.

**The six joints stand in a straight line along the foot of that face and every one of the six sits at the same place on that line, and a man can take a string along the stone on the shed floor with two pins in it and it comes to three hundred and forty-one feet from the north end of the first joint to the south end of the last, and there is nothing in the string's way.**

The shipwright put a piece of chalk at each end and stood back and looked at it for a while.

"**Three hundred and forty-one feet,**" she said, "**and six places on it, and a man can walk it in about eleven minutes, and every one of those six places is a hole in a stone wall with a joint in it, and I have walked that face twice a day since the sixth of May and I never once put a string along it.**"

"**Then lay the string.**

"**And I am going to say the next part before I forget it, and I am going to say it once. On Tuesday I said the word *chain* at the head of this dock in about eleven people's hearing and I have entered in the second column that it was wrong in three parts out of four, and the party is not using that word. Nine places is nine places. A line is a line. I am not going to be a man in a market who puts a name on a thing and then has to take it back, and if I have to take one back, that is a day like the eighteenth of July and not the day before it.**"

---

**And they went up to the market office at about eleven in the morning and asked the woman to let them see the four books, and she said yes, and she said it the way you say yes to a thing that has never once been a question.**

**The builder's day-book of the port of Ashtide** is in that press and has been in that press since a year nobody in that office names, and it came in on a cart with a lot of other things and has never been taken out, and it is a builder's account of a mole face, kept by a man who wrote down every place he worked by the seam and every third of a foot of it, because that is what a builder does and he was paid by the foot.

Six entries and six places, and the six places are all in the first four of the six, and **the six places are the six joints, and a man can lay a string on the page and put six pins on it and it is the string.**

> *… the tenth day, at the second seam, forty feet, and seventy-three feet, the stone came out of the second quarry and it is a green stone and it does not bed.*

> *… the fourth day, at the second seam, one hundred and forty-one feet, and two hundred and ten feet, a fair face and the best week I have had on this coast.*

> *… the ninth day, at the second seam, two hundred and seventy-nine feet. Two of my own hands away and I have not the two back. I have told the foreman three times and I have written it here a third time and if it is not answered in the spring I will write it to the clerk at Veyr.*

> *… the second day, at the second seam, three hundred and forty-eight feet. Nothing to report but the weather.*

> *… the sixth day, at the second seam, four hundred and seventeen feet. My father cut the first hundred feet of this face and I have heard him say so.*

> *… the last day, at the second seam. The face is done to the root and the ninth and I have been at this thirty-one years and I have never once been asked what the joints at the foot are for and I am not asking.*

**And the last entry is signed with the builder's name, and that name is cut into a stone at the mole head where a man can read it standing on the crown, and this party has read it a hundred times and had never once gone and looked for a book.**

**The weir-book** of this harbor keeps the herring weir. It is a book of tides and fish and weather and it is in a clerk's hand for about four hundred years and then in nobody's, and there are about nine pages in it in a fisher's own hand and they are the nine the clerk has never been able to make into a clerk's hand.

> *… the eleventh night, a westerly, and the weir took eleven hundred and forty and we lost the second boat and the eleven hundred was not worth the boat and I have written it here because nobody is going to write it. My grandfather's weir is under the eleven feet of stone at the south end of the mole and I have dived on it and I know where every pile of it is. J. Voke.*

**And the joint at the third place from the north end of that line is a foot and four inches below the weir's own sill stone, and the weir-book for the same page has the sill repaired and the repair is in the hand of the man whose grandfather built the weir and the repair is dated four years after the storm.**

**The poor-book** of this town is a book of about nine hundred pages and about eleven people in this harbor have ever looked in it. On one page in the middle of a month, in a woman's hand, there is a line about twenty-two children put out of a village and sent to a relation, and where they went, and what they were fed, and the names of about nine of them because the other thirteen were not entered and nobody has ever found out why not.

**And the place in that line is not on this coast, and it is not on any chart that anybody in this harbor has seen, and the page has a place on it that the woman who wrote it wrote as though it were four miles away, because to her it was.**

**The tide-gang's step book** is a count of how many times a gang of men has walked a length of mole in a year, kept because this office was once fined for not walking it, and it is a page of figures and nothing else for about sixty years. The column for the foot of this mole's face goes back a long way and then stops, and the year it stops is the year the gang stopped walking that foot, and the two columns after that are for the ninth seam only, and one of them carries the name of a sand and shell lay of this coast that is not on any chart in this harbor, and the yard looked at it for an hour and matched it to nothing, and has entered that it did not and is not going to guess.

---

**And so, by four in the afternoon, there were nine places on the shed floor, and six of them had a hole in a stone wall at them, and three of them did not.**

*First column, checkable by any man in this harbor who can get into the market office on a Friday morning: that six joints in a crevice at about four fathoms on the second seam's own face of this mole stand in a straight line, and that a string laid along that line with two pins in it comes to three hundred and forty-one feet; and that the six places are the six places named in six entries of a builder's day-book of the port of Ashtide kept in the market office press, being forty feet, and seventy-three feet, and one hundred and forty-one feet, and two hundred and ten feet, and two hundred and seventy-nine feet, and three hundred and forty-eight feet, in each case at the second seam; and that the builder's name is cut into the mole head; and that the third of the six from the north end lies a foot and four inches below the sill stone of this harbor's own weir; and that three further places have been entered, being a weir, a village not on this coast, and a sand and shell lay of this coast which is not on any chart in this harbor, and that the party has entered the three and has entered that it does not know why the three are there and is not going to guess and is not going to look for them this season.*

*And that the party declines the word. Nine places is nine places, a line is a line, and a builder who wrote down where he worked by the foot because he was paid by the foot is not a man who was writing a history of anything.*

*Second column, not checkable from a mole: **what any of the nine is, and what the six joints carried, and what the glass in the crevice and the glass in the packing and the glass in the tallow of a lead are, and the party will not join the two halves of this and will say why on the record and for the market. The account is about the sea. The finding is about the glass. Neither of them is a reason to believe the other, and a man in a city who puts them in one sentence is doing the thing this party did on the third of May, which is naming a road, and this party is not going to do it twice.**"

---

**And on Saturday the twenty-second of July, at about eleven in the morning, in daylight, in a market, the yard nailed its own fourth return up on the market wall of Morrow Quay.**

It went up on the same nails everything goes up on, and it went in over the printed sheet of Perry Ollett, because a sheet that goes up goes up on the outside of whatever is there, and that is the arrangement and it has been the arrangement since the ninth of November.

**It is one sheet in the harbor authority's own paper, with the place and the hour and the keeper and the limits against the head of it, and the object, and the line of what it is not in full, and the four lines of the second column, and it has no scale on it and no price at the head of it and a man cannot buy it, and there is nowhere on it to write a letter to.**

**A shilling changed hands at the foot of the dock at about half past eleven for a copy of the printed sheet, because there is a man at the foot of this dock who sells copies of whatever is on that wall, and the shilling is not this yard's income and is not in the bag and will not be.**

Perry Ollett was standing in front of it with his arms behind his back and read it from the top to the bottom twice, and then he read the four lines of the second column a third time, and nobody in that street said anything to him while he was doing it.

"**That is a Crown attachment,**" he said at last, "**on a party's own writ, and it is the only paper on this wall anybody in a city is obliged to look at, and I have known that since March and I have not had one.**"

"**You could have had a copy of the tenth thing in June for four marks and I would not sell it to you and I am not going to be sorry about that this morning.**"

"**No,**" said Ollett. "**I am not either, and I am not going to pretend we are two men with a difference. I am going to be standing here on Monday with a better sheet than that, because that is what I am for, and I am not going to pretend I would not be.**"

"**Then stand there. That is the arrangement and it has been the arrangement since the ninth of November.**"

---

**And at about ten past twelve, in the open air, in front of about forty people, in a market, Mara Venn said out loud that Calder Marr was wrong, and she said it to him, and she was right, and neither of them changed a word of anything after it.**

"**You have had a reading taken off a joint and a book out of a press and you have put them on the same afternoon, and you know exactly what you have done and so do I, and I am going to say the word for it because nobody else in this market is going to.**

"**You have made it public. That is the only thing that has happened this month that is not the sea. Everything else is six joints under a mole and a print in a city and a fight about a word. This is the first thing you have done since the third of May that you cannot take back, and you have done it because a wall with nobody able to be asked a question about it is a wall worth nothing, and I said that sentence to you in my own hearing in a shed on the sixth of July and I was right, and you used it, and here it is on a wall, and it is a good wall.**

"**And the cost of it is on the same sheet, about a foot below my hand, and the cost is that there is now a piece of paper in this harbor with a place, an hour, a keeper and limits on it, and that the keeper is a man with a name on it, and that anybody who wants to know where six joints are under a mole in this harbor now knows, and cannot un-know it, and cannot un-print it, and you did that on purpose.**

"**I do not think you are wrong. I think you are a man who has run out of money and a man who has not. And I want it said in a market that I said both halves of that in the same breath and did not take either back.**"

Nessa Pike said it to the slate and not to the wall, and her hand was steady, and what she wrote was this and it went up on the paper on Monday and not on Saturday, and it is the reason the paper is in a market at all:

*Second column, entered by Mara Venn, in her own hand, **unsigned at her own request**, on the twenty-second of July instant, in a market, in about forty people's hearing, and she has given the reason and the reason is that a finding of no office carries no seal and cannot be produced on demand by anybody, and a signature would make it a document and this is not a document: **that the party should publish the finding and the party should not publish the books, and that she thinks the party is wrong to do both, and that she has said so in a market and is not going to say it again in a shed, and that she is not going to be asked to sign it and that if anybody asks her to sign it the answer is no.***

And Calder Marr's answer to that, in the same market, in about nine words, and the shipwright wrote it down:

"**Both. And I will not be able to tell you which was right in October.**"

---

**And the third column was filled on the Saturday evening with the two times at the head of it and the lateness against them, and it is the seventh entry in that column since the second of May, and the lateness on this one is about three hours, which is the second longest this column has run and which the party is not going to make a practice of, and the reason the party is not going to make a practice of it is the same reason it gave when there were four of them.**

**And the money on Saturday the twenty-second, read out at the head of the dock in about eleven people with the market going in behind them, is one paragraph and it is the whole of the twenty days since the sixth of July.**

Four marks in a bag, and the four of them came in on Friday from a day at the second seam on a hirer's own account, gross fifteen, the eighth two and the share two and two hired hands at three apiece and the victuals one and rope one, that being twelve out of fifteen. Thirty-one on the note, not re-written, the three counts unmoved, five marks of compressor's gland unpaid, the tin empty since the twenty-eighth of April. **Thirteen lines and fifty-two marks in the wage column, the fifth of March the oldest, eight envelopes on a bench and none of them anywhere, and the next of them is four marks on Tuesday the twenty-fifth of July, and the bag is four.** Nothing has come in since Friday and nothing will come in before the twenty-eighth, and there is no office in this harbor that has paid this yard a mark since the eighth of May and none that is going to.

Forty marks standing whole to a boy of twenty-two. Forty marks to a conservator of no office, first after food, unpaid since the fifteenth of October, **who read at the head of a dock on Sunday the sixteenth, in her own words, that it is nine months, and put the number there herself, and asked for nothing.** Eight shillings and tenpence to each of two women, in no sentence together, and one of them has a crew of Ashtide coming on the twenty-eighth for twenty-six marks and a day. Eight marks and a day of a boat to a bellwright, unpaid since the twenty-third of May, unargued, and the standing sentence said in a street on the third of May stands unreversed. No air and no hose. The ninth seam out with an end of it entered for the first of September, the north berth out, the third lay full, the light not on, the hard at Ashtide, nineteen winters in a naval file and in a letter, eleven copies and a printed sheet, **and four returns, one of which is on a market wall with no price on it and a line in it by a woman of no office who will not sign it.** A man of the third lay, nine winters, not found, and this yard has not gone.

**And on Tuesday the twenty-fifth there is a wage, and the column goes to fourteen lines and fifty-six marks, and nobody in this harbor has any idea what that closes on.**
