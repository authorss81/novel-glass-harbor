# Chapter 106

*Monday the Fifteenth*

The boy was off the stone on Monday the fifteenth of May because on Monday the first of May, in a hearing room over the tide-gang store, in front of a clerk of the circuit office, the yard had said out loud that the boy was not to be sent because his hand was going, and had named the fifteenth in the same breath, and the clerk had entered it in the ledger in the clerk's own hand and had read it back.

It was a Monday and therefore the morning the hearing room's slate wall came down at eight, and the clerk kept his own page, and there was no sitting, and nobody noticed any of that in a shed at the top of a hill.

---

They went down to the cottage row behind the fish market on Sunday afternoon at about four because there was nothing to be said in a shed and everything to be said in a doorway, and Nessa Pike went with him and Calder Marr went with him and neither of them said a word on the way down.

She was in the doorway with her sleeves pushed up.

"Say it to me on the step," she said. "I have been told the truth about that boy once in a front room and once in a shed and I am not going to be told it in a room, and I have known since the first of May what day it is and I have not asked either of you and I am not going to ask you now."

"He is off the stone tomorrow," Calder Marr said. "The fifteenth. Because his hand is going, and because on the first of May in a room in front of a clerk of the circuit office the yard said he was not to be sent, and the date was named there, and it is written in a ledger in a clerk's hand and it is not a promise and it is not a lie. He is owed forty marks and he is owed none of it, and it stands whole and it is not reduced and it is not going to be reduced. His wage is four marks a week and we have paid what we could and we have paid it out of other men's pockets and not one mark of it out of this yard's own work, and I did not put that in a market to be thanked for it. He comes off the stone and he is not chased and nobody goes after him."

She looked at the step for a while.

"Then I have been told the same thing three times," she said, "and two of them were in a room and one of them was in a doorway, and you have been careful about it and I have not said thank you and I am not going to. **What I am going to say is the thing I have been not told twice, which is that there is a bonesetter's book in this town with a boat hand in June in it and a boat hand in June is four marks a week and a bunk and a man to talk to at the end of a day, and I have been counting since the fourth of May, and I am telling you now so that neither of you has to tell me anything else and can go back up that hill.**"

Nobody answered her. She went inside and shut the door, and the two of them stood on the step, and Calder Marr said, "She has been counting since the fourth of May," and Nessa Pike said, "She has," and neither of them said anything else about it on the walk up.

**On the Monday morning the boy read the position out one last time and read it twice, and it took him longer than it used to.**

He did it at the head of the dock at about nine in the morning with the wind in the south-east and the market not yet in, and the yard and the shipwright stood on either side of him and the fourth read-back was taken at half past ten by the harbor authority's clerk, who came up the hill on his own feet and was not asked.

*Thirteen marks in a bag on the third of May and twenty-five this morning, and thirty-one on the chandler's note after a casting of five marks in the creditor's own hand and a fortnight of bread at three paid out of the same bag, and the carting not on the page, and the three counts of the weeks of that page at twenty-two, thirteen and eleven as last written and none of them moved. Nothing in a tin. Five lines and twenty marks in the wage column, the thirteenth week entered on the fourth of May by me with the reason that it went in on the fourth, and the fifth of March the oldest line in it and staying there.*

*Twelve marks in on Monday the eighth at the second seam on the harbor authority's own account and four out on Tuesday the ninth for the fourteenth week and three out on the Wednesday for a fortnight of bread, and four in on the Wednesday for a pump and four more on the Thursday for the same pump, and a mark to a man and a handcart who went two miles up this coast road and came back with a casting. Twenty-one after the wage, eighteen after the bread, twenty-two at eleven o'clock, and twenty-five at six o'clock on the Thursday, and the twenty-five is the figure this morning.*

*Forty marks of claim standing whole, unpaid, not reduced, not asked to be reduced. Forty marks to a conservator with no office standing, first after food, unpaid since the fifteenth of October, not paid out of any market money and not reachable by that writ, and she is not of it. Eight shillings and tenpence to Sella Brigg standing and undiminished, and about four hundred feet of her own warp on the bottom of this harbor.*

*Twenty marks of unsold iron, and four hundred and forty pounds of old iron in three pieces off this mole's own face between the third and fourth seams raised on the twenty-eighth of April, in the same heap behind that shed, one of the three a bell-metal gun-port off something that had a gun in it, unsold and unweighed and unpriced and not offered to anybody, and the heap is not to be sold one mark's worth of without a buyer reading the declaration first.*

*No delivery hose in this harbor and no air in this harbor and a compressor gland on a page at a creditor's own figure of five, not fitted and not going to be for nine days, and a pump gone out of this shed for the first time since the fifth of April and two days of it and back again since Friday morning. A hand lead's line made badly in the net loft on the second of May and made again and not asked about since. The ninth seam out with no end of it entered, the north berth out, the third lay full, the tide gang four men short, a cistern on a rota that a man does not walk, the light not on, the repair account empty since the middle of February, the *Kittiwake* on the hard with no cradle, the hard at Ashtide unanswered since the ninth of November, nineteen winters disputed and unpaid. A man of the third lay, nine winters, out since the twenty-eighth of February, not found, and one line in this book about him and the yard has not gone.*

*A bell in eight and a half feet off the south end with its waist fouled and its frame cut through the casting on one side and **no tongue on the frame at all**, and two parts of a chain in a lead plug where the tongue was, and the going out that was bought in a market on the twenty-ninth of April was done at ten past three on the second of May in a flat calm and is spent, and the next one is in about four years at eight marks and a day of a boat then as it is now, and I have written that on a wall on Wednesday the third of May and I am not going to be asked about it again.*

*And a lump of green glass in a bucket that was in a shed from the twenty-eighth of April to the fourth of May, entered in a return to the circuit office in the yard's own hand without anybody asking, and it is worth nothing to anybody this yard has been able to find out about, and it is on this mole's crown under a tarpaulin and two stones and a spare fender, and a return about it was made on Wednesday the tenth on the day it was due, and the day of a finding is not counted and the return is the fourth day after it.*

*And eleven occasions logged since the twenty-sixth of October, and two of them logged with no call in them, and neither figure has moved and neither is going to move, and a figure taken on purpose on a flat calm is a figure and not a note heard out of the weather, and **a boy who has counted eleven is not going to make it twelve by writing a number.***

He read it back twice. The second time took him eleven minutes and the first had taken seven, and the difference was not a mistake and nobody in that dock said a word about it.

Then he was paid, and the paying is on the page and there is no speech attached to it.

*The yard pays Bevin Rook four marks, a week at the rate from the first of February, for the week of Monday the ninth to Sunday the fifteenth of May inclusive, on Monday the fifteenth, and not on Tuesday the sixteenth, and the reason is that this yard is not going to pay that boy a wage on a Tuesday with a hand that cannot hold a pencil in it, and the next wage week falls on Tuesday the sixteenth and no line goes in for it, and the reason that goes in is that this yard has no reader and a column with nothing in it is not a column with a week missing in it.*

"Four marks is a week," Nessa Pike said, "and a week is seven days, and four marks for seven days is not a day's rate and I am not going to work it out in front of you and put it on the page, and if anybody asks me for a day's rate for him I will tell them what a boy of twenty-two is worth a day and it is nothing."

He counted the four marks and put them in his own coat and did not say anything about them, and the forty marks of claim above them on the page did not move, and nobody in that crew asked him one word about either of the two omissions he had entered in February and in April, then or afterwards or ever.

**And then he put the book down on the bench with the pencil on top of it and said one sentence, and he said it once and would not say it again.**

"I am not going to write anything else in it," Bevin Rook said. "I want it said, in this shed, with four men in it, and I want it said once. **From tomorrow the day does not get read back, and the day does not get read back by anybody, and I am not going to have anybody in this yard invent a hand and write in the third column as though the man who did it was in the boat. The third column has got two times at the top of it because I put them there, and the two times are the time it was read on the steps and the time it went in the book, and the difference between those two times is the whole of what that column is for, and there is nobody in this crew now who is going to be late, because there is nobody in this crew who is going to be writing it.**"

"Write that on the last page," Nessa Pike said, "in your own hand, and leave the pencil on top of the book."

"I will write it on the last page," he said, "and I am not going to be thanked for it, and I am not going to be told what I was worth, and I am not going to have anybody say anything to me about the fourth of April."

Nobody said anything to him about the fourth of April.

**The hearing room's slate wall was bare at eight that Monday morning and the clerk's own page wanted a side and a half and the ledger is the record, and at about half past eleven in the morning the clerk came up the hill for the last time on a working errand and read the last page in Calder Marr's own left hand and then read it again in the boy's own right hand, and entered in his own book that the third column of this yard's book is of that yard's book and not of his office's, and that the head of it carries two times and that from Monday the fifteenth of May the yard states in its own book that no man is filling it, and that this office can enter that and cannot enter the two times, and that he had a right to expect them and was not given them and did not ask for them.**

"**This is the first thing in five months that I have come up this hill for and been given less than I came for," the clerk said, "and I am going to keep it in my book in those words, and I am not going to pretend to anybody that I did not want the two times.**"

Alden Marr was at the far bench with his back to the two of them and the lamp behind him, at sixty-one, with a cough that came every forty minutes, and he had not been asked for anything on that Monday or any Monday, and neither brother went over to him, and the boy did not go over to him either.

At about four o'clock the boy put his coat on and went down the hill and was not chased, and nobody went after him, and the book was on the bench with the pencil on top of it and the third column had a head on it and nothing under it and a line on the last page in a boy's own hand saying so.

The door hung on its hinge for about a minute afterwards and then somebody put it to, and nobody said anything for a while.

Calder Marr went and looked at the third column, because he had been not looking at it all day, and the head of it was in the boy's own right hand and there were two times on it: the hour it was read on the steps and the hour it went in the book. On the second of May there was an hour and forty minutes between those two times. That was the whole of what the column was for. It was also the whole of what the boy was, and Calder Marr had known that since about the middle of February and had never once put the two facts next to each other, because a man does not do that to a book he has to keep.

He had been in this trade nineteen years. Four masters, two dockets, and something over a hundred and forty positions read out in his own hand and in other men's, and he had never once had to hand a book on.

"You are not going to the net loft tonight," Nessa Pike said, from the bench with her own lamp.

"No."

"Go and sit on the mole for a while, then. There is nothing on that crown but a tarpaulin and two stones, and it will be dark in an hour."

"That is not what I meant and you know it is not what I meant."

"I know what you meant," she said, "and there is no page in this book it goes on, and I am not going to write one for you, and if you sit on that mole until dark you will come back down and read that column out loud to nobody in a shed, and it will be the best reading you ever gave and there will not be a man in this harbor left to say whether it was right. So go and sit on the mole, and leave the book where it is."

He left the book where it was. He went up the hill as far as the steps and sat on the top of them with his hands on his knees for about an hour, with the wind in the south-east and about two hours of it and the tide making. On the third of May a boy of twenty-two had stood on those steps with a slate and a stopwatch and read a position out twice and been right both times. On the fifteenth he went down the hill with four marks in his coat and two hundred days of a habit in his hands, and the difference between those two mornings was four marks and a hand, and the four marks had been paid and the hand was not going to be paid by anybody, ever, and there was nothing in this harbor that could be entered against that sentence, because a hand is not a debt and cannot be put on a page.

He came down at about six. The shed was shut and the lamp in the net loft was lit and there was nothing to be done, and he did it anyway, which is what a yard does on the evening a man leaves it.
