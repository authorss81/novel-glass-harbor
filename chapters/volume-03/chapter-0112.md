# Chapter 112

*The Rota on a Monday and a Thursday*

The wall behind the reeve's bench came down at eight on Monday the twenty-ninth of May, because it comes down at eight every Monday whether anybody has called a sitting or not, and it came down bare, and there was no sitting, and nothing went up in its place, and that was the fourth Monday running.

The clerk of the harbor authority came up the hill at about ten with his own page under his arm and no errand, which is a thing he had never done, and he read his own page out in the shed doorway for about two minutes and then put it back under his arm.

"The twenty-second wanted a side and three-quarters and this office is not working," he said. "The twenty-ninth wanted nothing. The wall came down bare. That is five Mondays and the first of May was the last figure anybody put on it and the office has not put one up since and I have not mentioned it and I am not going to go on mentioning it. **The ledger is the record and the wall is a convenience and after the first of May I found I could get along without the convenience.**"

"Then we will stop expecting a figure," Calder Marr said.

"I would not do that," the clerk said. "**I would go on expecting one and go on not getting it, and the difference between those two is the whole of what this office is at the moment and it is not one of your problems and I have come up a hill to say so and not to be thanked for it.**"

He went back down the hill and the yard went out at half past nine to walk out to a sluice.

---

**The cistern is behind the engine house and it is charged on a rota on a Monday and a Thursday, and the rota has four names on it, and three of the four are crossed out in the office's own hand in a way that is not a cancellation but a note that there was no money to pay the man.**

The man is of the harbor's own tide gang. He is on the office's wall. He is one of the four the tide gang is short from the first of May, and being short of men in June in this harbor does not mean standing about; it means being told at eight in the morning that there is no day work and going home, and the day work is the only work on that wall that is paid at anything.

He had not walked out on Monday the twenty-second. That had been in the reeve's own account of what the notice of the thirty-first of October had cost this harbor authority, read out in a room on the seventeenth of May, and it was the only line of the seven in that account that anybody in the room had not known before it was read.

They walked out with a hose and a bucket at about ten, in daylight, in front of the market going in, and it took three of them two hours because the sluice gate is stiff and the office's gate iron is the worst iron in this harbor and the fourth man of the party stayed on the mole with a bucket of green glass under a tarpaulin and did nothing whatever with it and did not go near the water.

**Ewan Tristram was on the second lay's boards at about half past eleven with his hat in his hand and he came and looked at what they were doing and did not offer, and then he said the thing he had come down the hill to say.**

"You have done that week's work in a morning and I have watched you do it from about thirty feet."

"Somebody had to."

"Somebody did not. That is not the argument and you know it is not the argument, and I have been the wharfinger of this second lay for twenty-two years and I am about sixty years old and I have been in this harbor fifty-one years and I have watched a great many people do a week's work in a morning." He put his hat back on. "**I have a wharf. The town has a cistern. That is the whole of it and I have said it out loud now so that a man can write it down and I cannot be asked again.**"

"Then we will write it down."

"Write it in your own book and not on my wall, because if it is on my wall it is a thing somebody can hold me to, and I have not got the years left for a thing like that." He looked at the tank for a while. "You will get the figure of it wrong, by the way, and I will tell you now so that you cannot say afterwards that nobody told you. **A cistern is not a debt. A cistern is a hole with a lid on it. What I have got is a hole with a lid on it, and the lid is on a Monday and on a Thursday, and the reason the lid is on those two days and not any others is that Monday and Thursday are the two days a man is not sent to the hard.**"

---

They were back up the hill at about one and the five things the town of Morrow Quay pays in were on the bench in front of Calder Marr, and he wrote them out in his own hand, in a list, with a figure against each of them where there was a figure, and Nessa Pike read it back and then said the thing she had come up the hill to say and she said it in her own trade's words and it was not the argument she had made in February.

**"A mooring, a berth, a warp, a boy's summer and a cistern. That is the coin of this town and there is no sixth and there is not going to be a sixth, and I have been in a boatyard since I was eleven and I could tell you what a town runs out of without asking a wharfinger and I have asked a wharfinger."**

*That the ninth seam's mooring has been out of service from the first of May with no end of it entered in any book in this harbor; that the north berth is out and the third lay is full, which is a full and not a repair; that about four hundred feet of a woman's own warp is on the bottom of this harbor under six feet and has been since the sixth of February, and fifty fathoms of the herring fleet's winter warp is on the north wall where ninety were to lie, and forty feet of this office's own marline has been in the sea off that wall since the twenty-seventh of February; that a boy of twenty-two who is owed forty marks standing whole is not in this yard and has not been in this yard since the fifteenth of May; and that a cistern behind an engine house is charged on a rota by a man this office cannot pay.*

"**Three of those five are already spent and have been since before this yard came back into the water,**" she said. "The mooring is out and nobody has entered when it comes back. The berth is out and the third lay is full and that is a full and not a hole. And there is a warp at the bottom of this harbor with about four hundred feet of a woman's own best rope in it, and she is owed eight shillings and tenpence and she has not asked for either. So there is a cistern and there is a boy who is not here, and that is what this town has got in hand on the twenty-ninth of May, and a cistern is the one a crew can do something about and I am not going to pretend that makes the other four any better."

"And the other half of it," Calder Marr said, "which is that a crew is the people who turn up."

"Yes. **And I said something in February that I was right about, and I am not going to say it again, because I have thought about it for three months and it was right about a man in a shed and it is not right about a man of fifty-one on a mole in a blow.** I said then that a yard that owes a boy four marks a week and a bellwright eight marks will not put a man off a boat in its shed. That was true in February and it is still true and it is no use at all today, because the bellwright's eight marks is paid and the boy is gone and there is no man in a shed to put anybody off a boat in."

"So what is the argument."

"**The argument is that a crew is the people who turn up, and that this yard is two and a half and turning up is now a thing the yard does to a town that has stopped being able to ask for it.**" She put her thumb on the bench. "And the other half of it, and Calder, I want the other half of it on the page as well, because a page with half a sentence on it is a lie a man in a city can read. **A thing done once is a favor and a thing done twice is a habit and a thing done three times is a thing the office expects, and a thing the office expects is a thing the office can stop whenever it likes, and I have watched this office stop four things this year for money and none of them for want of trying.** So write it: this yard knows what it is doing and it is going to go on doing it, and it is going to do it at its own cost and out of its own book, and it is not going to be paid for it and it is not going to call it a favor, and nobody in this yard is going to be thanked for it and nobody is going to be allowed to thank anybody for it."

"That is a long line for a Monday."

"It is a long month," she said.

---

**And the yard walked out to the sluice again on the Tuesday the thirtieth, at about eight in the morning, and the reason for the second time is not in any office's book and is entered in this yard's own book in Calder Marr's own left hand.**

*That on Tuesday the thirtieth instant, at about eight in the morning, this party walked out to the sluice behind the engine house a second time inside two days, not being a rota day; and that the reason is that a cistern behind an engine house cannot hold from a Monday to a Thursday on a wall where there is no fire party's hose laid out and no pump on the carts and forty feet of the herring fleet's winter warp where ninety were to lie; and that the party has been told by the wharfinger of the second lay that the lid of that cistern is on a Monday and on a Thursday and that a cistern is not a debt but a hole with a lid on it, and that the party has entered that in its own book and is not entering it on the second lay's wall; and that the party has not been paid for either day and is not going to be, and that neither day goes into a wage column, and that the reason is that the two days are not work this yard did for money and putting them in a column that a man can add up would be the first lie this yard has written in this book.*

They were at it four hours because the gate had been left standing open in Monday's wind and the tank was down to about a third, and two men of the third lay came and helped without being asked and would not take anything and were not thanked and did not want to be.

And on the Tuesday afternoon the column was done, because a wage week falls on a Tuesday and the next one after the fifteenth fell on the thirtieth.

*The yard pays four marks, a week at the rate from the first of February, for the week of Tuesday the thirtieth of May to Monday the fifth of June inclusive, on Tuesday the thirtieth, in Calder Marr's own left hand, and there is nobody in this shed to receive it and nobody in this yard has been told to carry it anywhere.*

*And the reason is that a column that stops is a column with a week missing in it, and the fifth of March is the oldest line in this column and is staying where it is, and the two Tuesdays of the sixteenth and the twenty-third went by with no line in them and the reason for those two is on the page and is that this yard has no reader.*

*And the four marks are put in an envelope and the week is written on the outside of it in the same hand, and the envelope is in the bench chest and not among the five rolled in oilcloth, and it is not sent anywhere this week and is not going to be sent this week; and the reason is that a wage is not a reason to go to a door.*

**Six lines and twenty-four marks, and the fifth of March the oldest.**

Calder Marr read the column back to himself in the empty shed, which is not a read-back, and entered that he had done it and why.

*That the party has read this column back in the yard's own hand and has entered that it was read back by the man who wrote it, and that a column read back by the man who wrote it is not a column read back, and that the reason is that the hand that used to do it is off the stone and is not coming back on a Tuesday, and that the party is going to go on writing the figures in this yard's own hand and is going to go on entering that, and it would rather have a page that says who read it back than a page with nobody's name against it.*

---

The two columns were read out at the head of the dock at about six on the Tuesday, and there was no market and about eleven people in the street and two of them were of this yard.

**Nine marks in a bag.** Thirty-one on the chandler's note and the three counts of that page at twenty-two, thirteen and eleven as last written, and none of them moved, and the note has not been re-written and is not going to be this year at a figure this yard cannot put down. Nothing in a tin. **Six lines and twenty-four marks in the wage column and the fifth of March the oldest, and an envelope on a bench with a week written on it in a hand that has not signed a page in this yard for two hundred days and had better get used to it.** Forty marks of claim standing whole. Forty marks to a conservator with no office standing, first after food, unpaid since the fifteenth of October, not reachable by that writ, and she is not of it. Eight shillings and tenpence to Sella Brigg, undiminished and unpaid, and the four hundred feet of her own warp still lying on the bottom of this harbor. Eight marks and a day of a boat to this harbor's bellwright paid on the twenty-ninth of April and a second eight marks and a day of a boat owing to the same man since the twenty-third of May, entered as owing and not as paid, and not paid and not argued away.

No delivery hose in this harbor and no air in this harbor. **A compressor's gland went onto the yard's own pump on Monday the nineteenth of May, out of a casting that is on a creditor's page at five marks and is not paid, and the five is still on that page and that page has not been re-written, and the gland has made no difference whatever, because the thing after the gland is the hose and there is not one in this harbor longer than about nine fathoms.** A hand lead's line made in the net loft on the second of May and made again and said on the twenty-fourth, in about nine words, to be the right line, with four knots in it that are the yard's and are not going to be taken out.

The ninth seam out with no end of it entered. The north berth out. The third lay full. The tide gang four men short. **A cistern behind an engine house charged on a rota on a Monday and a Thursday, and it was charged on Monday the twenty-ninth by three people of this yard and two men of the third lay who were not asked and not paid, and the harbor authority's own book records none of it, because a thing done for nothing is not a thing an office writes down.** The fire party's hose dead until the autumn. The mole-head light not on and not going to be, because a light costs oil and the repair account has been empty since the middle of February, and this office has had no boat of its own since the evening of the twenty-eighth of April, and the hard at Ashtide has been unanswered since the ninth of November, and the *Kittiwake* is on the hard with no cradle, and the *Sea Wren* is at the pile, and nineteen winters of dues are disputed and unpaid and are going up a coast in a file with the harbor's own account attached to them, and a man of the third lay, nine winters, out since the twenty-eighth of February, is not found.

And a lump of green glass in a bucket in a boat on this mole's crown with a finger's depth of harbor water in it, wetter than it was on the sixth of May, and two lines in a second column on two pages that are not on top of each other, written by two men, about two things, and no figure in either and no cause in either and no statement in either that anything knows anything.

**And on Wednesday the thirty-first of May there are three recoveries on this mole's steps that have to be named and costed before any one of them can be attempted, and the yard has a number in a bag and a number on a page and cannot do all three, and the third of the two numbers is thirty-one and has not been a different number since the tenth.**
