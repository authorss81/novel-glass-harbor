# Chapter 111

*A Bucket in a Boat*

The wind came round in the afternoon on Saturday the twenty-seventh of May and it did not come round the way the yard was used to. It came out of the west-southwest at about two o'clock with rain in it that was not cold, and by five it was west and it had gone up about half a point, and by the time it was dark it was doing the thing a westerly does in a harbor with a mole in it, which is to come over the crown in slabs and take the top of the crown off in a long smooth pull and take it again twenty seconds later.

It was not the weather of the twenty-sixth of April. That one was north-north-east for nineteen hours and came through the water and not off it, and this one was westerly and lasted about eleven, and a man on a mole at half past nine that night could have told the two apart in his own skin and would not have been able to tell anybody what either of them was.

They went up the steps at four o'clock with a lamp, because the bucket was on the crown under a tarpaulin weighted with two stones and a spare fender, and that tarpaulin was the only thing between a lump of green glass and nineteen hours of weather, and the second clause of the notice of the thirty-first of October keeps recovered transparent mineral out of every shed in this harbor, and this yard has not argued that clause and is not going to argue it on a Saturday in a blow.

**The boat was the harder half of it.** She lay at the north end above the twelfth, in the water, on this yard's own mooring, and in an easterly a mole stops a boat exactly where it puts her. There is a haul-off above the third seam's mark with a capstan in it and about sixty feet of stone crown, and it is above the reach of any tide this harbor has, and it is in the open air, and it is on the yard's own ground landward of the third seam's mark where no instrument of the harbor authority is required for a man to stand with a bar.

And there was no instrument at all to get her up there with, and not one of the reasons was a rule.

"There is no ninth," Calder Marr said. "**And that is the least of it. This office has a crane and the crane comes out of a repair account that has been empty since the middle of February, and a hand crane out of the net loft that went out on this office's account in January and is not in that loft now, and a tide gang four men short, and no boat of its own since the evening of the twenty-eighth of April.** So there is nobody in this harbor who can be asked, and I am not going to be the man who says that is a reason for leaving a boat where a westerly will find her."

"So we do it ourselves," Nessa Pike said, and was already coiling the tail. "**At about eleven o'clock at night, on our own capstan, with three men, two of whom I have hired for the night at no figure because I have not got a figure and I am not going to invent one, and the third of whom is you.** And I want the reason written down in our own book before the lamp goes out and not in the office's book, because this is a man's own account landward of the third seam's mark and we keep that practice ourselves and we are not going to have the two confused."

So they hauled her up on the capstan from about eleven until about one, and the crown of a mole in a westerly is a bad place to stand at midnight with a capstan bar, and twice the bar came up out of the socket and went back in and the second time it took two of them to get it in again. The two men Nessa had hired were the two of them, and they were paid in victuals out of the same tin the victuals come out of, and the tin has been empty since the twenty-eighth of April, and the victuals came out of the bag at a shilling and sixpence and neither of the two men was told that, and the reason is entered.

The boat sat on the haul-off with her stem up and a hand's breadth of daylight under her garboard and the bucket lashed in her with a line through the thwarts, and the tarpaulin went over the boat and over the bucket and was lashed to the gunwale, and the two stones and the spare fender went on top of that.

---

**The man of fifty-one came up the steps at about half past four in the afternoon and had a reason, and the reason was in this yard's book with a date on it, and the date was the twenty-second of May.**

*That on Monday the twenty-second instant, at the top of this mole's steps, at about ten in the morning, at the production of the writ, in the hearing of three men, a man of the *Grey Widow* of Morrow Quay, of no crew of this party and not of the Free Keel and not named on this page, said that on the twenty-sixth of April he had rung the handbell off this mole head at four o'clock in the afternoon in a half sea, that he had said in this harbor that he would be the man who rang it, and that he would be on this mole on Saturday the twenty-seventh; and that this party entered the whole of that in the hearing of three men and has not asked him one word about the twenty-sixth of April and is not going to.*

Nessa Pike wrote it, and Calder Marr read it back, and there were two men in the shed at the time and one of them read it back after him, and it went in the first column because a man on a mole can hear a man say a thing and be asked about it again on the mole tomorrow.

"He is not on the lead," Nessa Pike said, before anybody got wet and while there was still light.

"He is not on the lead."

"**He is not on the lead and he is not in the water and he is not being hired, and I want that in the book tonight in my words and not yours.** He is a man of another boat and he is in this harbor on his own account and he came up this mole because he said he would, and this yard is not going to put him on anything and is not going to ask him a question, and neither of those is a courtesy to him and neither of them is about his manner."

"It is not," Calder Marr said. "It is about our own book. **A man who has asked not to be told is not a man a yard can put on a lead and then write a reason about, and the yard's own book is a place where reasons go, and I am not going to be the reason that a thing about him ends up written in a column a man in a city is going to read.**"

"Then that goes in the second column with a reason against it," she said, "and the reason against the line is that no man in this shed can check what a man was asked not to be told about, and this yard has not been told it and is not going to be."

He stayed on the crown until about nine.

Nobody asked him anything. He was not on a lead. He was not in the water. He came up the steps at half past four because he had said he would, and he said nothing to anybody for four and a half hours except two things, and the first of them was at about seven and the second at about nine, and both of them were said in the same flat unhurried voice with the wind taking a third of each of them.

**"That's my wheel. Come his wheel round."**

Nobody in that yard heard it as anything but a man talking to himself, and nobody in that yard said so out loud, and Calder Marr heard it and could not say afterwards in what direction it had come from. The wind was on the port quarter. He was on the landward side of the man, at about four feet, with a lamp, and the lamp was on the seaward side, and he was certain for about a second and a half that it had come off the water and then certain for about the same length of time that it had come out of the man's own mouth, and the two certainties did not have a way of being in the book at the same time.

He had been like that since the eighth of December. He had been like it before that since the thirteenth of October. There was a ringing in both ears and under the ringing a second sound that arrived a fraction late, and on a good day a man could say which of the two was in the boat, and on a bad day he could not, and **there is no instrument in this harbor that will tell you which of the two you have got.** That had been a man of fifty's sentence, said on the water off the south end on the twenty-third of May, and it was put in the yard's second column in his own words on the same afternoon, and nobody in that crew asked him about it and nobody was going to.

"Write it," Nessa Pike said, at about one in the morning, when they were back in the shed with the wet still on them.

"Write what."

"Write what a man on a mole can check, which is that a man of fifty-one of the *Grey Widow* said a thing twice in a westerly, flat, on the crown above the third seam's mark, and that he said it at about seven and again at about nine, and that this party did not ask him one word about it and is not going to. That goes in the first column because you were four feet from him and two of us heard it and any one of us three can be asked about it on this mole tomorrow."

"And the other half."

"The other half goes in the second column, in your own hand, and it does not go on the same page as the line of the twenty-sixth of April, and the reason it does not go on the same page is that a man in a city is going to read two pages about two things and put them together, and **this yard does not know whether they are two things.**"

"And no cause," Calder Marr said.

"**No cause. And nothing in either line that knows anything.** A man said a thing twice. A boy on a roof heard his own voice behind him on the twenty-sixth of April, in a different wind, in a different month, in a yard where nobody had said anything, and that is the whole of the two pages, and if a man in a city wants a cause he can have this: two entries in a second column and no figure of any kind in either, and a statement that this yard has not got one and is not going to invent one at a cost of a night of a man's time."

He wrote it. He wrote it at about ten past two on Sunday morning and it took him a long time and the second column was the one he was slowest in.

**And the third column is the third column and nobody is in it.** It has a head on it with two times under it, written by a boy of twenty-two before he went off a stone, and a line under the head in Calder Marr's own hand on the twenty-first of May saying that no man in that party is filling it and that a column in that shape is the honest shape of this yard. There was no line under that one either, and there had not been for six days.

He filled it anyway, on Sunday morning, at about eleven, with the lateness entered.

*That this party has no man who reads a position back and is keeping the column anyway, and that the entry under it was read on the steps at about one o'clock on Sunday the twenty-eighth of May and was written in this book at about eleven o'clock the same morning, the two times being entered and the difference between them being about ten hours; and that the party is not going to pretend the two times are the same time and is not going to rename the column or scratch it out or stop using it, and the reason is that a column that a man in a city can check and a column that nobody is filling are both true and only one of them can be written down.*

Nessa Pike read it back. Calder Marr read it after her. There was nobody else in the shed to read it after that, and a column is not a place where a man puts the hand that is missing.

---

**The bucket was wetter and the yard said so on the page and did not do a single thing about it.**

They went up on Sunday morning at about nine and the tarpaulin was where they had lashed it and the boat was where they had left her, and the lashing on the after thwart had chafed a hand's width of tarpaulin open and there was water in the bottom of the boat and water in the bucket and the water in the bucket was about a finger's depth and it had been in there for some hours.

**The yard did not put a cloth into it. The yard did not take a cloth to it. The yard stood on a mole in the sunlight and looked at the water in a bucket and wrote the sentence down.**

*That on Sunday the twenty-eighth of May instant, at about nine in the morning, the object this party reported on the tenth was found in water in its bucket in the yard's own boat on this mole's crown, to a depth of about a finger, and that the water came over this mole's crown in the night of the twenty-seventh, and that the party has not dried the object, has not wiped it, has not put a cloth into it and is not going to, and the reason is that the second clause of the notice of the thirty-first of October keeps recovered transparent mineral out of every shed in this harbor, and a wet thing in a shed in this harbor is a thing in a shed in this harbor whatever is done to it, and the party has not argued that clause and is not going to argue it for the sake of a bucket of water.*

*And that the object is wetter than it was on the sixth of May, and that the party has not weighed it before or after because it is wet, and that the party enters the wetting as a fact and not as damage, and that the party does not know what a wetter lump of green glass is and is not going to find out this month.*

That went in the first column, in Calder Marr's own hand, and Nessa Pike read it back at about half past ten on the Sunday, at the top of the mole's steps, and Calder Marr read it after her, and the man of fifty-one was not there and had gone down the hill at about nine and had not said where he was going and had not been asked.

"Enter the other one," Nessa Pike said. "**Enter that a man of fifty-one stood on this crown from half past four until about nine in a westerly on a boat and a bucket that were not his work, and that he came because he said he would, and that this party did not put him on a lead and did not ask him one question, and that the party is not paying him and is not going to and did not offer.**"

"Both of those go in the first column and neither of them goes in the second," Calder Marr said. "A man on a mole can see a man stand on a mole."

"And the not-telling goes nowhere at all," she said.

"**The not-telling goes nowhere at all and I am not going to be the man who gives it a column, because the day a yard starts keeping a column on what it has not told a man is the day the column is about the yard instead of about the sea.**"

She looked at him for a moment in the sun on the steps.

"You have not been able to place that sound since December," she said, "and he has told nobody what he hears, and you have been four feet from him twice tonight and you did not say a word to him about either of them, and he did not say a word to you, and I have been standing on this mole for an hour and a half watching two men not say things to each other, and I am not going to be the woman who writes that down either."

He had the tarpaulin's edge in his hand and he did not let go of it for a moment longer than he needed to.

---

The two columns were read back at the head of this yard's own dock at about seven in the evening on Sunday the twenty-eighth of May. The wind had gone down at about four and the boards were wet and about nine people were in the street and none of them came and stood behind them, which is what a read-back in this harbor looks like.

Thirteen marks in a bag. Thirty-one on the chandler's note, and the three counts of that page at twenty-two, thirteen and eleven as last written and none of them moved. Nothing in a tin. Five lines and twenty marks in the wage column with the fifth of March the oldest and staying there, and the last line of that column is the fifteenth of May and the two Tuesdays after it went by with nothing in them, and the reason is on the page and the reason is that this yard has no reader.

**And a lump of green glass in a bucket in a boat on this mole's crown, with about a finger's depth of harbor water in it, in a wind of eleven hours that was not the wind of the twenty-sixth of April, under a tarpaulin chafed open at the after thwart, with a return about it made on the tenth of May on the day it fell due, and no return going up on either day of this weekend, because a day on a mole's crown with a bucket and a boat and a tarpaulin raises nothing and produces no return and a return about nothing is not a smaller return, it is not a return.**

Eleven logged occasions since the twenty-sixth of October, and two of them logged with no call in them, and neither figure moved this weekend and no occasion entered and none going to be.

**And it was Sunday night, and the cistern behind the engine house is charged on a rota on a Monday and a Thursday by a man who walks out to a sluice, and the harbor authority cannot pay a man to walk out to a sluice, and the man did not walk out on Monday the twenty-second, and the wall behind the reeve's bench comes down at eight in the morning whether anybody has called a sitting or not, and it is Monday tomorrow, and there is no sitting called, and the clerk keeps his own page.**
