# Chapter 131

*A Lead and a Slate, and a Thing Under the Mole*

The pot went out of the bag on Friday the seventh of July at about ten in the morning, two marks, at this yard's own two-mark rate, filled at the harbor authority's own compressor and carried up the hill on that office's own hose, and it is the whole of the air in this harbor and it is the fourth pot this yard has bought since the first of February and the second since the eighth of May.

**Two marks in a bag.** Thirty-one on the chandler's note, not re-written, the three counts of that page at twenty-two, thirteen and eleven as last written and unmoved since the fourth of January, and five marks of compressor's gland on it and not paid, and the tin empty and the tin has been empty since the twenty-eighth of April. **Eleven lines and forty-four marks in the wage column, the fifth of March the oldest and staying there, and six envelopes on a bench in the chest and not one of them anywhere.**

---

The market was full by nine and Sella Brigg was on the boards at about half past nine with a basket at her feet, and she had a figure written on the back of a slate with a piece of chalk, and she was not shy about it in front of anybody.

"**Twenty-six marks and a day and the twenty-eighth, and he says the twenty-eighth because that is when he can get the *Sallow Reach* out past the north wall and he is not going to do it on a Tuesday tide.**

"**And I have not told him no and I am not going to tell him no, and I have not told him about the eighth of March, because that is between me and my own rope.**"

Nessa Pike was at the back of the market and did not come forward.

"**Then that is your business and not ours, and we are not going to stand in the way of it, and there is nothing this yard can do for you that we have not already said out loud on these boards twice.**"

"**I know what you are going to say and I have read your sheet twice this week, and yours is not on this wall any more, and there is a man from Ashtide over the top of it with four marks at the head of it.**" Sella Brigg did not look round the market when she said it. "**And I am not saying that to be cruel. I am saying it because you put it there and somebody read it.**"

"**We put it there because the count was frightened and we have said so on a mole's steps in nine sentences, and if we had the four marks we would put a better sheet up over his and he would print that one, and that is the whole of what would change.**"

"**No it isn't,**" Sella Brigg said. "**It would change it for about a month.**"

And she picked up her basket and went and looked at the wall, which is three deep, and Calder Marr read out four marks and a note and thirty-one and a bag and eight shillings and tenpence, and none of it was hers and all of it was the truth, and nobody in that street argued with any of it, which is a thing that has happened about four times this year.

---

**The reason went in at ten past six on the Friday night for the Saturday, in Calder Marr's own left hand, in the yard's own book, and it is a working reason and not a return's reason and the two have never once been confused on this page.**

*That on Saturday the eighth of July instant this party goes out on its own account to the second seam's own face of this mole, landward of the third seam's mark, being ground no party claims and ground the first clause of the notice of the thirty-first does not reach, and that no instrument of any office is required for it and that this party keeps the practice in its own book anyway and is not going to have the two confused; that the purpose stated is four casts of the hand lead's line and a scrape of the foot of a hull lying against that face, which this party has been paid to look at by nobody and is looking at because it lies on its own ground and because the face has been sounded twice this summer and both times it has agreed with itself; that one man of this party goes in the water and the tender is the shipwright of this party, who has never been in the water; that the limit is the holder's own and is nineteen minutes and he set it himself at the boat and the clerk is not present and the absence is entered rather than filled in.*

"**Nineteen,**" Nessa Pike said.

"**Nineteen.**"

"**The last one was forty and you were inside it and you came up and you lost a thing you had when you were a child, and the figure on that page is in your own hand and I have read it.**

"**I am not saying nineteen is cowardly. I am saying that a man who sets himself a number in front of a woman is telling her the number and not the man, and if the number goes you will be at the mole's steps in an hour and I will be the one who has to read it back, and that is what I would be signing.**"

"**Then set it yourself and I will hold to it,**" Calder Marr said, "**and you will find out on Saturday whether I am telling you the number or the man.**"

---

The wind got into the west-southwest in the night and by seven on Saturday it was making four hours of run outside the mole head and the boat was working her way up the face in it and coming off it again.

**Before anybody was wet the six were read out in order at the boat, in daylight, on the second seam's own face landward of the third seam's mark, with the shipwright holding the line, and they go in the same words every time this yard goes in the water, and the reason for that is in the yard's own book and is that a man who reads his own six differently has a reason for it and does not know what it is.** Named tender who is not the diver. Independent line, on the boat's own bow. Fixed air limit on the slate, set at the boat by the diver and entered as the diver's, and it was nineteen and he set it himself and would not let the holder raise it. Seated named weight. A cutoff the tender can pull without asking anybody, and it is hers, and the word is *Enough*. And a stated reason, entered in the yard's own book before anybody went in the water, in the holder's own left hand, unedited, with the clerk absent and the absence on the page.

**At the top of the flood the hand lead's line, made in this yard's net loft on the second of May, badly once and made again, went down to eleven and a half on the mark.** That is the figure of the second of June and of the first of July and it is the figure a man in a city is going to print in about six weeks, and it agreed with itself for the third time and the yard entered the agreement as a fact and not as a proof, because three sets of four casts is twelve casts and a man who has kept a bar for nineteen years on four times a year does twelve in three years, and this yard has done twelve in five weeks and is not going to argue with anybody about which of the two is the better practice.

Then he moved his hand thirty feet south and forty feet down, because that is where the face breaks, and the lead went to **ten**, which is a fathom and a half under the mark and is a different bottom and not the same bottom.

"**Ten,**" said the shipwright, writing it on the slate. "**At forty feet south of the mark, thirty feet down the face. Go on.**"

He put it back on the same spot, four times, and got ten three times and **nine and seven-eighths** once, which is the lead's own answer and not the sea's, and he wrote the fourth one down without improving on it.

**And then he worked his hand down the face about two fathoms and found where the foot of that hull is, and that is a crevice where the mole's own stones at this seam come out and the side of the hull comes in, and the crevice is about four fathoms down and the top of it is clear because the face breaks above it and the fouling has never got over the break.**

---

**And then it snagged.**

Not a catch. A snag, the way a line snags on a thing that is not a rock: it went down straight and it stopped, all at once, without any weight in it, and the slack came up in his left hand in a heap and did not run out.

He held it. He put the spare turn on the line round the ring and took the weight off it and went down to it hand over hand on the face with about four feet of water over his shoulders, and the boat was being thrown a foot off the wall and coming back.

"**Hold,**" he said.

"**I am holding. I have been holding. I have got about four minutes of that pot and you have used two.**"

He got a hand down to it. It was not a rock and it was not a shackle. It was something with an edge on it that turned under his fingers, and it was thin, and it went into the stone and it came out of the stone and it went into the stone again, and there was more of it than he could hold in one hand.

He worked it up for eleven minutes.

**It came up at about twenty past eleven on a westerly of four hours, on the ring, in his own hands, and he put it on the deck of his own boat in the water and did not drop it and did not throw it and did not let go of it until Nessa Pike had hold of it, and that is entered because a man who is holding a thing in a seaway is a man who cannot be asked a question.**

---

It was about the length of a hand from the wrist to the middle knuckle.

**It was not iron.** Or it was iron, and it was not this harbor's iron, and after a hundred and forty days in four fathoms of salt water it was not rusted through, and the shipwright got her thumb on it and said one word and Calder Marr said the other one, and neither of them said the word out loud at the time because they did not have it.

"**That is not a thing this harbor casts.**"

"**It is an old thing,**" said the shipwright.

"**It is not a thing this harbor casts,**" Calder Marr said, "**and that is a first-column fact and you can take it to a smith this afternoon and he will tell you the same in about nine words, and the reason I want it in a smith's mouth and not mine is that I am not a smith and I have been wrong in public three times this year.**"

She turned it over on the boards with a cloth and did not use the cloth for anything except turning it over.

"**Three blows on the head of it,**" she said. "**Cut, not stamped. A stamped head has a die in it and this has a chisel in it and somebody hit it three times and stopped, and the third one is a different depth from the first two, which is what a hand does when it is tired and not what a die does.**"

"**Then it is a man's hand and not a yard.**

"**And it is a man's hand that is not going to be named today, and I want that on the page before anybody in this harbor starts guessing.**"

---

*First column, checkable by any man who can get off a mole in this harbor: that on Saturday the eighth of July instant, at about twenty past eleven in the morning, on a westerly of about four hours, on the second seam's own face of this mole, landward of the third seam's mark, at about four, in the crevice where the mole's own stones at that seam come out and the side of that hull come in, a piece of metal about the length of a hand from the wrist to the middle knuckle came up on a ring in the water; that it is not rusted through after a hundred and forty days in salt water, that the shipwright of this party says in her own hand that it is not a thing this harbor casts, and that a smith of this harbor has not been asked and is not going to be asked this week; and that its head carries three blows cut with a chisel and not stamped with a die, of which the third is a different depth from the first two; and that this party makes no claim to have dated it and no claim to have found out where it came from and no claim to know what it is joined to.*

*Second column, not checkable from a mole: that the person in the water could not, for about four minutes at the top of the flood on a westerly of four hours, tell which of the two hands on the line was holding it, and that he had both of them on the line, and that the reason this party enters that is that no man in a boat can check which of his own hands he was using at a given second, and that the party is not going to make a second column line of every ordinary thing a man cannot check and is making this one because it went with a day that lost an hour.*

---

**And they put it on a cloth on the bench and did not do one single thing to it that cost a mark, and the reason is on the page: a party with four marks in a bag and a note at thirty-one is not going to find out what a piece of metal is by paying somebody to find out, and a person of no office in this harbor who finds that out will say six marks and two days, and the six marks are not in the bag and the two days are two days.**

**And at about four in the afternoon Calder Marr walked down past the chandler's as far as the shed behind the pump house where a smith of this harbor works, with the piece of metal in his coat, and stood in the door of it for the better part of two minutes, and did not go in, and walked back up the hill, and the reason is on the page: a first-column line says a smith of this harbor has not been asked and is not going to be asked this week, and a man cannot un-ask a thing by standing in a door.**

**And at about six that night he wrote a line at the foot of the page and read it back to the shipwright and she read it back after him and there was nobody else in that shed to read it after that.**

*That on Saturday the eighth of July instant a man of this party brought up a piece of metal from the crevice at the foot of a hull lying against the second seam's own face of this mole, and that this party has entered the metal and the head of it and the place and the hour and the water it came out of, and that this party has entered no figure of what it is made of and no figure of how old it is and no name of anybody, on a line of its own in the second column with the reason against it, and that the reason is that this party is not able to check any of the three from a mole and is not going to pay six marks and two days to have one of them checked and is not going to have one of them checked for nothing by anybody who has not offered.*

"**Then what do we do with it,**" the shipwright said.

"**We go back on Monday morning and find out what it is joined to.**"
