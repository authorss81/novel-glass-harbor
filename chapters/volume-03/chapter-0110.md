# Chapter 110

*The Boat Off the South End*

Calder Marr put the difference on the page before anybody got wet, at about seven in the morning on Tuesday the twenty-third of May, in his own left hand, and Nessa Pike read it and the yard read it after her and there was nobody else in the shed to read it and he entered that as a fact and not as an omission.

*That on Saturday the twenty-ninth of April instant this yard bought a going out at eight marks and a day of a boat, at a fish market, out of four hands that were not this yard's and not the bellwright's, and the going out was done at about ten past three on Tuesday the second of May in a flat calm, with the bellwright, the holder, the shipwright and the master of the *Grey Widow* in the boat and a boy of twenty-two on the mole's steps with the hour, and it is spent.*

*And that on Wednesday the third of May instant, in a street at the head of this yard's own dock, to four men and a market, the holder said that a bell is going to be out in a boat in about four years at eight marks and a day of a boat then as it is now, and that he was telling the four of them then so that in four years there is no argument about it; and that sentence stands and is not to be broken or corrected by anybody, including this yard, including a man in this yard's own hand.*

*And that this is not a going out.*

**"A going out is bought, and named, and paid, and entered," Calder Marr said, "and it is eight marks and a day of a boat and it is the harbor's bellwright on the harbor's own ground on the harbor's own account, and this yard has no instrument for that man, did not buy one, and is not going to. This is a boat hired for a day and a man to row her, and the whole of the money for both of them came out of a day at a seam on Monday the eighth and a pump this yard let out of its own shed for two days at four marks a day, and those are the only two things this yard has earned since the first of February, and not one mark of either is in a going out and there never will be. Anybody in this harbor who hears me say a second going out is not a yard with a bell in it, and I am telling you now so that in four years there is no argument about it, and I am telling you now because I am the one who said it in a street."**

"And the bellwright," said Nessa Pike.

"He is coming because the ground is the harbor's and the gear is the harbor's and he is the harbor's bellwright, and he is not coming to be paid and there is no instrument in this shed by which we could pay him, and I am not going to invent one and put a mark against his name in a book a man in a city reads. **Eight marks and a day of a boat stands owing to that man a second time, entered as owing and not as paid, and it is the second time in six weeks and the first one was on a market in April.**"

---

**The boat was eight marks for the day and the man to row her was in the eight, and it is below what a boat is worth in this harbor and the man said so in one sentence and took it.**

He was a boatman of the second lay who had been in the market on Wednesday the tenth of May buying a pump, and who had walked down the hill on the Friday after the pump came back to ask whether there was a boat going off the south end, and had been told there might be one, and had come on the Tuesday.

"Eight for the day with me in her," he said. "A boat's day off this mole with a man to row her is eleven and I have charged eleven since I was twenty-four, and you are giving me eight, and I am telling you that out loud so that you know what you have got, and I am taking it because I have not had a week's work since the twenty-first of March and I would like the eight more than I would like to be right about the eleven."

"Then both of those go in the book," Nessa Pike said, "and both of them go in the first column, because you are a man in this shed and you can be asked about it again in a market in June, and I am not putting a shilling of it in the second column, and I have spent six months this spring putting shilling after shilling of this yard's arithmetic into a second column and I am glad of one in the first."

They were off the steps at ten past eight on a flat calm with the tide making, and the water off the south end was as flat as a wet floor. The top of the flood is the only hour a man can hear anything in this harbor, it was more than six hours off, and a boat hired for the day is a boat for the day, so they lay to off the south end and waited for it. It was not a going out, and it was done.

They lay to for the better part of six hours with the tide making under them and there was nothing whatever to do, which Calder Marr had not been in a boat to do since the eighth of December. He had spent the morning in a shed writing down the difference between two kinds of day, and he was going to spend the afternoon on a thwart with a man of fifty who had a question nobody had asked him, and no work in the world was going to make that easier.

So he did the thing he had been doing since he was twenty years old, which was to look at a mole and read it. Eleven feet of crown above the face. The tarpaulin and the two stones and the spare fender at the third seam, a mile off and not visible in this light. The ninth seam's marker at the eleventh with a ring of paint round it that has not been repainted since the thirtieth of October. And the south end itself, four hundred yards astern and closing, where a bell with no tongue on its frame was lying in eight and a half feet with a year's rope on it and forty feet of the harbor authority's own marline round its waist.

He had read a mole like that one since he was a boy, and he had never once read it the way a man reads a place he may have to go back to in the dark.

**Corvin Sarn went down the ladder with a line and a lantern at about a quarter to three and came up off it in four minutes, and he was not paid to be there, and he did not look away once, and nobody thanked him.**

The water was eight and a half feet by the pole, and the pole is the one with the mark cut in it in February, and it had not been looked at since.

"The fouling is there and it is about the same," Sarn called, "and I am not going to give you a figure and I will not have one given. **The mass of it is the same and the marks the flood has left in it are the same marks. That is the whole of what a man can say about a mass in nine weeks and it is a sentence and not a figure and I will not have it in a column as a figure.** The ground line is fouled where it was and I have not touched it, and there is forty feet of the harbor's own marline lying in it off this office's own moorings gear in February, and if this office ever wants it out it can have a diver and a bond. And the tongue is not on the frame."

**And at ten past three the tide bell went, three strokes, out of the third lay's wall, and the water off the south end of the mole gave it back a hand's width of a second later, and it did not move.**

Calder Marr turned his head for it out of thirty-one years of doing it, and the boy was not on the mole's steps with a slate and a stopwatch, and Calder Marr took the figure himself and did not write it down and could not have said, in that boat, on that water, at that hour, which of the two sounds was in the boat.

Nessa Pike did not turn round.

"You are going to say you cannot place it," she said, to the water, "and then somebody is going to give you a sentence for it, and then you are not going to know which of the two you have been given, and then it goes in the second column with a reason against it, and the reason is that no man in a boat can check where another man's sound went, and that has been the form of that line since the twenty-sixth of December. Say it."

"I cannot place it," Calder Marr said.

"There is a ringing in both ears and a second sound under the first that arrives late," Sarn said, "and I have had it since I was thirty-one and I have never had a name for it and I have never asked a doctor about it, and on a good day you can tell which sound is in the boat and on a bad day you cannot, and there is no instrument in this harbor that will tell you which of the two you have got tonight."

"And that goes in the second column in your own words," Nessa Pike said, "with the reason, and nobody is going to ask him about it, and I am not going to ask him about it, and there is nobody left in this yard who would think of it."

It went in that afternoon in Calder Marr's own left hand, with the reason, and the line is the form the line has had since the twenty-sixth of December and there is no name in it and there is not going to be one this year.

**Sarn stood in the boat with his hand on the gunwale and said the part of his account that was new, and only that, and he had given the rest of it in the same boat on the second of May and a man is not to be asked to say the same thing twice.**

"Three months and a half of water," he said. "That is not checkable from a mole and I am not going to make it checkable by guessing. The scour on the seaward face is not what it was in the seventeenth of February and I cannot tell you whether it is different better or different worse, and I said that once and I am not saying it again on the water. And the frame is cut through the casting on one side, and I have said what I am not going to say about what has been swinging against it, and I am not saying it today either: one man has seen it and one man has not and one man has been asked nothing and will not be asked. That is the whole of the second column and it is the same three lines I gave you in a shed on the first of May with the same reasons against them."

"That is a man of fifty saying he will not be asked," Nessa Pike said, "and it is a man of fifty saying it twice, and the yard is entering that he said it twice."

**And then the yard had the question ready and did not ask it, and said out loud why, and the reason was not that the man was difficult.**

Nessa Pike looked at Calder Marr across the boat and Calder Marr looked at the water, and it was Sarn who said it first, because he has said things off ladders before.

"You have had that in your mouth since the first of May," he said.

"I have."

"Then I will say my own words are still standing and still mean what they meant on the first of May, and I will say it in a room if somebody asks me in a room and not before, and I have not been asked and I am not going to say I have been." He put the lantern down. "I will say one thing about not being asked and then I will shut up about it. **The boy who could have read it back twice went off the stone on Monday the fifteenth, and I know that from a wall and a market and not from your mouth, and if a number comes out of me in a room in a fortnight and it goes into a book with a hand against it, then whoever reads that book in four years has got a number and a reason and the two times on the head of the column are both empty.** You have a yard that cannot write a thing down asking a man in a room to say a thing that cannot be written down. Ask me when you can write it."

"You may have it—" Calder Marr began, and stopped.

"You may have it when you can write it," Sarn said, "and not before, and I am fifty and I have been at this trade since I was a boy and I have never once been asked anything by a man in a shed and I have said out loud that I will say it in a room, and I have been in two boats for nothing this month and I am not going to be asked in a third one either."

**"Then I am not asking you," Calder Marr said, "and I am going to say the reason out loud on this water so that it is on the page in a form somebody can check, and the reason is the boy's hand and not your manner. A number said in a room on the twenty-third of May is a number with no hand to enter it and no second column to put a reason against, and a yard that cannot write a thing down is a yard that has no business asking a man in a room. That is the whole of it and it is not an insult to you and it is not a favor I am doing you by not asking, and I am not going to be thanked for it and neither are you."**

He had the answer, or most of one. It was a number, and it had been in his head since the twelfth of January, and there was nobody in that boat who could write it down. That was not why he was not going to say it and he knew it was not, and Sarn watched him not say it and let him go on not saying it, which was the most generous thing that man did in a whole afternoon.

---

They were on the mole's steps at half past three with the tide making and the wind in the south-east, and Sarn said the thing he had said in a shed doorway on the twenty-ninth of April, on his way off, and there is no end of it in a book.

**"The light on that mole head is a light this office pays for with oil out of an account that has been empty since the middle of February, and the third lay went dark on the nineteenth of March because a light costs oil, and a bell in the dark is a bell that is not there. Somebody in a room ought to be told that, and I am not the man to say it in a room, and I said it in a shed doorway in April and I am saying it now on a mole's steps and it is the last time I am going to say it where nobody has written it down."**

"Somebody was told in April," Calder Marr said. "It went on a market wall on the third of May in a sheet that four men read out. The somebody has read it. That is what happened to it, and I am not going to improve on it for you, and I am not going to pretend it was a good outcome, because it was a wall and a man went away and the light is not on."

"No," Sarn said. "It is not on." And he went down the steps.

---

**On the Wednesday the twenty-fourth the two columns were read back at the head of the dock in the evening, and there is no fourth man in this crew and there was not going to be. The third column has one line under the head, written on the Sunday in Calder Marr's own hand. The two times at the head of it were entered by a boy of twenty-two before he went off the stone, and the yard has worked out since, out of the hour in the first column, that the two of them are about two hours apart, and nobody in this crew can now say which of the two was the right one, and the reason went against the line.**

Then the yard went up to the net loft at half past ten at night, because a line made badly in that room on the second of May had been said in that room on the second of May to be right in a fortnight or not at all, and it was the twenty-fourth.

She was at the bench with her own warp and she did not put down the needle.

"I said on the second that it would be right in a fortnight or not at all, and on the eleventh that I would tell you in about a week, and it is the twenty-fourth, and that is six days past the week I gave you, because on the fifteenth a boy of twenty-two went off a stone in your yard and I was not going to stand in this room and say a thing about a line in that week." She turned the flax about a foot from the eye splice. "It is the right line. The four knots in it are the four knots a man ties when he is thinking about something else, and they are not wrong, and they are his, and I have not taken them out. Put that on your page and put my name to it and I will not be thanked, and if anybody asks me in four years I will tell them it is the right line and that I said so in about nine words on a Wednesday night in May, and that I said four extra words for nothing."

Corvin Sarn was in the doorway. He put a hand on the flax and left it there for about three seconds and said one word.

"Aye."

And neither of them said one word about that either.

The shed was shut. The note is at thirty-one, there is nothing in a tin, and there are thirteen marks in a bag — the same thirteen that were in it on the third of May, on the day a writ was granted and a boy was told in a room that he was going off a stone. The four hundred and forty pounds of old iron is not in the bag because it is never going to be. The bell-metal gun-port was not looked at that day and was not priced and was not offered to anybody, and a man of fifty who would know that casting in about four seconds was in a net loft on a Wednesday night and put a hand on a line and said one word. The question he has been carrying since the first of May was not asked. He is owed eight marks and a day of a boat for the second time in six weeks and is not paid and is not going to be asked to be. And the delay out of the water off the south end of this mole is a hand's width of a second and has not moved, and no man in this harbor can say why.
