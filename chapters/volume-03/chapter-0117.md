# Chapter 117

*A Boat Hand in June*

Elias Voke offered it in a market on the Thursday the eighth of June at about ten in the morning, on the boards, in daylight, to anybody who wanted it, which is the only way anybody in this harbor offers anything that is not for sale.

**"A season's hand in the *Anna Ray*, from the middle of June, on the herring and out of it, with a bunk and the boat's gear and a place in a fish market at the end of a day. Four marks and a share of what the boat takes, and I am telling you the share out loud because I have been on the other side of a share for nineteen winters and I would not do it to a boy of twenty-two.**"

"And what is behind it," Nessa Pike said, from about four feet away, "is a boat with nineteen winters of disputed and unpaid dues on the books of a harbor authority that is four men short, and a mole with a berth out of service at the north end and a third lay that is full. That is not a knock, Mr Voke. That is the answer to the question you have not been asked."

"That is the answer and I have given it before anybody asked and I would like it read back, because I am not a fool and I know exactly what I am standing in front of." He turned round. "**And I am asking in a market, and not in a shed, and not at a man's door, because a man who is offered a thing in a shed is a man who is going to be told he is not ready for it.**"

There were about thirty people on the boards. Two of them were of this yard and one of those two was carrying a coil of hemp and had come down to buy it.

---

**The boy was in the market at about half past ten on the Thursday morning, and he was on the boards, and he was twenty-two years old, and he had not been on them since the fifteenth of May.**

Calder Marr was at the fish end with a coil of hemp under his arm and about fifty feet of open boards between himself and the man of the third lay, and he did not go over, and he stood where he was for about a quarter of an hour and watched a boy he had paid four marks a week for two hundred days stand in a market and listen to a herring skipper.

Somebody said it in the market, and Calder Marr heard it, and it was said by a man of the third lay who was not in the yard and was not asked by anybody in the yard.

**"You want a road, you have got no legs for it and you have got no money for it and you are two and a half men on a hill, and there is a boy of twenty-two standing on my boards who read a book for two hundred days and can hold a line in weather, and I am telling you what this harbor has lost and you did not lose it and neither did I."**

It went in the yard's book in the second column, with the reason that a man in a market cannot be checked, and it went in the first column too, because he said it on the boards and there were thirty people there and any one of them can be asked about it on the boards tomorrow.

*That on Thursday the eighth of June instant, in a market, in about thirty people's hearing, a man of the third lay said out loud that this harbor has lost a reader, a hand, a third column, a wage of four marks a week that was a wage and is now a habit, and a house with a leak in it that was carrying four men and is carrying two and a half; and that the party has entered the whole of that in his own words and has not denied one word of it, and the reason it is in the first column is that thirty people heard it and any one of them can be asked about it tomorrow.*

---

They were in the shed at about seven that evening and Calder Marr wrote three lines down and read them out loud to the shipwright before he put them in the book, which is the practice, and which is the reason the two of them are the only two people who know what is in this book tonight.

*First. That this party does not write to Bevin Rook. Second. That this party does not go to see him. Third. That this party does not ask any person who has seen him what he said to them.*

*And the reason against all three of them, and it is one reason and it is the yard's own and it is not about the boy: that a party which has had a boy of twenty-two write down what a man said on a mole's steps for two hundred days does not get to use the same hand and the same book to ask him a question about himself, and that the whole of what this yard owes him is in that book already, being forty marks of claim standing whole and a wage at four marks a week going into an envelope on a bench every Tuesday, and that neither of those is a reason to knock on a door.*

"And I want the other half of it in as well," Nessa Pike said, "because a page with a rule on it and nothing under it is half a lie."

"Go on."

"**Forty marks is forty marks. It stands whole and it is not reduced and it is not asked to be reduced, and a wage of four marks a week is a wage and is not a reduction of the forty and is not offered as one, and neither of those is a claim on his June.** A boy who takes a berth and comes back in September is a boy who took a berth, and a boy who takes a berth and does not come back is a boy who took a berth, and there is nothing in this harbor that can be put against either of those sentences except the fact that we are sorry, and we are not going to put our sorry on a page and call it a claim."

"And the wages," Calder Marr said. "We are putting four marks in an envelope every Tuesday for a man who is not on the stone."

"Yes, and I am not going to stop you, and I am going to write down why I am not going to stop you, and it is not sentiment. **An envelope is not a knock. A wage is not a visit. And the day this yard uses a wage as an excuse to go and see a man it has owed money to for seven months, that is the day the column is worth nothing and a man in a city can be lied to in it without anybody lying.**"

The two lines went into the second column with a reason against each of them, and the three nots went into the first column because the two of them said them out loud in a shed and can be asked about it tomorrow.

**He did the third column afterward, on his own, and it took him the best part of an hour, and the lateness went in with it.**

*That on Thursday the eighth of June instant, at about eight in the evening, this party entered in the third column, which was read on the steps at about seven and written in this book at about eight, the whole of what a man of the third lay said in a market; and that the third column is not being renamed and is not being scratched out and is not being quietly stopped, and that the party is going to go on putting into it every figure the yard cannot read back to itself, and that the difference between the two times on the Thursday is about an hour and the difference between the two times the boy of twenty-two used to put at the head of it was about two hours, and that the party has entered both differences and has not entered a reason for the change because it does not have one; and that the reason the party is still filling the column is that a yard which stops is a yard whose next page is written by whoever is standing nearest, and this yard has decided that if that is going to happen it will happen in a column with two times at the head of it and not in a column without.*

Nessa Pike read it back at half past nine and Calder Marr read it after her, and there was nobody else in the shed, and he did not pretend otherwise and she did not ask him to.

"**You are going to have to get somebody to read these back," she said. "Not a boy. Somebody. A hand that will last a week.**"

"I know. I have known since the fifteenth of May and I have not said it out loud in this shed because saying it out loud in this shed makes it a thing we are doing instead of a thing that has happened to us." He shut the book. "And I am not going to hire a man to read back figures on a Tuesday for four marks a week when I cannot pay four marks a week."

"You are going to have to, or you are going to have to stop, and I do not care which and I would rather you hired."

---

And then the thing that is the truth about the offer, which is not generosity and which none of the three of them had said out loud until a man said it in a market at about eleven o'clock and Calder Marr was fifty feet away with a coil of hemp in his arms.

**"A boat hand in June is a man who can hold a line in weather."**

A man of the third lay, in the middle of the boards, to nobody.

"And that is a stone. **That is a stone, and it is a wet stone, and it is the stone the bonesetter's book was talking about when it said a boat hand in June, and a boy with a hand that has been going since the fourth of April can hold a line on a herring boat in June for four marks and a share and a bunk, and the harbor that has written *he is not to be sent, his hand is going* in a ledger on the first of May in front of a clerk is the same harbor that is now offering him the stone.**"

Calder Marr stood in the middle of the fish market with a coil of hemp in his arms and did not move for about a minute.

*That this party has entered, on the boards on the eighth of June, in a man's own mouth and with his name against it, that a boat hand in June is a man who can hold a line in weather and is therefore a stone; and that this yard's own objection to the bonesetter's sentence of the fourth of April was that the boy is not to be sent and his hand is going; and that this yard has not argued the difference and is not going to, and has entered that it has not argued it, and the reason is that a man of twenty-two with a hand that is going is the best judge of a line in weather of any of us and this yard is not going to be the man who tells him what his hand is for in a market.*

He put the coil down on the boards and paid for it and did not go over to the boy, and the boy did not look over, and that is the whole of what passed between them in a market on the eighth of June and neither of them has said so out loud since.

---

**On the Friday the ninth the yard bought a pot of air off this harbor authority at its own two-mark rate, filled at the office's compressor and carried on the office's own hose, and a hundred fathoms of hemp for the lead line, and both of those came out of the bag on the Friday morning, and neither of them was ever going to be in it again.**

**Five marks in a bag.** Thirty-one on the note. Nothing in a tin. Seven lines and twenty-eight marks in the wage column and the fifth of March the oldest and three envelopes on a bench in the chest and not among the five rolled in oilcloth.

No air in this harbor and no delivery hose and a gland fitted to the yard's own pump and not paid for, and a pot of air bought for a Saturday and it will be the whole of the air in this harbor on Saturday. The ninth seam out, the north berth out, the third lay full, the tide gang four men short, the cistern charged on both of its days this month by people nobody pays, the fire party's hose dead, the light not on, no boat of this office of its own, the *Kittiwake* on the hard, the hard at Ashtide, nineteen winters, the *Sea Wren* at the pile, eleven copies and four unreached holders not asked, a man of the third lay nine winters not found and one line in this book about him. A lump of green glass in a bucket in a boat on this mole's crown. Two lines in a second column on two pages, and no figure in either and no cause in either, and eleven logged occasions since the twenty-sixth of October with two of them empty, and neither moved.

**Two and a half men, confirmed in this yard's own book and in front of people, and a third column with a head on it that this yard intends to keep and cannot yet fill, and about four hundred feet of a woman's own warp on the bottom of this harbor with her name and a figure and a date on a market wall since yesterday.**

And at about one o'clock in the afternoon on the Friday the ninth of June, in the market, the boy of twenty-two shook hands with a man of the third lay, in front of about nine people, and one of those nine was thirty feet away with a coil of hemp he had already paid for, and he did not go over.
