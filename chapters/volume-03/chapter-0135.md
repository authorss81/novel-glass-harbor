# Chapter 135

*Six Marks and Two Days, and Six Things She Can Prove*

She was on the mole's steps at about eleven on Saturday the fifteenth of July with a book under her arm and a lens on a cord round her neck and a pair of brass calipers in a fold of oilcloth, and she had been in this harbor since about the thirtieth of June, and nobody in this yard knew and nobody had asked.

"**I have been above the chandler's since the Wednesday before the last,**" Mara Venn said, before anybody had said anything at all, "**and I have not come up this hill and I have not asked anybody for anything, and I am going to be here for about four days and then I am going, and I am telling you that at the top so that nobody in this yard has to work out whether I am waiting to be useful. I am not. I have not been useful since the nineteenth of May and I have not been asked to be and I would like that entered in whatever you keep, because it has been three months and it is going to be a thing somebody reads about you and not about me.**"

"**Then it goes in the first column,**" said the shipwright, "**because you said it on a mole's steps in about nine people's hearing and any of them can ask you tomorrow whether you said it.**"

"**Good. I will read it back, and if you put my name on it I will take the pencil, and you know that.**"

---

"**Now the price, and I am going to say it before I do anything and not after,**" she said. "**Six marks and two days to have anything entered at a counter on this coast. That is my figure and it is the figure on every coast and it does not go down because the person is difficult. I am not going to have anything entered at a counter. I am going to look at what is on your bench and I am going to look at the crevice from a boat, and neither of those costs a counter fee, and the two days are two days of mine and not two days of yours, and if you try to make them two days of yours I will stop.**"

"**Nobody is going to make them anything of ours.**"

"**Then I will do it, and I want it written down in words I have chosen, and here they are: a finding of no office in a yard's own book, unsigned, with a reason on the page, given on my own account, and not to be paid, and not to be thanked, and not to be treated as a favor, and if any of those three words appears in your book I will read it out in a market.**"

Nobody wrote it down wrong.

---

**She went out in the yard's own boat at about half past twelve with one man on the oars and no pot and nobody in the water, and it was the top of the flood, and she lay on the gunwale over the crevice with the lens in her right eye for about twenty minutes and said four words in that time, and two of them were somebody's name.**

They came up at one o'clock and the shipwright asked her one question on the steps, and it was the only question she asked all day.

"**What is the packing made of.**"

"**I do not know,**" Mara Venn said, "**and I have been looking at it for twenty minutes and I can tell you four things about it and not one of them is what it is made of. That is not modesty. That is a trade. Do you know what your weir is made of.**"

"**Stones and stakes.**"

"**And what is under your weir.**"

"**Mud.**"

"**Then I will not tell you what that packing is made of until somebody stands a caulker next to it. Go on. You have a lump of it on a bench and a chip of glass out of a lead's tallow, and you have not asked me one question that I can answer, and I have about two days and I am going to spend them on the two days I have got.**"

---

**On Sunday the sixteenth she was in the shed from ten until about four, and she would not let anybody else in the room, and the reason she gave was that a thing said in a room with a man in it is a thing a man in a city can be told about, and there was nobody else in the shed anyway except the two of them and Alden Marr at the far bench with his back to them.**

She did it in order. She would not do it out of order and she said why: **a conservator who gives you her findings in the order she finds them convincing is giving you her opinion with a rule on it.**

*First. That the top of that crevice is not one timber and not two timbers. It is a scarf, cut long and hollowed to a many-sided hollow, and one side of it is cut to the other side of it, and a scarf that is cut to the thing it is going into was cut by a man who had already measured the thing he was going into, and that is true and a shipwright of this harbor can go and look at it in ten minutes with a rule and will not need me.*

*Second. That the packing and the fastenings are one age with each other, being the same batch and not two batches, and the reason is that they have gone the same color and the same way on the same face.*

*Third. That both of them went in over a fouling that was already there, being about three inches of shell and weed on the seaward side of that crevice and not on the landward side, and the landward side of that crevice is clean stone and has been dry-footed in some seasons and wet-footed in others and has never had three inches on it.*

*Fourth. That the packing came in from the seaward side. There is a runnel of it running out of the joint and thinning seaward, and the seaward side of the joint is the hard side, and a man packing a joint presses from the side he is on.*

*Fifth. That there are nine fastenings in about seven feet of the joint at a hand's breadth apart, and that every one of the nine carries three blows on the head of it, cut with a chisel and not stamped with a die, and that the third blow on each of the nine is a different depth from the first two, and that the nine are the same three blows.*

*Sixth. That there is a chip of green glass in the packing, about the length of a thumbnail, sea-worn to a frosted face on one side, with a scalloped edge of about three in about half an inch, all the same hand — and that it is the same glass as the chip that came out of the tallow of your lead on the Monday — and that it was put in on purpose, by somebody, deliberately, at the moment the packing went in, and I will tell you why I will say that in a conservator's own words and not in a fisherman's: **if a man has a chip of glass in his hand at a boat he is packing a joint, it goes in the packing anywhere, and it goes in the seam by accident about once in a thousand, and I have seen it happen twice in nine years and neither of those two was in a seam that somebody was packing on purpose for a job.***

---

**And then she stopped and she said the other thing, and she said it before anybody asked it, and the two of them had not been going to ask it at all.**

"**I have just given you six things and I can prove all six and any one of them can be checked, and I am not going to give you a seventh, and I am not going to give you a name.**

"**A joint is made by a body and two hands and a decision. A decision is not a mark. A body does not leave anything on a thing that a trade can read, and I have been at that crevice for two days with the best lens in this harbor and there is no name on it and there is no trade on it and there is not going to be one, and anybody in this coast who tells you a conservator can tell you who made a thing is selling you a thing. That is my whole trade and I have just told you the boundary of it and I have never told anybody the boundary of it before because nobody has ever paid me enough to make it worth saying.**"

"**We have not paid you anything since October,**" said the shipwright.

"**No,**" said Mara Venn. "**And that is on your page and not on mine.**"

---

"**Now the last of it, and this is the part I would like a man in a city to read, and I am not going to pretend I have read it and I am going to tell you why I cannot.**

"**There is a thing in that crevice that was put there on purpose by somebody who expected somebody to come. That is a glass. You cannot read it in a shed and you cannot read it in a boat and you cannot read it with a rule, and I could not read it either if I went into that water, and I am not going to, and the reason is not courage, the reason is that the trade that can read what a thing carries costs six marks and two days and there is no discount for a mole. You had a chip in the tallow of a lead a week ago and none of you can tell me what is in it. I can tell you that is what is in it. That is where my competence stops and I would like it written down with a rule on it in the second column, because the second column is the only place in this book where a person of my trade is allowed to say *I do not know* and mean it.**"

She wrote it herself and she did not sign it, and the reason she did not sign it is on the page in her own hand and has been on the page in her own hand since the seventh of April.

*Unsigned, at a conservator's own request, on a finding given on her own account and not to be paid. Reason: a finding of no office carries no seal and no office and cannot be produced on demand by anybody, and a signature would make it a document, and this is not a document, it is what a woman with a lens and a pair of calipers of her own could see in four days.*

---

**And at about six that evening, at the head of the dock, with the market gone in and about nine people in the street, the two columns were read out, and the shipwright did the reading of it because Mara Venn said she would not read at the head of a dock and gave the reason, which is that a person reading their own finding out loud in a market is a person asking a market to believe them, and that is the one thing a person of her trade cannot do in public and get anything for.**

"**Two marks in a bag and nothing has moved in it since the Tuesday,**" said the shipwright. "**Thirty-one on the note, not re-written, the three counts of that page at twenty-two, thirteen and eleven and unmoved since the fourth of January, and five marks of compressor's gland on it and not paid, and the tin empty since the twenty-eighth of April. Twelve lines and forty-eight marks in the wage column, the fifth of March the oldest, and seven envelopes on a bench and not one of them anywhere, and the next of them is four marks on Tuesday the eighteenth of July.**"

"**Stop,**" said Mara Venn.

The shipwright stopped.

"**Say that one again. The one about the conservator.**"

"**Forty marks to a conservator with no office, first after food, unpaid since the fifteenth of October, not reachable by the writ, not an assistant, not asked to be useful, and not one mark of it paid out of anything that has come into this yard since the eighth of May.**"

"**First after food,**" said Mara Venn. "**Say it again slower and say who put it there.**"

"**The yard put it there.**"

"**Then say the date on it.**"

"**The fifteenth of October.**"

"**Nine months,**" said Mara Venn, "**and I have had bread every day of it, and that is not a complaint and it is not a plea and I am not asking any man in this street for anything, and I would like the ninth month said out loud at the head of a dock in about nine people's hearing, because in about two years there is going to be a page in a book in a city that says what a conservator of no office costs a town that owes her money, and that page is going to be read, and the number on it is going to be nine months, and it is going to be nine months because of this yard and not because of the four hundred offices.**"

Nobody said anything, and she went up the boards, and the two of them went on with the reading.

**And what is left of it: eight marks and a day of a boat to this harbor's bellwright owing since the twenty-third of May, unpaid, unargued, not paid in a month, and a bell is going to be out in a boat in about four years at eight marks and a day of a boat then as it is now and that sentence stands. Eight shillings and tenpence standing to each of two women, in no sentence together. Forty marks of claim to a boy of twenty-two, standing whole. No air in this harbor and no delivery hose. The ninth seam out with an end of it entered for the first of September. The north berth out and the third lay full. The light not on. The hard at Ashtide. Nineteen winters in a naval file and in a letter. Eleven copies and a printed sheet over the top of them. A man of the third lay, nine winters, not found, and this yard has not gone.**

**And a day at the second seam on Monday the seventeenth on a woman's own account, and on Wednesday and Thursday a man of the *Grey Widow* is going to be asked in person whether he will go in the water on his own account to scrape a fouling off a joint, and he is the fourth person on a writ of this party and he was asked in person on the first of May and said he was content to be on it, and he is not of this party and is not of any association and is not to be paid and does not want to be.**
