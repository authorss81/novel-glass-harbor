# Chapter 136

*Two Talks at Once, and the Wrong Thing Said at the Head of the Dock*

The wall behind the reeve's bench came down at eight on Monday the seventeenth of July and it came down bare, and that was eleven Mondays, and there was no sitting and the clerk kept his own page and did not mention it.

**And the cistern was not charged, and the reason is on the page and it is a working reason and it is in this yard's own hand.**

*That the rota falls on a Monday and a Thursday and that this party has walked it ten times since the end of May at no figure and that the gate takes four hours with three men, and that on Monday the seventeenth instant this party was on the second seam's own face from seven in the morning until about two on a woman's own account, and that the party could not walk a four-hour gate and do a day's work at the same time with the same three men, and that the party has not walked that gate and has entered that it has not walked it, and that the cistern has held since the nineteenth of June and this party is not going to be thanked for that and is not going to use it for anything.*

---

**Edric Pringle's day.** She said the cause out loud before she said the figure, which is how she has always done it.

"**The face is not shut for looking at it. I want to know what I am buying. A day of scraping a fouling off a joint is a day's work and I will pay a day's work, and if it is a day of looking I am not going to have it and neither are you.**"

"**It is both,**" said Calder Marr, "**and we have said so to each other, and I told you on Wednesday it was going to be the busiest three days this yard has had since the first of May and that one of them was going to go wrong.**

"**The face is clear. The crevice is clear. What is in front of the crevice is fouling and it is the fouling that has to come off to be read, and it comes off at the top of the flood and only at the top of the flood, and there is one top of the flood a day.**"

"**Then I have bought a day and not three,**" said Edric Pringle, "**and you have told me a thing about my own money that I could not have worked out, and I am not going to say thank you for it and I am going to say the rest of it.**

"**Eight shillings and tenpence. Since the sixth of February. Undiminished. And I am not going to be one of those two women you keep not putting in a sentence together, and if I ever am, I will come up that hill and I will not be quiet about it.**"

Gross on the board seventeen. The eighth two. The share two. The victuals of the day two, and a hired hand three and a second hired hand three, paid at the mole by the woman who hired the day, that being twelve out of seventeen and the yard's being five. **The pot of air came out of the bag on Friday the seventh and is not charged to this day, and about ninety fathoms of line came on the ring, and nothing else came off it.** That is two marks less than the same day of work brought on the twenty-fourth of June, and the yard wrote the two figures side by side on the mole's steps in front of everybody without saying which one it hoped for.

**Five marks in a bag, and they are the first marks to come into this bag since the tenth of July, and they came in this morning.**

The six were read out in order at the boat before anybody was wet, in daylight, on the second seam's own face landward of the third seam's mark, with the shipwright holding the line. Named tender who is not the diver. Independent line, on the boat's own bow. Fixed air limit on the slate, set at the boat by the diver and entered as the diver's, and it was seventeen and he set it himself. Seated named weight. A cutoff the tender can pull without asking anybody, and the word is *Enough* and the word is hers. And a stated reason, written into this yard's own book before anybody was wet, in the holder's own left hand, unedited, with the clerk not present and the absence on the page rather than filled in.

---

**He went down at about nine and the day went like this and it is entered as it happened and not as anybody would have wanted it.**

The two hired men cleared the fouling off the top of the crevice with flat irons and put it in a basket. The crevice came open about two feet of joint. The joint came clean in about half an hour and it was, in the plainest terms in the world, a scarf cut long and hollowed to a many-sided hollow, and there were nine fastenings in about seven feet of it at a hand's breadth apart, and the shipwright counted them twice from the boat and got nine both times and wrote nine on the slate.

**And at about ten, on his second call down, he put his left hand flat on the cut where the two ends of it meet, and he stopped counting the boat.**

He has put a thumb on a mended thing in this harbor perhaps forty times in eleven years. A scarf is a scarf. So is a clamp and a spike and a butt and a carvel, and every one of them is a man in his own trade on a particular afternoon, and a man who can name four yards on this coast can feel which of the four it was most days.

**What came up under his hand on the seventeenth of July, on his second call down, inside a limit of seventeen minutes that he had set himself, was not that.** It came up as a break, and the break was not in the wood and not in the fastenings and not in the packing. It was in the two cuts. The hollow on the north side of that joint and the hollow on the south side of it have different ages in them, and the difference is not two days and is not two years, it is the difference between a cut made once and a cut made on top of a cut that was there first, and it came into his hand the way a change of angle comes into a hand, which is without any picture at all and is simply *that way and not this way*, and there was no sound with it and nothing arrived before its source and he was not in a storm.

**And then, sitting at four fathoms with his hand on it, he did the thing that took the rest of his day: he made the old mending into a fact. Not the mending. The *age* of it, the *number* of mendings, and the *hand* that made them. He supplied all three. The hand on the wood told him none of them.**

He was in the water four times after that, seventeen minutes each, and the limit was his and he kept it four times out of four, and the day went on without him. The two hired men worked. The fouling came off in baskets. The basket went up the face and came down and went up again, four times, and he did not know how many, and the shipwright knew and he did not, and when she said *four* to him at about eleven he said *three* to her, and she said it again, and he said it again, and then he stopped talking and put both hands flat on the joint on the bottom and did not take them off for about nine minutes of the seventeen.

At about eleven the boat came off the face.

**Two hours and a quarter before the flood.** The tide at the second seam's face on the seventeenth of July has half an hour of slack at about eleven in the morning and the flood is not good at that face until twenty past one, and the two hired men had been in the water since nine and were cold, and the pot was at about a third, and the holder had lost the count of the boat and not the count of himself.

They came up. The day was charged at seventeen gross with twelve of it gone at the mole by the woman who hired it, and the yard's five is in the bag, **and the crevice was clear for two feet and the fouling below it was not off, and there is one top of the flood a day, and there are six more floods before the fouling on that seaward face can be read with a rule.**

---

"**The book can be checked in a morning, so I am going to do it now while I have got it,**" Calder Marr said, "**and I am going to say what I have got in the order I got it, and the order is the order.**"

"**Tuesday the eighteenth, at the top of the flood, on a party of this party, on the second seam's own face landward of the third seam's mark, on a pot of air bought at the harbor authority's own compressor on that office's own hose at this yard's own two-mark rate, being the whole of the air in this harbor this morning.**"

The same six, in the same order, on the same boat, with the same tender and the same word, and the reason of that order read out in the same words because a man who reads his six conditions differently on Tuesday than he did on Monday has a reason for it and does not know what it is. Named tender who is not the diver. Independent line, on the boat's own bow. Fixed air limit on the slate, set at the boat by the diver and entered as the diver's, being seventeen. Seated named weight. A cutoff the tender can pull without asking anybody. And a stated reason, entered in this yard's own book before anybody was wet, in the holder's own left hand, unedited, with the clerk not present and the absence entered rather than filled in.

**Two men were in the water and neither of them was hired. One of them was of this party. The other was Tam Slee of the *Grey Widow* of this harbor, who is the fourth name on the writ of the first of May, who was asked in person on the first of May and said he was content to be on it, and who is not of this party and is not of any association and is not to be paid and did not want to be, and who said so himself on the oars before he came alongside.**

---

**They scraped the fouling off the seaward side of the joint and read it with a rule.**

The work is ordinary. A flat bar laid flat across the seaward face of the joint, a scraper, and two men with their hands in the water at about four fathoms holding the bar, and a rule put in against the stone and read off the top of it by a third person on the gunwale. It cannot be done at any other state of tide, it cannot be done faster, and it cannot be done by anybody who will not put a hand on a stone that has been in salt water for a hundred and forty days.

**There are six joints. There is one top of the flood a day and the boat will not stay on that face at any other hour, and six readings at one a day is three days, and the third day is Thursday the twentieth, and there is a pot of air and a day of a boat for it and this yard has not got six marks or a day of a boat for anything else this week and has entered that it is not going to try.**

**The first of them, which is at the root end of the crevice, and which is therefore the north one, read a quarter of an inch. The second, which is thirty-three feet south of the first, read a quarter of an inch.**

He wrote the two out on the slate in the order he had taken them and did not sort them, because the man who took them had not known what he was taking them for, and a man who sorts his own figures before he has said out loud what they are is a man putting a name on a thing.

---

**And at about half past three on the Tuesday afternoon, at the head of this dock, with the market going in behind them and about eleven people in the street, Calder Marr said the thing out loud, and he said it in a market, and he said it to nobody in particular, and it was the strongest thing he has ever said in the open air.**

"**That is a chain.**

"**Six joints and they run the length of this mole and the spacing is the same at every one of them and the fastenings are the same at every one of them, nine in about seven feet, and I have never in my life seen a thing like it and neither has anybody in this harbor and neither has anybody in this coast that I have ever met in a boat.**"

"**And it was made once. It was made along this mole, from the north end down, and the whole of it is one time and it is one set of hands, and I would put the two ends of it against a wall in this harbor and it would run true.**

"**And whatever it is for, it is not to keep two boats off the bottom in a blow. I have been in that crevice and there is nothing in that hole that will hold anything in a sea. It is to hold two things together that somebody wanted to go on being one thing. That is not a repair. That is a man tying a knot.**"

---

**And the shipwright did not stop him, and that is entered against her and it is entered with a reason.**

"**I am not going to write down what I was thinking at that moment and I am going to write down the reason I did not open my mouth, because the reason is better than what I would have said.**

"**I did not stop him because a man saying his own trade out loud in a market is not doing anything wrong, and because he has got the crevice and the count and the chip and the letter and the return, all of them this month, all of them first, all of them on the day, and a man with four in a row does not get contradicted by a shipwright on a Tuesday in a market about a mole four fathoms under their boots.**"

"**And I did not stop him because if I had, he would have stopped, and he is the one who goes in the water.**"

She wrote it in the second column, with a reason against the line, in her own hand, and read it back, and he read it back after her.

*Second column, not checkable from a mole: that a person of this party said in a market on Tuesday the eighteenth of July instant at about half past three in the afternoon, in about eleven people's hearing, that six joints in a crevice on the second seam's own face are a chain, and that they were made once, in one time, by one set of hands, from the north end downwards; that the party has entered the whole of what was said and has not shortened it; and that the reason the party did not stop him is that a man saying his own trade in the open air is not doing wrong, and that he has been right in public four times this month, and that a shipwright who contradicts a man in a market is a person who has decided that a man's mouth is a thing to be managed, and this party has not decided that.*

*And that this line goes in the second column and not the first, and the reason is that a man can stand on this mole's steps tomorrow morning and count nine fastenings in about seven feet and measure the crevice and look at the fouling with a rule, and none of that will tell him what the man meant by the word chain, and that is exactly what the second column is for, and that is exactly the reason the party is frightened of what it has just heard.*

---

**And a pot of air went out of the bag on the Tuesday at about two marks, which is the whole of the air in this harbor and the second pot this week, and the wage went in at eleven in the morning at four marks, and the column is at thirteen lines and fifty-two marks with the fifth of March the oldest, and there are eight envelopes on a bench and not one of them has been anywhere.**

**One mark in a bag, on Tuesday the eighteenth of July, at about six in the evening, and that is the whole of the money of this yard.**

Thirty-one on the note, not re-written. Nothing in a tin. Forty marks standing whole to a boy who is on a herring boat. Forty marks to a conservator of no office, first after food, unpaid since the fifteenth of October, and she was on a mole's steps on Saturday and read at the head of a dock on Sunday and has not been paid one mark and has not asked. Eight shillings and tenpence apiece to two women, undiminished, and the two debts are in no sentence together in this book. And the eight marks and a day of a boat standing to this harbor's bellwright since the twenty-third of May. No delivery hose and the longest thing of hose in this harbor about nine fathoms. A cistern not charged on Monday because the gate takes four hours with three men and the same three men were in the water. A day bought on Monday that did half of what it was bought for, and the half that did not get done is on the page in the hirer own words and the figure she was given in advance.

**And a man of the *Grey Widow* in a boat on his own account on his own time, not paid, not of this party, not of any association, who came because he is the fourth name on a writ and was asked in person on the first of May and said he was content to be on it, and who said out loud at about four o'clock on the Tuesday, to a shipwright, in a boat, with his hands wet:**

"**Your man said a thing at the top of the steps and I heard it and I want you to know that I have heard it, and that on Wednesday and Thursday I am going to scrape stone on his account and I am not going to do it because he said a chain.**
