Sunday the first day of May, with that market shut for the morning and open for the afternoon, a yard below that market with a shed in it, and about nine people in that shed at half past five that afternoon.

**A Sunday, and the hundred and fourth time that wall behind a reeve's bench has come down, and no rota day and no wage Tuesday in it, and that butt covered from nine until four with no charge on it. Two men began to take that wall down at about ten past ten and it was bare at about half past ten and it was back on its holders at about eleven, at the angle it has carried since the twelfth of January, and the thing above the frame is still not whole. At about ten past ten on that Sunday two men began to take that wall down off its holders and it was bare at about half past ten, for the hundred and fourth time, and they had it back on the holders at about eleven at the angle it has carried since the twelfth of January, and it did not go up whole.

Twenty-one minutes off the wall, eight standing bare, twenty-one back, which is fifty minutes from about ten past ten to about eleven. Nobody in that market stopped what they were doing except about five people near enough to see it happen, and that is the same as it has been for nine years.

At about two in the afternoon the man who keeps the leaf read that leaf out in the open at the flat of that bench stone, in about nine people, with all four of its columns and the empty one read as well as the three that carry anything, and he read it out twice because about five of the people who come to that yard cannot read a column.

"**This is the leaf. There are four columns on it and I am going to read all four, and the third one is empty, and I am going to read that it is empty, because a yard that reads three out of four is a yard that has decided the fourth does not matter.

**The first column takes one entry a day, whatever else that day prints. Three hundred and thirty-three were on this leaf before the first of those fifty days. One has gone in on each of the fifty days from Sunday the thirteenth day of March to this day, and not one of those fifty days has not got an entry against it. Three hundred and thirty-three and fifty is three hundred and eighty-three at the close of it, and the entry against this day goes in after this reading and not before it, so the count at the hour of this reading is three hundred and eighty-two.

**The second column takes five a day, three before the hour of that day's reading and two after it, and it is that ledger's own and it is not a count of anything that anybody can check from a mole. Two hundred and fifty have gone in over those fifty days. Two hundred and forty-five of them went in on the forty-nine days before this one and three went in this morning before this hour. Two hundred and fifty less two is two hundred and forty-eight, and one thousand five hundred and thirty-nine and two hundred and forty-eight is one thousand seven hundred and eighty-seven at the hour of this reading, and one thousand five hundred and thirty-nine and two hundred and fifty is one thousand seven hundred and eighty-nine at the close of it.

**The third column has taken nothing at all. A boy's hand of twenty-two is at the head of it, his surname is on no page in this harbor, and he is not on the water. Nobody has been appointed to that column and nobody has been asked for it.

**The fourth column is one hundred and twenty-five. It has taken no line in fifty days, it is neither shut nor struck, and it holds no hundred and twenty-sixth line in it. I am not going to say anything else about that column and I am not going to say why.

Those are the four. That is the reading. I am going to read the same four again now, in the same words, and then somebody is going to ask me a figure and I am going to give the figure that is on this stone.**"

He read the same four again in the same words, and the figures went into a minute with the day and the hour against them.

At about half past three in the afternoon the man who keeps the leaf wrote three figures at the head of the three columns of that sheet of days, being one and four and four, and put the day and the hour against them, and gave the sheet to a man of about fifty-three with a boat of about fourteen tons and said nothing else about it.

The man of about fifty-three added those three figures up with a leaf and a pencil in about a minute and got nine, and then read the nine names down the side of that sheet, being the Morrow Line, the Saltmarch Weatherway, the Tidewatch Channel, the Ashtide Run, the Ternwatch Passage, the Lowfen Reed, the Cinder Bell, the Pale Shoals and the Orison Descent, and counted those and got nine again, and gave the sheet back and went to work.

Those three figures are not in any slip and are not in any minute and were not said out loud in that shed, and the man of fifty-three was not asked a second time what he had got.

At about four in the afternoon a man of about fifty-one who has not said a word to anybody in this harbor about a page of six names since before the frost stood at the flat of that bench stone and said one thing out loud in about nine people, in about 164 words, and it went into that market's book after that, as asked, with the hour against it and no name against it.

"**There is a thing behind that seal that one man on this coast has a right to ask for and has not asked for, and this yard has written it down more days running than it has written down anything else.

I am not asking anybody here and I am not asking him. I am asking it out loud in nine people, and then it goes in the book as asked, and no name goes against it, because a question with a name on it is a question about a person and this one is about a decision.

Here is the part I cannot get past. If that is the last of it anywhere, and if there is no second copy, and if the man it belongs to is the man on this coast who wants it most and has the least right to be the only one holding it, then what is that man supposed to do with it. Not this week. Ever.**"

Nobody in that yard answered him, nobody agreed with him, nobody argued with him, nobody thanked him and nobody told him he was wrong, and about four people in that nine heard the whole of it and the rest of that market was not told.

At about ten past four in the afternoon a man of about thirty-two of this harbor came down to that bench stone and took a wooden tally slate out of the inside of his coat and laid it flat on the flat of that stone beside that leaf, and left it there, and did not pick it up again.

He said one thing out loud about it, in about 292 words, and about four people in that yard heard it and the rest of that market was not told, and nobody in that yard asked him what it was for and nobody gave it a name and nobody thanked him.

"**I have carried that slate since the winter and there is not one thing on it that six men could not do.

**The proof that I am not standing here on my own is a page in that shed and not a feeling. Six men at five hours each, and the multiplication printed at the head of it, and six times five is thirty hours, and thirty hours is the figure at every part of it. That is on a page. It is not in me and it is not in my chest and it does not go away when I go out of that yard.

**And what I am connected to is not a witness. It is not the woman who stood in the tender. It is not a run of mine. It is six men with a bucket and a splice and a page, and it is four people who can read four sheets on a wall, and it is a man of fifty-three with a leaf and a pencil, and it is a woman with a counter who will not read a name over her own shop.

It settles nothing by itself. It prices nothing. Not one penny of it goes on that money sheet and not one line goes on it, and I am not going to put a figure on it, because the whole of it is that I no longer hold the only hand on any of it.

The cost is that slate. It is lying on that stone and I am not going to pick it up, and from tonight that leaf is not read by me, and what I say on it does not carry more than what the next man says on it.**"

Nobody in that yard said a word about the slate and nobody asked him to take it back and he did not.

and that at about seven in the evening the reason this slip ends on four columns of a leaf that was read in the open this afternoon and not on any of the four things that happened in that yard is that a slip is read at half past five by the people who were standing there, and that three of the four things that happened this afternoon are going to be carried about this harbor by people who were not, and that the one that is not going to be carried about is the one that was said out loud in front of nine people and written in a book with an hour on it and no name on it.*
