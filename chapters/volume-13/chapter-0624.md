# Chapter 624

*Tuesday the Fifth Day of April, the Seventy-Sixth Wage Tuesday, the Column on the Lid of That Step Read Out at About Ten in the Morning in Four Minutes by a Man of About Thirty-One of the Second Lay and Standing at Twenty-Six Lines and One Hundred and Four Marks and Not Moved for the Seventy-Sixth Time, the Chest With the Twenty Envelopes in It Shut from Ten Until Four, the Seventy-Sixth Week Standing Fallen Due at the Close of That Day, the First of Seven Days in Which Three Routes Are to Change and None of Them Changed on This Day, the Ashtide Run and the Ternwatch Passage Held and the Lowfen Reed Dormant on a Sheet of Days in That Shed and Nine Names in Three Columns Adding to Nine, a Boy of Twenty-Two on the Stool at the End of That Stall With a Board Across His Knees and Off the Water and Nobody Having Said a Word to Him About the Water, Nobody Asked the Woman of About Thirty-Nine Anything, Nothing Read Out of a Gap and No Figure Moved by One, the Gap Put Out Loud at About Half Past Five, the Bag at Twenty-Eight Pence, Being Two Shillings and Fourpence, the First Column at Three Hundred and Fifty-Seven and the Second at One Thousand Six Hundred and Fifty-Nine*

Tuesday the fifth day of April, with about forty people on the boards at the back of that market from ten in the morning, and a yard below it with a shed in it and about nine people in that shed at half past five in the afternoon.

**The seventy-sixth wage Tuesday, and not a rota day and not a wipe, and that cistern covered and untouched from eight in the morning until four in the afternoon. On the lid of that step the column stood at twenty-six lines and one hundred and four marks and it was read out at about ten by the man of about thirty-one of the second lay and it took him four minutes and it did not move, and behind that door the chest with the twenty envelopes in it stood shut from ten until four. The seventy-sixth week stands fallen due at the close of that day behind the seventy-fifth and behind the seventy-fourth, and a man has now read that lid seventy-six times and nobody in this harbor has ever asked him what he thinks of it and nobody is going to start this week. This is the first of seven days in which three of the nine routes are to go from what they are to what they are going to be, and not one of the three has changed yet, and the sheet of days that carries their state in three columns stands in that shed with nine names on it and the three columns add to nine. Nothing went onto the money sheet and nothing came off it, and a day asked for by name is not a line on that sheet and a route changing its state is not a line on that sheet either. This yard drew in a hundred and sixty-eight pence at about ten and let the same sum go out at about four, and what it finished the day with under that slate was twenty-eight pence, being two shillings and fourpence. Measured against four marks, being six hundred and forty pence, that is six hundred and twelve pence wanting, being three marks eleven shillings. On that sheet, one hundred and eight thousand two hundred and seventy-six pence has gone out of this harbor, being six hundred and seventy-six marks, nine shillings and eightpence; ten thousand six hundred and forty pence has ever been put in, being sixty-six marks, six shillings and eightpence; and it stands ninety-seven thousand six hundred and thirty-six pence short, being six hundred and ten marks and three shillings. No tin in that shed has been opened since the twenty-ninth day of April past and nothing in this harbor has a figure on it.**

**The nine went on again this morning and the eighth of them is the only one of the nine that is nailed to a wall in a street with a seal on it. The nine-line sheet with a seal on it in that front street, its two hundred and thirty-third day. Two objects of nobody's on that crown, six hundred and sixty-two days. That notice of the seventeenth of May past, three hundred and twenty-two. The bellwright of this harbor, not asked for, three hundred and twenty-four. The bench under that pump, bare for the four hundred and twenty-eighth night. A man of about fifty-one, two hundred and sixty-four days without a word to anybody about a page of six names. The two lights at about a mile off that point, two hundred and forty-six days. The six-line sheet at the back of that end stall, its two hundred and first day. The paper of four lines in Fish Street, its two hundred and sixteenth. On the board at the head of that mole there are five papers, of which two have never been opened by anybody in this harbor. Four lines are behind a door in an upper room over that market, nothing has been written under the fourth of them, and the answer stands at no on four out of six with the fifth not asked. The basket in that boathouse stands where it stands with the paper in it and nothing else in it. About two sea miles off that mole head are nine hulls this afternoon. A man of about twenty-nine can hear out of the left side of his head and it is one hundred and twenty-two days, one more than yesterday, and no figure is set against it and he is not on the water.**

---

The column was read out on the lid of that step at about ten in the morning and it took four minutes, and the man of about thirty-one of the second lay who has read that lid every Tuesday since the twenty-fifth of January read it for the seventy-sixth time, and about forty people were on those boards and about eleven of them could not see a line of it from where they were standing.

He read twenty-six lines and the last of the twenty-six came to one hundred and four marks, and there his finger stopped, and he gave the figure and he gave the fact that it had not shifted, and behind that door twenty envelopes lay shut from ten until four.

Nobody said a word about the seventy-sixth week but the man reading it, and about four people heard him and the rest of that market was not told.

---

At about one in the afternoon a boy of twenty-two was on the stool at the end of that stall with a board across his knees, writing weights into a book in a hand that gets smaller as the afternoon goes on, and he was not on the water, and that is the fifth time he has been off the water in twelve weeks, and it is not a punishment, and nobody in that market has said one word to him about the water and nobody put a name in the third column of anybody's leaf.

---

At about three in the afternoon the man who keeps the leaf read the sheet of days out of his own notebook to about four people who can read it, and he did not read it out loud and he did not write anything on it.

It stands in that shed on a board against the wall with nine route names down the side of it in three columns headed held, restricted and dormant, and a state against a day for each of the nine. The held column carries four and the restricted column carries one and the dormant column carries four, and four plus one plus four is nine, and a stranger with a leaf and a pencil can read the nine names off it and get nine and add the three columns and get nine again.

**The held column is the Morrow Line, the Saltmarch Weatherway, the Ashtide Run and the Ternwatch Passage. The restricted column is the Tidewatch Channel. The dormant column is the Lowfen Reed, the Cinder Bell, the Pale Shoals and the Orison Descent.** He read it to four people this afternoon and nobody else heard it and nobody in that shed was asked to agree with a name on it, and the three columns have stood exactly like that since before the winter and they are not going to be like that by the end of next week.

A route that is held is held by the community that uses it and by nobody else. The sheet is made in that shed out of what the route crews say and it decides nothing and it is not this party's to decide, and no route crew in this harbor is going to be asked a state twice.

---

And at about half past five on that Tuesday the man who keeps the leaf said the shortfall out loud at that bench stone, in about five hundred and sixty-one words, and nobody in that shed was asked which three routes he expected.

"**Twenty-six lines and one hundred and four marks, read out at ten this morning in four minutes for the seventy-sixth time, and it did not move, and behind that door twenty envelopes stood shut from ten until four, and the seventy-sixth week stands fallen due at the close of today.** Ninety-seven thousand six hundred and thirty-six pence, being six hundred and ten marks and three shillings, is the number on the top line of this slip, and it is sitting where the twenty-sixth of last month put it. Under this slate there is twenty-eight pence, being two shillings and fourpence, and a hundred and sixty-eight pence came into that yard at ten and a hundred and sixty-eight went out of it at four, and twenty-eight plus one hundred and sixty-eight less one hundred and sixty-eight is twenty-eight.

**That sheet of days against that wall in this shed has nine names on it in three columns and the columns stand at four, one and four, and I read it to about four people this afternoon and nobody else heard it.** I am not going to say what it is going to read by this day next week and I am not going to guess at it in front of anybody, because a sheet like that is not this yard's and is not mine and I only hold the pen because the thing the crews say arrives in nine mouths and this is the one place in this harbor where nine mouths land in one evening.

**A state on a route is not put on that sheet by a storm and it is not put on it by a loss and it is not put on it by a Crown instrument.** It is put on it by the crew that owns that run, once, and once only, and nobody in this shed is going to ask any of those nine crews what their state is on any morning of this week or any morning after this week. If they say nothing, the row stands empty and the empty row is the correct state of it and there is nothing to put in it.

And the thing I have said three times this month and am not going to say a fourth time in that form: nobody is being asked to agree with a name on that board. A name on that board is a thing somebody down that coast said out loud and came up here with, and this yard's whole business this winter was to write down what people said when they said it and to stop there.

The boy of twenty-two was on that stool at one this afternoon and he was not on the water and nobody said a word to him about it. The sheet with the fourteen things on it was not read out of today and no figure on it went up or down by one.

That leaf took one entry on the first column at about six this evening and five on the second, and I did not stand there and read it out. Three hundred and fifty-seven stands against the first of those figures and one thousand six hundred and fifty-nine against the second, my third is empty, and my fourth is at one hundred and twenty-five with no hundred and twenty-sixth line in it."

Nobody was thanked for the arithmetic and nobody was thanked for a column of nine names on a board. That shed was clear of everybody by about six with its door standing open on a slip with nothing on it, and the chest with the twenty envelopes in it was still shut at six, and that sheet of days stood against that wall with four in one column and one in another and four in the third.

---

**THE COLUMN ON THE LID OF THAT STEP WAS READ OUT AT ABOUT TEN IN THE MORNING ON THAT TUESDAY, BEING THE SEVENTY-SIXTH TIME, AND STOOD AT TWENTY-SIX LINES AND ONE HUNDRED AND FOUR MARKS AND DID NOT MOVE, AND THE CHEST WITH THE TWENTY ENVELOPES IN IT STOOD SHUT FROM TEN UNTIL FOUR, AND THE SEVENTY-SIXTH WEEK STANDS FALLEN DUE AT THE CLOSE OF THAT DAY. THE SHEET OF DAYS AGAINST THAT WALL IN THAT SHED CARRIES NINE ROUTE NAMES IN THREE COLUMNS HEADED HELD, RESTRICTED AND DORMANT WITH A STATE AGAINST A DAY FOR EACH, AND THE COLUMNS STAND AT FOUR, ONE AND FOUR, AND FOUR PLUS ONE PLUS FOUR IS NINE. IT WAS READ AT ABOUT THREE IN THE AFTERNOON TO ABOUT FOUR PEOPLE AND WAS NOT READ ALOUD AND NOTHING WAS WRITTEN ON IT. THAT STATE IS NOT PUT ON A ROUTE BY A STORM AND NOT BY A LOSS AND NOT BY A CROWN INSTRUMENT, AND NO ROUTE CREW IS TO BE ASKED A STATE TWICE. A BOY OF TWENTY-TWO WAS NOT ON THE WATER AND HIS SURNAME IS ON NO PAGE. THE FOURTEEN WERE NOT READ OUT OF AND NOT ONE FIGURE ON THAT SHEET WENT UP OR DOWN BY ONE. NINETY-SEVEN THOUSAND SIX HUNDRED AND THIRTY-SIX PENCE, BEING SIX HUNDRED AND TEN MARKS AND THREE SHILLINGS, IS WHAT THAT SHEET DOES NOT CARRY; TEN THOUSAND SIX HUNDRED AND FORTY PENCE, BEING SIXTY-SIX MARKS, SIX SHILLINGS AND EIGHTPENCE, IS ALL THAT HAS EVER GONE INTO IT; AND ONE HUNDRED AND EIGHT THOUSAND TWO HUNDRED AND SEVENTY-SIX PENCE, BEING SIX HUNDRED AND SEVENTY-SIX MARKS, NINE SHILLINGS AND EIGHTPENCE, IS WHAT IT HAS COST THIS HARBOR. THE WHOLE OF WHAT IS UNDER THAT SLATE IS TWENTY-EIGHT PENCE, BEING TWO SHILLINGS AND FOURPENCE.**

*First column, entered on Tuesday the fifth day of April at about six in the evening in that shed: that the said day was the seventy-sixth wage Tuesday and was not a rota day and was not a wipe, and that nothing was put on that cistern on it, the covers lying on it unbroken from eight in the morning until four in the afternoon; and that the column on the lid of that step was read out at about ten in the morning and that it took four minutes and that the man who read it is of about thirty-one and of the second lay and has read that lid every Tuesday since the twenty-fifth of January, being seventy-six times, and that about forty people were on those boards and about eleven of them could not see a line of that lid from where they were standing, and that he read twenty-six lines and the last of the twenty-six came to one hundred and four marks and there his finger stopped, and that he gave the figure and gave the fact that the figure had not shifted, and that behind that door twenty envelopes lay shut from ten until four, and that the seventy-sixth week stands fallen due at the close of the said day behind the seventy-fifth and behind the seventy-fourth, and that nobody said a word about it but the man reading it and about four people heard him and the rest of that market was not told; and that at about one in the afternoon a boy of twenty-two was on the stool at the end of that stall with a board across his knees writing weights into a book in a hand that gets smaller as the afternoon goes on, and that he was not on the water, and that that is the fifth time he has been off the water in twelve weeks and it is not a punishment, and that nobody in that market has said one word to him about the water and nobody put a name in the third column of anybody's leaf; and that the held column is the Morrow Line, the Saltmarch Weatherway, the Ashtide Run and the Ternwatch Passage, and that the restricted column is the Tidewatch Channel, and that the dormant column is the Lowfen Reed, the Cinder Bell, the Pale Shoals and the Orison Descent, and that four plus one plus four is nine and that a stranger with a leaf and a pencil can read the nine names off that sheet and get nine and add the three columns and get nine again; and that nothing was read out of the sheet of the fourteen today, that not one figure on it went up or down by one, and that none of those fourteen was added to and none taken off; and that at about half past five the man who keeps the leaf said the shortfall out loud at that bench stone in about five hundred and sixty-one words in about nine people; and that at about six this evening one line went in on that first column and five went in on the second, and that the day of this run counted from Sunday the thirteenth day of March is the twenty-fourth, and that the first of those two figures is three hundred and fifty-seven and the second is one thousand six hundred and fifty-nine, and that the third took nothing, and that the fourth stands at one hundred and twenty-five with no hundred and twenty-sixth line in it.*

*Second column, not checkable from a mole, entered on the said Tuesday at about six in the evening in that shed, and no name is against it: that at about three in the afternoon the reason I read that sheet to four people and not to nine is that about five of the nine in this yard cannot read it and a name read out of a column is a name in a mouth that somebody else then has to carry to the five, and that this yard has four different ways of carrying a thing at once and each of them loses a part of it; and that at about eleven in the morning the reason I have written nothing in this column about a season's work on a bar of hard sand is that Saturday printed it once and a second printing of a cost is not a second cost, and that what I have learned from a barrel-maker this month is that a sentence about somebody else's loss is a thing a man says once; and that at about ten in the morning the reason the seventy-sixth reading of that lid has no figure of my own against it is that a figure a man repeats seventy-six times is his and not mine, and that the only figure I am answerable for is on this slip and it has not moved since the twenty-sixth of last month; and that at about half past two in the afternoon the reason I wrote down that those columns have not moved since before the winter is that a sheet that has stood still for four weeks and then moves three times in seven days is going to be looked at hard by everybody, and that the only defence I have got for it is that I did not put a single state on that board and nine crews said nine things and I wrote down nine things; and that at about four in the afternoon the reason nobody went up to the end of that stall and read that sheet out is that the twelve men on that rota board this harbor has never asked a question of are not going to be asked one by a man who keeps a leaf, and that the seventy-sixth week standing fallen due is a fact about a harbor and not an excuse to go and find somebody to make it better; and that at about half past five the reason I am not saying tonight which three of those nine names I expect to move is that I have not asked and I am not going to, and that a guess printed at half past five in a shed in this harbor would be a figure set on that board by me, and there is one name on that board this month that is set by me and I am not proud of it and I cannot take it off.*
