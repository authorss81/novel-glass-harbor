# Chapter 647

*Thursday the Twenty-Eighth Day of April, a Rota Day, That Cistern Behind That Market Filled for the Hundred and Forty-Fifth Time by Two Men and a Bucket Going Up a Ladder Eleven Times a Little After Eight in the Morning, Nothing Walked on, the Covers Back on It by a Quarter Past Nine and the Water Up to the Rim of the Second Hoop Until About Four in the Afternoon, the Name at the Head of That Rota Board Still Off It, a Woman of About Forty-Four With a Chandler's Counter in That Front Street Saying at About Eleven in the Morning in About Two Hundred and Thirty-Four Words What She Will Do and What She Will Not Do With the Paper on That Bench Stone, Being That She Will Read the Face of It and Stop at the Seal and Will Not Read the Name Under That Seal, and That She Wants About Twenty People There of Whom About Eleven Cannot Read It, and That She Is a Woman With a Counter and Not a Mouth for Anyone, Nobody in That Yard Arguing With Her and Nobody Thanking Her and Nobody in That Yard Having Asked Her One Thing Before, Nothing Read Out of a Gap and No Figure Moved by One, the Bag at Twenty-Eight Pence, Being Two Shillings and Fourpence, the First Column at Three Hundred and Eighty and the Second at One Thousand Seven Hundred and Seventy-Four*

Thursday the twenty-eighth day of April, with that cistern at the back of that market charged before that market opened, and about nine people at the flat of that bench stone at half past five that afternoon.

**A Thursday rota day, and not a wage Tuesday and not a wipe. That butt was filled by hand for the hundred and forty-fifth time at a little after eight in the morning, eleven trips and nothing walked on and nothing carried up that ladder on any other errand, the covers went back on it before a quarter past nine, and it stood at the rim of its second hoop until about four in the afternoon with not one bucket drawn out of it, and the name at the head of that rota board is still off it. At about eleven in the morning a woman of about forty-four with a chandler's counter in that front street stood at the flat of that bench stone with that paper still lying on it and said what she would do with it and what she would not do with it, in about nine people, in about 234 words, and nobody in that yard argued with her and nobody thanked her and nobody in that yard had asked her one thing about it before she spoke. The paper was not read out today and it is not going to be read out twice and the name at the foot of it is on the paper and is in no mouth. Nothing went onto the money sheet and nothing came off it, and a woman deciding how she will do a thing is not a line on that sheet, and a cistern is not a line on that sheet. The figure in chalk on that slate at that end stall is a hundred and sixty-eight pence a day with a boat and two men, and it went into that yard at about ten and it came out of that yard at about four, and twenty-eight pence, being two shillings and fourpence, is what the slate was holding at the close of the day, which is six hundred and twelve pence, being three marks eleven shillings, short of a wage of four marks, being six hundred and forty pence. That sheet of money stands on one line and is not taking a second this month, and it is one hundred and eleven thousand eight hundred and seventy-six pence out, being six hundred and ninety-nine marks and three shillings, set against ten thousand six hundred and forty pence in, being sixty-six marks, six shillings and eightpence, with one hundred and one thousand two hundred and thirty-six pence, being six hundred and thirty-two marks, nine shillings and eightpence, still wanted, and nothing standing was paid, advanced, cut back, bettered, struck or closed today.**

**The nine went on a day each and the seventh of them is four lines behind a door in an upper room over that market that have not come out of it in eight months. The paper of four lines in Fish Street, its two hundred and thirty-ninth. That notice of the seventeenth of May past, three hundred and forty-five days. The bellwright of this harbor, not asked for, three hundred and forty-seven. Two objects of nobody's on that crown, six hundred and eighty-five days. The bench under that pump, bare for the four hundred and fifty-first night. The two lights at about a mile off that point, two hundred and sixty-nine days. The six-line sheet at the back of that end stall, its two hundred and twenty-fourth day. The nine-line sheet with a seal on it in that front street, its two hundred and fifty-sixth day. A man of about fifty-one, two hundred and eighty-seven days without a word to anybody about a page of six names. Two of the five papers on that board at the head of that mole have never been opened by anybody in this harbor. Nothing is written under the fourth of those four lines, nothing ever has been, and the answer to them is no on four out of six with the fifth not asked. That basket in that boathouse has a paper in it and nothing else has ever been put in it. That board's mole has nine hulls standing off its head at about two sea miles, it had nine there on the twenty-first of January, and no man in this harbor has said that any of them has eased. A man of about twenty-nine can hear out of the left side of his head and it is one hundred and forty-five days, one more than yesterday, and that is not a gift, no figure is set against it, and he is not on the water.**

---

Those two men went up that ladder at a little after eight in the morning, eleven times, with a bucket between them and with nothing walked on, and the covers were back on that butt before a quarter past nine, and it stood to the rim of its second hoop until about four in the afternoon with not one bucket drawn out of it.

The name at the head of that rota board is still off it, and it has been off it for long enough that nobody in that market remembers whose it was.

---

At about eleven in the morning the woman of about forty-four with the chandler's counter in that front street came down those boards and stood at the flat of that bench stone with that paper still lying on it where she had put it on Wednesday, and about nine people were there, and she said one thing out loud in about 234 words, and nobody in that yard had asked her for it.

"**I am going to read it. I am not saying I will and I am not saying I will not, I am telling you what I am going to do so that you cannot say afterwards that nobody told you.**

**I am going to read the face of it and I am going to stop at the seal. I am not going to read the name under that seal. Not because I have not been asked to and not because somebody might be cross. Because a name that a shopkeeper reads out over her own counter is a name that belongs to that counter, and it will be the next thing that counter is known for, and I have been twenty-two years keeping a counter and I know exactly what that does to a street.**

**I want twenty people there and about eleven of them will not be able to read a line of it and I want them there anyway, because the day a paper gets read out to the people who can read it is the day the people who cannot read it are not in the room. And I am not the mouth for anybody. I sell rope and nails and lamp oil. If what comes out of my mouth on Friday is something you do not like, you will have to say so to me and not to a diver.**"

Nobody in that yard argued with her, about four people heard the whole of it and the rest of that market was not told, and nobody thanked her for it, and nobody in that yard has asked her one thing since.

---

At about two in the afternoon a man of about thirty-two of this harbor came down to that stone, looked at that paper, and did not touch it, and said one thing to the man who keeps the leaf and one thing only.

"**Then it is Friday morning and twenty people and the boards, and I am not going to stand next to you while you do it and I am not going to sit down while you do it either, and if a man in this yard comes up to you before Friday and asks you to stop at a different place than the seal, you come and tell me and I will come and stand there.**

**And that is all I am going to say about it, and I am not going to say it again, and if I say it again you are to write down that I have said it again and not that I have added anything.**"

The man who keeps the leaf wrote that down and nothing else, and nobody thanked either of them for it, and they are still married to each other and this is not about that.

---

And at about half past five on that Thursday the man who keeps the leaf said the shortfall out loud at that bench stone, in about 655 words, and nobody in that shed was asked whether a paper ought to be read out at all.

"**A hundred and forty-five charges have gone past that cistern, eleven trips by two men before that market opened this morning, not one of them walked on, and that butt up to the rim of its second hoop until about four this afternoon, and the name at the head of that board is still off it.**

**Yesterday a paper came ashore addressed to a trade. This morning the woman it was addressed to stood on that stone and said out loud what she is going to do with it, and she said it before anybody asked her, and she is going to read the face of it and stop at the seal and not read the name under the seal, and she wants twenty people there of whom about eleven cannot read it, and she said she is not the mouth for anybody.**

**I am putting that in this slip in her words and not mine, because if I put it in mine it becomes the yard's arrangement and it is not. It is hers. The day is Friday morning. The paper has not been read out. Nobody has read it out today and nobody is going to read it out twice.**

And a man of about thirty-two came down to that stone at two this afternoon and said he would not stand next to her while she did it and would not sit down either, and that if anybody in this yard comes up to her before Friday and asks her to stop somewhere other than the seal, she is to tell him and he is to come and stand there. Nobody thanked him for saying that and nobody argued with him about it.

That sheet of money has not moved a halfpenny today and it carries the one line it has been carrying. The figures on that sheet are one hundred and eleven thousand eight hundred and seventy-six pence gone, being six hundred and ninety-nine marks and three shillings, ten thousand six hundred and forty pence come in, being sixty-six marks, six shillings and eightpence, and one hundred and one thousand two hundred and thirty-six pence still to find, being six hundred and thirty-two marks, nine shillings and eightpence. **No standing item was touched in this harbor today, in any of those six ways, and the figure of four hundred and fifty pence is where it has been on that board.**

The fourteen on that sheet at the end of that stall were not read out of and not one figure on that sheet moved by one, and none of those fourteen was added to and none was taken off. The positions were not counted again and the page that carries them was not read out of. Kept is not a column, and no figure has been set against it against any day of this run. The three columns of that sheet of days were not counted today and that count is not in this slip.

What that slate is holding is twenty-eight pence, being two shillings and fourpence, and a hundred and sixty-eight pence came into that yard at ten and a hundred and sixty-eight went out of it at four, and twenty-eight plus one hundred and sixty-eight less one hundred and sixty-eight is twenty-eight, and it has been that figure on every day of this run. A man of about twenty-nine can hear out of the left side of his head and it is one hundred and forty-five days, and no figure is set against it and he is not on the water.

At about six this evening one line went on that first column and five on the second, and the reading is not done, and the four of them stand at three hundred and eighty, one thousand seven hundred and seventy-four, none, and one hundred and twenty-five, and I am not going to say the third one twice tonight."

Nobody was thanked for the arithmetic and nobody was thanked for a woman deciding out loud where she would stop. That shed stood empty by about six with its door standing open on a slip nobody was on, and that paper was still lying on the flat of that bench stone, and it has been read out by nobody.

**THAT CISTERN BEHIND THAT MARKET WAS FILLED BY HAND FOR THE HUNDRED AND FORTY-FIFTH TIME AT A LITTLE AFTER EIGHT IN THE MORNING, TWO MEN AND A BUCKET GOING UP THAT LADDER ELEVEN TIMES BEFORE THAT MARKET OPENED, NOT ONE BUCKET WALKED ON, THE COVERS BACK ON IT BY A QUARTER PAST NINE AND THE WATER AT THE RIM OF ITS SECOND HOOP UNTIL ABOUT FOUR IN THE AFTERNOON, AND THE NAME AT THE HEAD OF THAT ROTA BOARD IS STILL OFF IT. A WOMAN OF ABOUT FORTY-FOUR WITH A CHANDLER'S COUNTER IN THAT FRONT STREET SAID AT ABOUT ELEVEN IN THE MORNING IN ABOUT NINE PEOPLE IN ABOUT 234 WORDS THAT SHE IS GOING TO READ THE FACE OF THAT PAPER AND STOP AT THE SEAL AND IS NOT GOING TO READ THE NAME UNDER THAT SEAL, AND THAT SHE WANTS TWENTY PEOPLE THERE OF WHOM ABOUT ELEVEN CANNOT READ IT, AND THAT SHE IS NOT THE MOUTH FOR ANYBODY. THE PAPER HAS NOT BEEN READ OUT AND IS NOT GOING TO BE READ OUT TWICE, AND THE NAME AT THE FOOT OF IT IS ON THE PAPER AND IS IN NO MOUTH. NOTHING WAS READ OUT OF A GAP AND NO FIGURE ON THE SHEET OF THE FOURTEEN WENT UP OR DOWN BY ONE. THAT FITTING-OUT SHEET STANDS WHERE THE TWENTIETH OF LAST MONTH LEFT IT, ON ONE LINE. WHAT THIS HARBOR HAS SPENT IS ONE HUNDRED AND ELEVEN THOUSAND EIGHT HUNDRED AND SEVENTY-SIX PENCE, BEING SIX HUNDRED AND NINETY-NINE MARKS AND THREE SHILLINGS; WHAT HAS EVER GONE INTO IT IS TEN THOUSAND SIX HUNDRED AND FORTY PENCE, BEING SIXTY-SIX MARKS, SIX SHILLINGS AND EIGHTPENCE; AND WHAT IT IS SHORT IS ONE HUNDRED AND ONE THOUSAND TWO HUNDRED AND THIRTY-SIX PENCE, BEING SIX HUNDRED AND THIRTY-TWO MARKS, NINE SHILLINGS AND EIGHTPENCE. AND TWENTY-EIGHT PENCE, BEING TWO SHILLINGS AND FOURPENCE, IS UNDER THAT SLATE.**

and that at about eleven in the morning the woman of about forty-four with the chandler's counter in that front street came down those boards and stood at the flat of that bench stone with that paper still lying on it where she had put it on the Wednesday, and that about nine people were there, and that she said one thing out loud in about 234 words, being that she is going to read it, and that she is not saying she will and is not saying she will not but is telling them what she is going to do so that they cannot say afterwards that nobody told them, and that she is going to read the face of it and stop at the seal, and that she is not going to read the name under that seal, not because she has not been asked to and not because somebody might be cross, but because a name that a shopkeeper reads out over her own counter is a name that belongs to that counter and will be the next thing that counter is known for, and that she has been twenty-two years keeping a counter and knows exactly what that does to a street, and that she wants twenty people there and about eleven of them will not be able to read a line of it and she wants them there anyway, because the day a paper gets read out to the people who can read it is the day the people who cannot read it are not in the room, and that she is not the mouth for anybody and that she sells rope and nails and lamp oil, and that if what comes out of her mouth on the Friday is something they do not like, they will have to say so to her and not to a diver; and that nobody in that yard argued with her, that about four people heard the whole of it and the rest of that market was not told, that nobody thanked her for it, and that nobody in that yard has asked her one thing since; and that at about two in the afternoon a man of about thirty-two of this harbor came down to that stone, looked at that paper and did not touch it, and said one thing to the man who keeps the leaf and one thing only, being that it is Friday morning and twenty people and the boards, and that he is not going to stand next to her while she does it and is not going to sit down while she does it either, and that if a man in that yard comes up to her before the Friday and asks her to stop at a different place than the seal, she is to tell him and he will come and stand there, and that that is all he is going to say about it and that if he says it again the keeper is to write down that he has said it again and not that he has added anything; and that the man who keeps the leaf wrote that down and nothing else, that nobody thanked either of them for it, and that they are still married to each other and that this is not about that; and that the paper was not read out on that day and is not going to be read out twice and the name at the foot of it is on the paper and is in no mouth; and that the four things standing at the end of that stall on the sheet of the fourteen were not read out of, that not one figure on that sheet moved by one, and that none of those fourteen was added to and none was taken off; and that the count of positions was not taken again, that the fifth word on the back of the other page is not a column and carries no figure against any day, and that the three columns of that sheet of days were not counted on that Thursday and that count is not in that slip; and that at about half past five the man who keeps the leaf said the shortfall out loud at that bench stone in about 655 words in about nine people; and that the Morrow Line is in the dormant column of that sheet of days, and is not struck, and is still one of the nine, and is still at Orison; and that a single entry went in on that first column at about six that evening and five went in on the second, and that the day of that run, counted from Sunday the thirteenth day of March, is the forty-seventh, and that the first of those two figures is three hundred and eighty and the second is one thousand seven hundred and seventy-four; the third of those four took nothing, a boy's hand being at the head of it with his surname on no page and himself not on the water, and the fourth of them is unmoved at one hundred and twenty-five and has no hundred and twenty-sixth line in it; and that four hundred and fifty pence is the figure on that board this Thursday as it was last Thursday, and that nothing standing was paid, advanced, cut back, bettered, struck or closed.*

*Second column, not checkable from a mole, entered on the said Thursday at about six in the evening in that shed, and no name is against it: that at about eleven in the morning the reason I wrote that woman's terms down in her words and not mine is that in mine they become an arrangement of this yard and they are not one, they are hers, and that the only part of them this yard is answerable for is the twenty people and the eleven of them, and that the rest belongs to her and to a counter in the front street that has been there twenty-two years; and that at about two in the afternoon the reason I wrote that man's sentence down and added nothing to it is that he told me what to do with it, and that a sentence a man asks to have kept exactly as he said it is a sentence that is going to be read out to him one day, and that this yard has spent the winter finding out what happens when it keeps a sentence and improves it; and that at about four in the afternoon the reason there is nothing in this column about the seal is that I have not got as far as the seal, that a seal is a mark pressed into paper by an office and this shed does not have an opinion about offices, and that what this shed has an opinion about is the twenty people and the eleven of them; and that at about one in the afternoon the reason a hundred and forty-five charges is the whole of what I am going to say about that butt is that it is a butt and it is filled on a rota and the name at the head of that rota board has been off it long enough to be a fact about this market rather than a gap in it, and that a man who keeps asking about a blank on a board is a man looking for somebody to blame for a blank; and that at about seven in the evening the reason this slip ends on four columns and not on a paper waiting for a Friday is that a slip is read at half past five by the people who were in that yard, and that most of the people who will be in that yard tomorrow were not in it this afternoon, and that this yard has stopped believing that a page read to nine people is read by the harbor.*
