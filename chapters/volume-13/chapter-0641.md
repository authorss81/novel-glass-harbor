# Chapter 641

*Friday the Twenty-Second Day of April, Not a Rota Day and Not a Wage Tuesday and Not a Wipe, That Cistern Covered from Eight Until Four and Untouched, a Man of About Thirty-Three Who Hires Hands Reading a Bearing Off the Head of That Mole at About Ten in the Morning and the Casting of That Bell Carrying a Figure of Nine and Nobody in That Yard Able to Say Where the Nine Was Cut, a Boat of About Eleven Tons In from the South-West at About Two in the Afternoon With Four of the Lowfen Reed Aboard and a Sheet of Their Own Put Down on the Flat of That Bench Stone With a Day and an Hour on It and Not Asked For and Not Read Out Loud in That Yard, Nobody Asked the Woman of About Thirty-Nine Anything, Nothing Read Out of a Gap and No Figure Moved by One, the Bag at Twenty-Eight Pence, Being Two Shillings and Fourpence, the First Column at Three Hundred and Seventy-Four and the Second at One Thousand Seven Hundred and Forty-Four*

Friday the twenty-second day of April, with that market opened on the boards from about ten in the morning under a sky the colour of a wet slate, a yard below that market with a shed standing in it, and about nine people in that shed at half past five that afternoon.

**A Friday with nothing standing against it: that butt covered from eight until four with no charge written on it, no rota day on the board at the back of that market, no wage Tuesday and no wipe in the day. The word dormant is still on that sheet of days against the first of the nine names, with a day and an hour against it, and the word held is still on that row above it and is still readable, and nobody has come into this yard this morning to ask me about either of those two words and nobody is going to be asked. At about ten this morning a man of about thirty-three who hires hands stood at the head of that mole with a slate against his eye and read a bearing off the pressure bell that is set in the stone there, and what he read was Orison, and the inside face of that bell carries a figure of nine cut into the metal, and about four people at that mole head looked at the nine and not one of them could say where it had been cut or by whom, and I have put the bearing on the back of an old slate in this shed and not on that sheet of days, because a bearing is not a state. At about two this afternoon a boat of about eleven tons came in from the south-west with four of the Lowfen Reed aboard, and one of those four put a sheet of their own down on the flat of that bench stone with a day and an hour on it, and nobody in this yard read it out loud and nobody in this yard asked them for it, and about four people read it themselves and none of those four is the party. Nothing went onto the money sheet and nothing came off it, and a sheet another crew put down is not a line on that sheet, and a bearing off a bell is not a line on that sheet either. Into that yard at about ten in the morning went a hundred and sixty-eight pence and out of it at about four in the afternoon went a hundred and sixty-eight pence, and under that slate at the close of the day lay twenty-eight pence, being two shillings and fourpence, which set against a wage of four marks, being six hundred and forty pence, is six hundred and twelve pence short, being three marks eleven shillings. That fitting-out sheet carries one line on it and is not going to carry a second one this month, and what it has cost this harbor is one hundred and eleven thousand eight hundred and seventy-six pence, being six hundred and ninety-nine marks and three shillings, and the whole of what has ever been put into it is ten thousand six hundred and forty pence, being sixty-six marks, six shillings and eightpence, and what it does not carry is one hundred and one thousand two hundred and thirty-six pence, being six hundred and thirty-two marks, nine shillings and eightpence. Nothing has been out of a tin in that shed since the twenty-ninth day of April past.**

**The nine went on a day each and the last of them is a man who has gone longer than anybody on this coast without saying a thing to anybody. A man of about fifty-one, two hundred and eighty-one days without a word to anybody about a page of six names. Two objects of nobody's on that crown, six hundred and seventy-nine days. That notice of the seventeenth of May past, three hundred and thirty-nine days. The bellwright of this harbor, not asked for, three hundred and forty-one. The bench under that pump, bare for the four hundred and forty-fifth night. The two lights at about a mile off that point, two hundred and sixty-three days. The six-line sheet at the back of that end stall, its two hundred and eighteenth day. The paper of four lines in Fish Street, its two hundred and thirty-third. The nine-line sheet with a seal on it in that front street, its two hundred and fiftieth day. Five papers stand on that board at the head of that mole and two of the five have never been opened by anybody in this harbor. Those four lines are still behind a door in an upper room over that market with nothing ever written under the fourth of them, and the answer to all of it is no on four out of six with the fifth not asked. In that boathouse there is a basket with a paper in it and nothing else in the basket. About two sea miles off that mole head lie nine hulls this afternoon, they were nine there on the twenty-first of January, and no man in this harbor has said that any of them has eased. A man of about twenty-nine can hear out of the left side of his head and it is one hundred and thirty-nine days, one more than yesterday, and that is not a gift, no figure is set against it, and he is not on the water.**

---

At about ten in the morning a man of about thirty-three who hires hands went out onto the head of that mole with a slate and a length of cord and stood over the pressure bell that is set in the stone there for the use of the two men who take a bearing off it every Sunday morning.

He read a bearing off it and got Orison, and the ninth bell at Orison is a bell and not that bell and is not the same thing as that bell, and about two days' sail from that quay in a boat and not one day less.

He came back down that mole and put the slate on the bench in that shed with the bearing written on the back of it, and the inside face of that bell carries a figure of nine cut into the metal by somebody, and about four people went up and looked at the nine and could not say where it had been cut.

Nobody in that yard asked him what the nine was for and nobody in that yard has asked him since, and the bearing is on the back of an old slate and not on the sheet of days, and no state is written against any of the nine names because a man with a slate and a cord is not a route crew and cannot put a word on a run.

---

At about two in the afternoon a boat of about eleven tons came in from the south-west and made the tide under that mole and lay alongside the west quay with her gear wet and four men in her who were not of this harbor.

One of those four put a sheet of their own down on the flat of that bench stone with a day and an hour on it, and it is a sheet of about six lines and about four people in that yard can read it, and nobody in that yard read it out loud, and nobody asked that man which position his sheet stood in, and he was not asked what his crew wanted and he was not asked what they would do with a route.

They were under awning by three and they were gone by five and the sheet is still on that stone, and it is not the sheet of the fourteen and it is not the sheet of positions and it is not the fitting-out sheet and it is not the four heads on that wall above the stone, and the man who keeps the leaf wrote on the back of his own slate that a sheet of another crew's came in today and was not asked for and was not read out, and put the date and the hour against that, and that is the whole of what this yard did with it.

---

At about four in the afternoon a woman of about thirty-nine who mends net stood at her own stall at the end of that market and worked, and nobody in this harbor came to that stall and asked her a single thing.

---

And at about half past five on that Friday the man who keeps the leaf said the shortfall out loud at that bench stone, in about 692 words, and nobody in that shed was asked what a crew's own sheet is for.

"**One day after a word went onto a sheet of days in this shed, and the word is still on that sheet, and the word that was true before it is still readable above it, and nobody has come in here this morning to ask me about either of those two and nobody is going to be asked. I wrote a route down yesterday in a man's own mouth with the day and the hour against it and I am not going to say one word about it this evening that is not on that sheet already.**

**There is a bearing on the back of an old slate in the corner of this shed that was not on it yesterday and it is a bearing and not a state, and the inside face of that bell on our mole head has a figure of nine cut into it and about four of us looked at the nine today and not one of us can say where it came from. I have written the bearing down and I have not written the nine down in any column, because I do not know what a nine is for and a page is not the place to find out.**

A boat of about eleven tons came in from the south-west at two this afternoon with four of the Lowfen Reed in her and one of those four put a sheet of their own on that stone with a day and an hour on it, and I did not read it out and nobody in that yard asked them for it. **A sheet four people can read is a sheet four people can be angry at, and this yard is not going to be the body that decides anything about a run, and it did not ask that man one thing and it is not going to ask him one tomorrow.**

The money sheet did not move today and it carries one line on it and is not going to carry a second this month. It stands at one hundred and eleven thousand eight hundred and seventy-six pence out, being six hundred and ninety-nine marks and three shillings, against ten thousand six hundred and forty pence in, being sixty-six marks, six shillings and eightpence, and the gap is one hundred and one thousand two hundred and thirty-six pence, being six hundred and thirty-two marks, nine shillings and eightpence, **and nothing standing in this harbor was paid, advanced, cut back, bettered, struck or closed to make any part of that smaller.**

The fourteen at the end of that stall were not read out of today, not one figure on that sheet went up or down by one, and none of those fourteen was added to and none was taken off. The count of positions was not taken again, and the fifth word on the back of the other page is not a column and carries no figure against any day of this month, and the three columns of that sheet of days were not counted on this day and that count is not in this slip.

What is lying under this slate is twenty-eight pence, being two shillings and fourpence, and a hundred and sixty-eight pence went into that yard at ten this morning and a hundred and sixty-eight came out of it at four this afternoon, and twenty-eight plus one hundred and sixty-eight less one hundred and sixty-eight is twenty-eight. A man of about twenty-nine can hear out of the left side of his head and it is one hundred and thirty-nine days, and no figure is set against it and he is not on the water.

One line went in on that first column at about six this evening and five on the second, and the reading of it was not done, and the first of those two figures is three hundred and seventy-four and the second is one thousand seven hundred and forty-four, my third is empty and nobody has been put in it, and my fourth is one hundred and twenty-five with nothing in it that has not been in it all winter."

Nobody was thanked for the arithmetic and nobody was thanked for a sheet another crew put down. That shed stood empty by about six with its door standing open on a slip nobody was on, and the bearing lay on the back of an old slate in the corner of it, and the sheet of another crew lay on the flat of that bench stone where they had put it.

---

**A BEARING OFF THE HEAD OF THAT MOLE READ AT ABOUT TEN IN THE MORNING BY A MAN OF ABOUT THIRTY-THREE WHO HIRES HANDS, BEING ORISON, WHICH IS A BEARING AND A NAME AND A THING ABOUT TWO DAYS' SAIL FROM THAT QUAY IN A BOAT, AND THE INSIDE FACE OF THAT BELL CARRIES A FIGURE OF NINE CUT INTO THE METAL AND ABOUT FOUR PEOPLE AT THAT MOLE HEAD LOOKED AT THE NINE AND COULD NOT SAY WHERE IT HAD BEEN CUT. THE BEARING IS ON THE BACK OF AN OLD SLATE IN THAT SHED AND NOT ON THE SHEET OF DAYS, AND A BEARING IS NOT A STATE, AND NO STATE WAS PUT ON ANY OF THE NINE NAMES TODAY BY ANYBODY. A BOAT OF ABOUT ELEVEN TONS CAME IN AT ABOUT TWO IN THE AFTERNOON WITH FOUR OF THE LOWFEN REED IN HER AND ONE OF THEM PUT A SHEET OF THEIR OWN DOWN ON THAT STONE WITH A DAY AND AN HOUR ON IT AND NOBODY IN THAT YARD READ IT OUT AND NOBODY ASKED THEM FOR IT. NOTHING WAS READ OUT OF A GAP AND NO FIGURE ON THE SHEET OF THE FOURTEEN WENT UP OR DOWN BY ONE. ONE LINE STANDS ON THAT FITTING-OUT SHEET AND IT IS THE ONLY LINE ON IT. WHAT THAT HARBOR HAS SPENT IS ONE HUNDRED AND ELEVEN THOUSAND EIGHT HUNDRED AND SEVENTY-SIX PENCE, BEING SIX HUNDRED AND NINETY-NINE MARKS AND THREE SHILLINGS. WHAT HAS EVER BEEN PUT INTO IT IS TEN THOUSAND SIX HUNDRED AND FORTY PENCE, BEING SIXTY-SIX MARKS, SIX SHILLINGS AND EIGHTPENCE. AND THE FIGURE IT DOES NOT CARRY IS ONE HUNDRED AND ONE THOUSAND TWO HUNDRED AND THIRTY-SIX PENCE, BEING SIX HUNDRED AND THIRTY-TWO MARKS, NINE SHILLINGS AND EIGHTPENCE. UNDER THAT SLATE IS TWENTY-EIGHT PENCE, BEING TWO SHILLINGS AND FOURPENCE.**

*First column, entered on Friday the twenty-second day of April at about six in the evening in that shed: that the said day was no rota day and no wage Tuesday and no wipe, and that no charge was put on that cistern upon it, the covers lying on it unbroken from eight in the morning until four in the afternoon; and that at about ten in the morning a man of about thirty-three who hires hands went out onto the head of that mole with a slate and a length of cord and read a bearing off the pressure bell that is set in the stone there, and that what he read was Orison, and that the ninth bell at Orison is a bell and is not that bell and is not the same thing as that bell, and that it is a thing about two days' sail from that quay in a boat; and that the inside face of that bell carries a figure of nine cut into the metal and that about four people at that mole head looked at the nine and that not one of them could say where it had been cut or by whom, and that nobody in that yard asked him what the nine was for and that nobody in that yard has asked him since; and that the bearing was put on the back of an old slate in that shed with the day and the hour against it and was not written on the sheet of days, and that a bearing is not a state and that no state was put against any of the nine names that day by anybody in that yard; and that at about two in the afternoon a boat of about eleven tons came in from the south-west and made the tide under that mole and came alongside the west quay with four men in her who were not of this harbor, and that one of those four put a sheet of their own down on the flat of that bench stone with a day and an hour on it, being a sheet of about six lines, and that nobody in that yard read it out loud and that nobody in that yard asked them for it and that nobody asked that man which position his sheet stood in or what his crew would do with a run; and that about four people in that yard can read that sheet and that none of those four is the party; and that those four were under awning by three and gone by five; and that that sheet is not the sheet of the fourteen and is not the sheet of positions and is not the fitting-out sheet and is not the four heads on that wall above that stone, and that the man who keeps the leaf wrote on the back of his own slate that a sheet of another crew's came in that day and was not asked for and was not read out, and put the day and the hour against that; and that at about four in the afternoon a woman of about thirty-nine who mends net at the end of that market was asked nothing, and that about nine people stood at the flat of that bench stone and of those nine about four could read what is on the wall above it; and that the sheet at the end of that stall with the fourteen things on it was not read out of, and that not one figure on that sheet moved by one, and that not one of those fourteen was added to and not one was taken off; and that the count of positions was not taken again, and that the fifth word on the back of the other page is not a column and has no figure set against it for any day, and that the three columns of that sheet of days were not counted on that Friday and that count is not in that slip; and that at about half past five the man who keeps the leaf said the shortfall out loud at that bench stone in about 692 words in about nine people; and that the Morrow Line is in the dormant column of that sheet of days, not struck, still one of the nine, still at Orison; and that one line went in on that first column at about six that evening and five on the second, and that the day of this run, counted from Sunday the thirteenth day of March, is the forty-first, and that the first of those two figures is three hundred and seventy-four and the second is one thousand seven hundred and forty-four; of those four columns the third took nothing at all, a boy's hand being at the head of it with his surname on no page and himself not on the water, and the fourth stands unmoved at one hundred and twenty-five and has no hundred and twenty-sixth line in it; and that the figure of four hundred and fifty pence came down no board on that Friday, and that nothing standing was paid, advanced, cut back, bettered, struck or closed that day.*

*Second column, not checkable from a mole, entered on the said Friday at about six in the evening in that shed, and no name is against it: that at about ten in the morning the reason I have put a bearing on the back of an old slate and not on that sheet of days is that a state on a run belongs to the people who use that run and this yard only holds the pen, and that a man with a slate and a length of cord is not a crew and cannot put a word on anything, and that the ninth of those nine names has been on that sheet since the spring without a word against it and is not going to get one from me this month; and that at about half past ten in the morning the reason I did not write the figure of nine into any column is that four people looked at it and not one of them could say what it was for, and that a figure this yard cannot account for is not a figure this yard is entitled to put on a page, and that what I have written instead is that it is there; and that at about half past two in the afternoon the reason nobody in that yard read that other crew's sheet out loud is that a thing said in nine people is a thing nine people have to carry home, and that a sheet four people can read is a sheet four people can be angry at, and that this yard did not ask that man one question and is not going to; and that at about four in the afternoon the reason I have said nothing this week about the stall at the end of that market is that I have said it every week since the twenty-fifth and that the saying of it has not made anybody ask her, and that a thing said every week stops being said and starts being a habit, and a habit is not a record; and that at about six in the evening the reason this slip ends on a number and not on that bearing is that a bearing is a fact about a coast and this shed is a place where things get added up, and that the two of those are different work and the second one is mine.*
