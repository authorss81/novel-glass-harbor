# Chapter 474

*Friday the Fifth Day of November, a Day on Which No Charge Was Placed on That Cistern and on Which No Wage Tuesday Came and on Which the Wall Behind a Reeve's Bench Did Not Come Down at All, a Slate Written Before Anybody Went Down With a Figure of Forty Minutes of Air and a Named Tender on the Surface and Not in the Bell and a Cutoff That Tender Could Pull Without Asking, a Woman of About Thirty-One Going Down on the Ebb and Past the Last Row of Racks to a Place Where Nine Lengths of Metal Were Set Into Nine Holes and Coming Up at About Ten to Eleven With Her Right Hand Shut on Nothing and Not Being Able to Open It for About Forty Minutes Afterwards, and Nobody Thanked, Fourteen More Frames Found in Rows Beyond the First With the Same Mark Cut on Every One of Them and the Same Figure Beside It and the Same Two Figures Given and Not Argued With, a Channel Between Every Two Frames With a Curved Thing in It Worn Deep at the Rim by Something Put In and Held and Taken Out Many Thousands of Times, the Thing on That Floor Being Identified on the Said Friday and on No Other Day of That Week and Said Once Each by Three People Who Do Not Agree, Three People Saying One Thing Each in Their Own Mouths About What It Is and the Three of Them Not Agreeing, a Man of About Thirty-Six Named Corwin Tace Still Setting Marks Around It and Working and Asked One Question About His Own Work and Answering About Marks and Given Nothing and Thanked by Nobody, and the Reason the Party Is There Being a Different Reason on This Day From the Reason It Was on the Twenty-Ninth Day of October Past and Said Out Loud on That Deck in the Yard's Own Mouth, the Shortfall Unmoved at Four Thousand Seven Hundred and Twenty-Two Pence, Being Twenty-Nine Marks, Six Shillings and Tenpence, and the Bag at Twenty-Eight Pence, Being Two Shillings and Fourpence, Against a Wage of Six Hundred and Forty*

Friday the fifth day of November, in about twenty-two fathoms, on the floor of a trench, and on a boat of about nineteen tons that is two days' sail from that harbor.

**The said Friday was not a rota day and was not a wage Tuesday and was not a wipe, and no charge was placed on that cistern on it and the wall behind a reeve's bench in that harbor did not come down on it at all. One hundred and sixty-eight pence came into that yard at about ten in the morning, being a day's work with a boat and two men at a berth at the figure in chalk, and one hundred and sixty-eight pence went out of it at about four in the afternoon, being a day asked for by name, and there are twenty-eight pence in the bag at the close of the said day, being two shillings and fourpence, and the figure that bag is short of the wage of four marks is six hundred and twelve pence, being three marks eleven shillings. Nothing has been in a tin in that shed since the twenty-eighth day of April past, and not one penny of the whole of that trip came out of that bag, out of the owner of any boat in that harbor, or out of the Crown.**

**A woman of thirty of no office is on that deck and is going nowhere and forty marks first after food stand against her and are neither reduced nor paid. Nobody in that crew has said one word about the three nots standing against the eighth of June past and they are a seventeenth week. A boy's hand of twenty-two is at the head of the third column and that boy's surname is on no page of this harbor. Apart from that day's own figures, the whole of what is standing in that harbor is nine numbers and the same nine as yesterday: the notice of the seventeenth of May past at one hundred and seventy-two days, the bellwright of that harbor not asked at one hundred and seventy-four, the two objects of nobody's on that crown at five hundred and twelve days, the bench under that pump bare for the two hundred and seventy-eighth night, the two lights at about a mile off that point at ninety-six days, the six-line sheet at the back of that end stall on its fifty-first day, the paper of four lines in Fish Street on its sixty-sixth, the nine-line sheet with a seal on it in that front street on its eighty-third, and a man of about fifty-one who has said nothing to anybody about a page of six names for a hundred and fourteen days. Nothing in that harbor is paid, advanced, reduced, improved, struck or closed and the figure of four hundred and fifty pence is still on a board at an end stall. A man of about twenty-nine on that boat cannot hear out of the left side of his head, and no figure is set against it anywhere and it is not a motive in anything he does.**

---

**The reason on the slate was written by Sabra Loach, of about thirty-one, in her own words, with the hour against it, and the air limit was a figure of forty minutes, and the tender was named and was on the surface of that boat and not in the bell with his hand on the chain weight and a cutoff he could pull without asking anybody's leave.**

The reason was that nine holes in a bar needed counting and that the far end of that floor had not been counted, and nobody went into that mouth at the top of a flood on that day and nobody went in twice.

She was in the water about fifty minutes and on the bottom about thirty-four of them and came up at about ten to eleven. She came up and she had to open her right hand with the left one before she could get up the ladder out of the bell, and she stood on the deck of that boat with her right hand shut on nothing for about forty minutes and could not open it, and a man of about thirty-seven held it open for her twice and the second time she asked him not to, and nobody thanked her and no figure is set against it anywhere.

---

**There are fifteen frames on that floor in all, and the first of them is the one they found on the Thursday, and the other fourteen are beyond it in rows, and the whole of it is described here in one place on one afternoon because a thing that is described across five days is five things.**

Each frame is about nine feet long and about four wide and is made of bar and not of rolled metal with cut corners and three rivets in it, and each of them carries nine holes in two rows, and in each hole there is a short length of newer metal in a collar with a nut on it, and each of those nine has been in its hole about three weeks and not three days, because there is silt lying in the top of the bright ring round the base of one of them on every frame they put a slate over.

**Every frame carries the same mark cut with a cold chisel, being a hammer and a square crossed with a chisel, and every frame carries the same figure cut beside it, being six hundred and eleven, and there is no year cut on any of them and no name beside any of them.**

Between every two frames there is a channel about the width of a hand, and in each channel there is a curved thing set into the floor, and each of those curved things is the shape of a cupped hand with a hole bored through the middle of the cup, and the inside of each cup is worn deep at the rim and bright across the middle, and the wear is deep at the rim and bright across the middle because that is where a thing about the size of a sheet of paper goes in and is held and comes out again.

Ollum Trevask put a slate over six of those channels and gave the same two figures over all six and was not argued with about either of them, and nobody asked him a third.

**And on the said Friday the party stopped describing and identified the thing, on that floor, in that bell, in the middle of a working day, and it was not said twice and it was not explained on any other day and it is not going to be explained again in this harbor.**

---

**The man of about fifty-one out of the coast town four days down said it first, and he said one thing, and he said it in his own mouth, and he gave his reasons for it.**

"It is a machine for taking an account off a living person and standing one account up where nine of them used to be. That is what those nine are for and that is what those cups are for and that is what the forty racks you have been standing in front of since Sunday are for, and I will tell you how I know, and it is not by being clever. The rack takes a sheet. The cup takes a sheet. Nine frames with nine cups each and forty racks at the end of them and all of them at one height and all of them the same size and all of them the same shape. It was built by people who had nine communities arguing with each other about a disaster for two years and who wanted one version they could all put their finger on, and I am not going to stand here and tell you that was wickedness in the year it was built, because I have read what people wanted in a bad year and wanting one thing everybody can point at is not wickedness, it is an answer. It is a bad answer. It is still an answer, and there is a maker's mark on it and a figure cut beside the mark and a man cut nine fittings into it three weeks ago, and that is what I have to say."

**Sabra Loach, of about thirty-one, said it second, and she did not agree with him, and she said why.**

"I do not care in the least whose hands it was in and I am not going to stand on this deck being impressed by a maker. It is a machine that takes a thing out of a person who is alive. There are nine cups in a frame and there are frames in rows and I counted them from the ladder this morning, and I am going to tell you that counting is on the face of that floor and a man on a mole could have it off a plan, and there is no sense in nine of us sitting here being clever about an arithmetic that anybody can do. What I am not going to write down is what the nine were for, and nobody on this boat is going to write that down either, and that is the difference between a number and a reason and the number is the one that costs nothing to keep. There are nine of us on this boat. That is the whole of what I have to say about it and I have said it and I am not going to say it a second time this week."

**Calder Marr, the holder of that writ, said it third, and he did not agree with either of them, and he said what he did not know as well as what he did.**

"It was built to hold nine accounts at a height where a man could walk round them and look at all nine at once, and that is the honest reading of that geometry, and the frame is the old part and the nine fittings are the new part and I have a man on this boat who gave me those two figures on the Thursday and did, and I am not going to pretend that three weeks of fittings changed what a thing is for. What I will not do is write down which of those two readings I hope for. I do not know whether those cups were made to take a sheet out of a rack or out of a hand, and I have been in a hand twice in my life and I am not the man to decide it for nine of us, and I would rather be wrong on a page in my own hand than be right in a mouth I have not opened."

**All three went into the second column of that leaf that evening in the order they were said, and the man who keeps the leaf wrote at the foot of them that they do not agree, that the party does not know which of them is right, that none of them has been asked to say it again, and that nobody in that boat is going to vote on it.**

---

**At about ten past one in the afternoon a man of about thirty-six named Corwin Tace came up out of the dark on the far side of that floor with his tool roll under his arm and about four inches of iron in his hand, and he went on working.**

He had been on that floor about three weeks and was working under a warrant with a seal on it and no figure on it, and he was asked one question that day and it was about his own work and not about the frame, and he answered it in the words of a man who lays out survey for a living.

"I set the marks. They go in at the ends and the ends are about four inches apart and I do not set them any other way because there is a paper that says four inches and I have it in the roll and you do not, and that is not the book the man of about thirty-four keeps on the second lay and it is not on his shelf and I have not asked him for it. That is my answer and it is the only one I have got. You can measure them yourself if you have a rule and half an hour."

Nobody gave him anything. Nobody thanked him, he was not argued with, nothing was settled by anything he said, and he was in the water again at about half past two with the same slate conditions as the woman who went before him and about the same air, was in the water about forty-five minutes and was on the bottom about thirty of them and came up at about a quarter past three and set two more before the light went.

---

**AND AT ABOUT HALF PAST FIVE IN THE AFTERNOON ON THAT DECK, IN FRONT OF ALL NINE OF THEM, THE HOLDER OF THAT WRIT SAID OUT LOUD THAT THE REASON THAT PARTY WAS THERE WAS A DIFFERENT REASON FROM THE ONE IT HAD BEEN, AND HE SAID IT IN THE YARD'S OWN MOUTH AND PUT THE HOUR AGAINST IT.**

"The reason this boat left that harbor on the twenty-ninth day of October past is written on a slate in that shed and it is in my own words and it is one sentence, and the sentence is that we are going down here to find out whether a sheet of about four feet of paper answers, and if it answers, what it is answering. That is the reason I gave a room over a fish market and it is the reason on nine slates in a book in a woman's coat.

"The reason on this deck at half past five on the fifth day of November is that we are going to get the shape of that frame up into daylight where a man on a mole can put a hand on a page and see the same thing he was told about, and that is a different sentence and it is a better one and I am not going to pretend the first one led to it. The first one is wrong. I do not know by how much and I am not going to guess out loud in front of nine people.

"And I am not going to use that sheet in the pocket of my coat to decide where this boat goes tomorrow. That is not me being careful. That is a rule a woman of thirty wrote out loud in a room over a fish market before about eleven people who cannot read, and if I broke it out here in twenty-two fathoms where nobody could find out, then the rule was only ever a rule for the water we could be seen in."

Nobody argued with him. Nobody thanked him. Nobody asked him which of the three accounts he believed, and the question a man of about thirty-four asked at the head of a slip on the twenty-fourth day of October past was not asked again on that day and was not answered on that day.

---

**THE THING ON THAT FLOOR IS A MACHINE, AND IT WAS MADE BY PEOPLE, AND IT CARRIES A MAKER'S MARK AND A COUNT CUT BESIDE THE MARK, AND IT IS NOT A MONSTER AND IT IS NOT A CURSE AND IT IS NOT A GOD AND IT IS NOT A TOMB AND IT IS NOT A GHOST, AND NOBODY ON THAT BOAT SAID ON THAT DAY THAT IT HAD TAKEN ANYTHING OUT OF ANY PERSON BECAUSE IT WANTED ANYTHING. THREE PEOPLE SAID ONE THING EACH ABOUT IT AND THE THREE OF THEM DO NOT AGREE AND ALL THREE ARE IN ONE COLUMN IN THE ORDER THEY WERE SAID AND NONE OF THEM WAS ASKED TO SAY IT AGAIN. THE FORTY RACKS ARE THE FORTY RACKS AND THE MAN WHO KEEPS THE LEAF SAID OUT LOUD THAT HE WAS NOT GOING BACK TO ANY PAGE TO CHANGE ONE WORD OF WHAT HE HAD WRITTEN ABOUT THEM ON THE THIRTY-FIRST DAY OF OCTOBER PAST. A MAN OF ABOUT THIRTY-SIX IS STILL SETTING MARKS AROUND IT AND WAS GIVEN NOTHING AND WAS THANKED BY NOBODY. THE REASON THAT PARTY IS THERE IS A DIFFERENT REASON FROM THE ONE IT WAS. AND THAT SHEET IS FOUR THOUSAND SEVEN HUNDRED AND TWENTY-TWO PENCE SHORT AT THE CLOSE OF THIS DAY, BEING TWENTY-NINE MARKS, SIX SHILLINGS AND TENPENCE, AND NO FIGURE HAS BEEN ASKED OF ANYBODY TO MAKE IT OTHERWISE.**

and that one hundred and sixty-eight pence came into that yard and one hundred and sixty-eight went out of it, and that twenty-eight plus one hundred and sixty-eight less one hundred and sixty-eight is twenty-eight pence at the close of the said day, being two shillings and fourpence, being short of the wage of four marks by six hundred and twelve pence, being three marks eleven shillings; and that nothing went onto the fitting-out sheet on that day and that it stands at ten thousand six hundred and forty pence in and fifteen thousand three hundred and sixty-two pence out and is four thousand seven hundred and twenty-two pence short, being twenty-nine marks, six shillings and tenpence.*

*Second column, not checkable from a mole, entered on the said Friday at about half past nine in the evening on that deck, and no name is against it: that the reason the whole of that floor was described in one place on one afternoon and not across five days is in the yard's own mouth, and is that a thing described across five days is five things, and that a stranger with a pencil cannot take five descriptions off a page and get one floor; and that the reason the three accounts were not reconciled is that the party is nine people on a boat and not a room, and that the man of about fifty-one out of the coast town four days down, the woman of about thirty-one, and the holder of that writ were each asked the same question and each gave one answer and none of them was asked to give it better, and that a party that votes on what a thing is has stopped being a party and started being a jury, and there is no jury on this coast and there is not going to be one; and that the reason the holder of that writ said his own reason was wrong out loud, in front of nine men, on a deck, with an hour against it, is his own, and is that a man who has written a wrong reason on a slate and has carried it down that coast with it has an obligation to a page rather than to a room, and that the room would have been easier; and that the reason he said he was not going to use the sheet of about four feet to decide where that boat goes tomorrow is that a rule read out in a room over a fish market that only holds in rooms where somebody can watch a man is not a rule, and that he said so out loud where nobody could find out and did it anyway; and that the reason the question asked at the head of a slip on the twenty-fourth day of October past was neither asked again nor answered on that day is the yard's own mouth, and is that the man who asked it is on that deck and he asked it once and he has not asked it twice and nobody in that party has an answer that would not be a guess wearing a coat; and that the reason the question asked at the head of a slip on the twenty-fourth day of October past was neither asked again nor answered on that day is the yard's own mouth, and is that a man who has asked a question once and been thanked for asking it has been given a reason to stop, and that this yard is not going to hand him one.
