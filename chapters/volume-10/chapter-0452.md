# Chapter 452

*Thursday the Fourteenth Day of October, a Rota Day, and the Ninetieth Time That Cistern Was Charged and About Half of It Went Over the Stone Lip and Nobody Walked on Any of It and the Top Name on That Rota Board Is Still Off It, a Sheet of Good Paper Bought Blank at a Counter in a Room off That Market for a Penny out of a Man's Own Coat and Blank at the Head of It for Forty Minutes While Nine People Stood in a Shed and Waited, the Head of It Written Afterwards in the Holder's Own Hand Saying What the Money Is For and What It Does Not Promise, Six Thousand and Eighty Pence Against Two Lines on It by the Close of the Said Day, Being Thirty-Eight Marks, a Name and an Hour Against Each Line, and a Sheet That Carries No Count of Persons on It, a Pump Hand of About Forty-Four Named Ivo Skell Who Has Run Compressors for Nineteen Years and Who Is Asked What a Compressor Wants and Who Answers the Question Nobody Asked Him, a Second Refusal From a Woman of About Thirty-One Who Crews a Lighter and Who Gives a Reason and Is Not Asked Again, One Hundred and Sixty-Eight Pence Coming Into That Yard and One Hundred and Sixty-Eight Going Out of It, and the Bag at Twenty-Eight Pence, Being Two Shillings and Fourpence*

Thursday the fourteenth day of October, in that shed, at the head of that third lay, and at the back of that market.

**The said Thursday was a rota day and was not a wage Tuesday and was not a wipe. The cistern at the back of that market was charged for the ninetieth time at about eight in the morning and about half of it went over the stone lip, and the man who charged it wrote the date on the board and then looked at the top of that board for about as long as it takes to write a date, and the top name has been off it since before the middle of last month. One hundred and sixty-eight pence came into that yard at about ten in the morning and one hundred and sixty-eight pence went out of it at about four in the afternoon for a day asked for by name, being a caulker of about twenty-three who crews in no boat and holds no office and is on no sheet, and there are twenty-eight pence in the bag at the close of the said day, being two shillings and fourpence, and the figure that bag is short of the wage of four marks is six hundred and twelve pence, being three marks eleven shillings. Nothing has been in a tin in that shed since the twenty-eighth day of April past, and not one penny of what was bought on the said day comes out of that bag, out of the owner of any boat, or out of the Crown.**

**And the far bench was occupied from ten until four and was not approached, and the holder of that writ did not go over to him. Mara Venn was in that harbor and going nowhere, of no office, not on the crew, not restored, not forgiven, forty marks first after food, neither reduced nor paid, and nobody took her name off either sheet it is on and nobody thanked her. Tobias Wren was on the crown of that mole from ten until four, being sixty-one, and did not move the eleventh piece. The notice of the seventeenth of May past is on its wall in a different street for one hundred and fifty days. The bellwright of that harbor has not been asked for one hundred and fifty-two days. The three nots standing against the eighth of June past are a fourteenth week. The hand of a boy of twenty-two is at the head of the third column and that boy's surname is on no page of this harbor. The two objects of nobody's on that crown are four hundred and ninety days old. The bench under that pump is bare for the two hundred and fifty-sixth night. Two lights at about a mile off that point have not moved in seventy-four days. A man of about fifty-one has said nothing to anybody about a page of six names for ninety-two days. The sheet of six lines at the back of that end stall is on that wall for the twenty-ninth day, the paper of four lines in Fish Street is on that door for the forty-fourth day and has been answered by nobody, and the nine-line sheet with a seal on it is in that front street for the sixty-first day.**

---

The sheet was bought at a counter in a room off that market at about half past nine in the morning for a penny out of the holder's own coat, and it is about three feet long, and it came into that shed blank at the head of it.

There were nine people in that shed and the sheet was on the bench stone with its head end towards the door, and nobody wrote on it for about forty minutes.

Ollum Trevask, of about thirty-four, of the second lay, who keeps the whole fouling ladder in that harbor in his own book and has never shown it to anybody, said the same thing he said about the other sheet in that harbor, in the same words, and nobody told him he was repeating himself.

"There is a head of paper at the top of that thing and it is empty, and I am not going to be the one who writes on it, and anybody who wants to write on it can write on it and I will not help and I will not stop them."

Just before ten past ten a woman of about fifty-eight with an end stall and a slate behind her said that if a page of money and a page of rules were both blank at the head of them for forty minutes then the only difference between them was what people were willing to write, and that she was not going to be one of the nine and had said so in the room over the fish market and had not been asked again.

At about ten past ten the holder of that writ picked up the pencil and wrote the head of it, and it took him about four minutes, and he read it out afterwards to the nine people in that shed and they did not stop him at any word because the eleventh sentence in the other room says a person who cannot read may have a line read to them, and the head of that sheet has a line at the foot of it that says that is allowed.

**It says that the money is for a trip to a shelf under the drowned city, to about nine men, for two days down that coast road and two days back. It says that the money does not promise anybody a find, and that if it comes back with nothing then nothing is owed to anybody by anybody, and that if it comes back with something then what is found comes up with the name of the man who found it and the hour. It says that no penny of it comes out of that yard's bag, which stands at twenty-eight pence, being two shillings and fourpence. It says that the sheet of four heads on the wall in the room over a fish market is not changed by anything on it. It says that the refusals given about this trip stand on it at the same size as the figures, whether or not the person who gave one put money in. And it says, at the foot, in a line under all the rest, that the sheet carries no count of persons and has no figure on it for how many people are on it, and that the figure that matters on it is money.**

A man of about fifty who crews a lighter asked whether that last line was meant to keep the number of people off it so that nobody could add it up and work out how many were in it. The holder of that writ said that it was meant to keep the number of people off it so that nobody could add it up, and did not say which of the two reasons was the real one, and a woman of about forty-three who keeps a store on the second lay, who was in that shed, said out loud that it was the second reason, and that a person who puts four marks in a sheet in this harbor is telling everybody in the room how much they have, and that this harbor does not need telling twice.

Two lines went on that sheet on the said day, with a name and an hour against each of them, and they were the only two lines on it by the close of that day. The first was nine figures of four marks each, being five thousand seven hundred and sixty pence, and it went against nine names that were written against those nine lines on the Wednesday before, and the second was two figures of one mark each, being three hundred and twenty pence, and it went against two names written on the same Wednesday in the same room. **The six who had said out loud what the money was for on the Wednesday said it again in that shed before they wrote the figure, and the other five of the nine said nothing at all while they wrote theirs, and nobody asked either lot one question about it.** And it came to six thousand and eighty pence, being thirty-eight marks, and a woman of about fifty-eight with an end stall and a slate behind her asked out loud, in that shed, whether the sheet was going to carry a count of the people on it, and was told that it was not, and said that was right, and that she had said in the room over the fish market that she was not going to be one of the nine, and that a page which counts how many people are on it is a page a man reads twice, and that a page which counts how much money is on it is a page a man reads once and pays.**

---

At about half past two in the afternoon a man of about forty-four came into that shed with a tool bag he did not put down and said his name was Ivo Skell and that he had run a compressor for nineteen years and that he was in nobody's crew and would not be anybody's and that he had come to be asked what a compressor wants.

He was asked what a compressor wants and he answered that first and then he answered the other thing.

"A compressor wants air, and it wants to be told what the bottom is, and it wants the pressure on the face of that gauge to be a number somebody wrote down before the bell went down and not a number somebody decided was about right afterwards." He put his hand on the gauge on the wall of that shed, which is a yard gauge and not a sea one and reads nothing. "And a compressor wants the tender to be somebody who has not got a reason of his own to keep you down there, because a man on a chain and a man on a line in that bell and a man in a boat are three men with three reasons and there is only ever one of them who is being paid to go down."

"And what happens when the plan fails."

"Then it fails at about four fathoms and not at forty, and everything in that boat is made of stuff you can lift." He took his hand off the gauge. "That is the whole answer. You build it so that being wrong is cheap, and if you cannot make being wrong cheap then you are not going down there at all, and that is not a figure of money, that is a thing you do to the boat."

Nessa Pike wrote that in the second column with the hour against it, in the yard's own mouth and not in his, and he read it over and said that he had not said it that way and that it was right, and that he was not going to be asked to say it again.

**And a woman of about thirty-one who crews a lighter in that harbor was asked in that shed at about four o'clock in the afternoon and said no, and gave a reason, and the reason was that she was asked to go out on a Tuesday in August by three men in a coat for a day and went, and that it was the same three men, and that she has not crewed a day for anybody out of a coat since and that she is not starting on a sheet. She said that out loud in front of nine people and she did not raise her voice about it and she was not asked again on any of the eight days after it.**

---

**THAT PAGE HAD SIX THOUSAND AND EIGHTY PENCE AGAINST TWO LINES ON IT BY THE CLOSE OF THE SAID DAY, BEING THIRTY-EIGHT MARKS, AND TWO LINES OF COST ON IT BEING NINETY-SIX PENCE FOR A PATCH OF HOSE WITH CANVAS, IRON STRAPS AND NAILS AND TWO HUNDRED AND FOUR PENCE FOR TWELVE FATHOMS OF THREE-INCH LINE, AND NONE OF EITHER FIGURE CAME OUT OF THAT BAG, WHICH STANDS AT TWENTY-EIGHT PENCE, BEING TWO SHILLINGS AND FOURPENCE. A PAGE THAT COUNTS HOW MANY PEOPLE ARE ON IT IS A PAGE A MAN READS TWICE, AND A PAGE THAT COUNTS HOW MUCH MONEY IS ON IT IS A PAGE A MAN READS ONCE AND PAYS, AND THE SHEET ON THAT BENCH STONE CARRIES THE SECOND FIGURE AND NOT THE FIRST. A MAN OF ABOUT THIRTY-FOUR REFUSED TO WRITE THE HEAD OF A BLANK PAGE IN THAT SHED AND SAID IT AGAIN IN THE SAME WORDS HE SAID IT IN A ROOM FOUR HUNDRED YARDS AWAY, AND THE ONLY THING THAT CHANGED BETWEEN THE TWO PAGES IS WHAT PEOPLE WERE WILLING TO WRITE ON THEM. A PUMP HAND OF ABOUT FORTY-FOUR SAID THAT BEING WRONG HAS TO BE CHEAP AND THAT A FIGURE OF MONEY IS NOT A THING YOU DO TO A BOAT, AND HE WAS NOT THANKED AND WAS NOT ASKED TO SAY IT AGAIN. A WOMAN OF ABOUT THIRTY-ONE SAID NO IN FRONT OF NINE PEOPLE AND WAS NOT ASKED AGAIN, AND HER REASON NAMES THREE MEN IN A COAT AND A TUESDAY IN AUGUST AND NOBODY IN THAT HARBOR WENT AND ASKED HER ONE THING ABOUT IT. THE CISTERN WAS CHARGED FOR THE NINETIETH TIME AND WALKED ON BY NONE OF IT.**

*First column, entered on Thursday the fourteenth day of October next at about half past nine in the evening in that shed: that the said day was a rota day and was not a wage Tuesday and was not a wipe, and that the cistern at the back of that market was charged for the ninetieth time at about eight in the morning and about half of it went over the stone lip, and that the top name on that rota board has been off it since before the middle of the last month and was off it at eight in the morning and was off it at half past nine; and that one hundred and sixty-eight pence came into that yard and one hundred and sixty-eight went out of it, and that twenty-eight plus one hundred and sixty-eight less one hundred and sixty-eight is twenty-eight pence at the close of the said day, being two shillings and fourpence, being short of the wage of four marks by six hundred and twelve pence, being three marks eleven shillings; and that a sheet of good paper about three feet long was bought at a counter in a room off that market for a penny out of the holder's own coat and was blank at the head of it for forty minutes with nine people standing in that shed, and that a man of about thirty-four of the second lay said in that shed that he was not going to be the one who writes on it; and that the head of that sheet says what the money is for, that it does not promise anybody a find, that no penny of it comes out of that bag, that the four heads on the other wall are not changed by anything on it, that the refusals stand on it at the same size as the figures, and that it carries no count of persons and has no figure on it for how many people are on it; and that two lines went on that sheet on the said day with a name and an hour against each, and that they came to six thousand and eighty pence, and that two lines of cost went on it, and that a pump hand of about forty-four named Ivo Skell was asked what a compressor wants and answered it and then answered a question nobody asked him, and that a woman of about thirty-one who crews a lighter said no at about four in the afternoon in front of nine people with a reason in her own mouth and was not asked again on any of the eight days after it.*

*Second column, not checkable from a mole, entered on the said Thursday at about half past nine in the evening in that shed, and no name is against it: that the reason a blank page stood blank for forty minutes in that shed is that the last blank page in that harbor stood blank for forty minutes in a room four hundred yards away, and that a man of about thirty-four said the same sentence in both rooms and was not told he was repeating himself, and that what the two pages have in common is that nobody in that harbor was willing to be the first name on either of them; and that the reason the woman of about fifty-eight with the end stall said that out loud is in her own mouth, and that she is not on that sheet and was not asked a second time about it; and that the reason the head of that sheet carries no count of persons is in the holder's own mouth and in a woman of about fifty-eight's, and that a person who puts four marks into a sheet in that harbor is telling everybody in the room what they have, and that a page which counts how many people are on it is a page a man reads twice; and that the reason no figure of the whole of the trip is against anything on that sheet is that the trip has not been costed and that a cost goes on a sheet when somebody has said out loud what it is for; and that the reason a pump hand of about forty-four said that being wrong has to be cheap is in his own mouth and is a thing about a boat and not a figure of money, and that Nessa Pike wrote it in this yard's own mouth and not in his, and that he read it and said it was right and that he was not going to be asked to say it again; and that the reason a woman of about thirty-one said no and named three men in a coat and a Tuesday in August is her own and belongs to her, and that nobody in that harbor has been and asked her one thing about it and is not going to; and that the reason the man of about thirty-three of the second lay who said no on the Wednesday is not on that sheet with a name against a figure is that a refusal is not a contribution and that his reason stands in the yard's own mouth with the hour against it and no name; and that the standing list entered in full on the fourteenth day of August past is not entered in full on the said Thursday and stands, and no part of it was paid, reduced, advanced or improved on that day.*
