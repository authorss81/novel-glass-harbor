# Chapter 488

*Saturday the Twentieth Day of November, a Day on Which No Charge Was Placed on That Cistern and on Which No Wage Tuesday Came and on Which the Wall Behind a Reeve's Bench Did Not Come Down at All, a Second Pair of Hands for a Day off That Same Supply Boat Going onto the Sheet That Carries the Money at About Ten in the Morning at One Hundred and Sixty-Eight Pence, Being One Mark and Eightpence, and Nothing Going into That Sheet in Any Hand, the Fourth and Last of Those Four Hired Days of Air and the Twenty-First and the Twenty-Second of November Not Being Opened Under Any Line and Nobody Asked Why, a Man of About Thirty-Six Named Corwin Tace Still Setting Marks Around That Machine and Working on That Floor Since Before That Party Came Out Here in October and Given Nothing, Three People Saying One Thing Each About What Is on That Floor and Said Once Each and the Three of Them Not Agreeing, Being the Master of a Boat of About Nineteen Tons and a Pump Hand of About Forty-Four and a Man of About Thirty-Seven, and Nothing Explained a Second Time and Nothing Settled by Any of Them and No Vote, a Man of About Thirty-Eight Working That Compressor on the Day After the Day He Came Up With Something That Was Not His, the Shortfall at Thirteen Thousand Nine Hundred and Seventy-Six Pence, Being Eighty-Seven Marks, Four Shillings and Eightpence, and the Bag at Twenty-Eight Pence, Being Two Shillings and Fourpence, Against a Wage of Six Hundred and Forty*

Saturday the twentieth day of November, in about twenty-two fathoms, on the floor of a trench, and on a boat of about nineteen tons that is two days' sail from that harbor.

**The said Saturday was not a rota day and was not a wage Tuesday and was not a wipe, and no charge was placed on that cistern on it and the wall behind a reeve's bench in that harbor did not come down on it at all. A second pair of hands for a day off that same supply boat went onto the sheet that carries the money at about ten in the morning at one hundred and sixty-eight pence, being one mark and eightpence, and it is the fourth of the four days hired off that supply boat on the seventeenth of November and the last of them, and no line has been opened for the twenty-first or the twenty-second and nobody asked for one. One hundred and sixty-eight pence came into that yard at about ten in the morning, being a day's work with a boat and two men at a berth at the figure in chalk, and one hundred and sixty-eight pence went out of it at about four in the afternoon, being a day asked for by name, and there are twenty-eight pence in the bag at the close of the said day, being two shillings and fourpence, and the figure that bag is short of the wage of four marks is six hundred and twelve pence, being three marks eleven shillings. That sheet stands at twenty-four thousand six hundred and sixteen pence out, being one hundred and fifty-three marks, eleven shillings and fourpence, and ten thousand six hundred and forty pence in, being sixty-six marks, six shillings and eightpence, and it is thirteen thousand nine hundred and seventy-six pence short, being eighty-seven marks, four shillings and eightpence. Nothing has been in a tin in that shed since the twenty-ninth day of April past.**

**The nine that a stranger in that harbor could have read off any wall are the nine below, and not one of them was found by looking: the notice of the seventeenth of May past, one hundred and eighty-six days; the bellwright of that harbor, not asked for one hundred and eighty-eight; the two objects of nobody's on that crown, five hundred and twenty-six days; the bench under that pump, bare for the two hundred and ninety-second night; the two lights at about a mile off that point, one hundred and ten days; the six-line sheet at the back of that end stall, its sixty-fifth day; the paper of four lines in Fish Street, its eightieth; the nine-line sheet with a seal on it in that front street, its ninety-seventh; and a man of about fifty-one who has said nothing to anybody about a page of six names for a hundred and twenty-eight days. Nineteen winters are disputed in that coast and unpaid. A woman of about fifty-eight who sweeps out the room at the top of Fish Street on Sundays has not been sent for, has not been thanked, and is not a teacher. A man of about twenty-nine on that boat cannot hear out of the left side of his head, and no figure is set against it anywhere and it is not a motive in anything he does.**

---

**A slate was written before anybody went down and a man of about fifty-one out of a coast town four days down wrote his own reason on it, being that there is a matter of about two inches of water standing in that room on the top of that tide and that the bottom of the long rack is in it, and that paper standing in water in a room like that is paper that will have gone.**

He went down at about ten o'clock in the morning on that ebb under that slate with a figure of forty minutes of air on it and a named tender on the surface and not in the bell with his hand on the chain weight and a cutoff he could pull without asking anybody's leave, and the air was out of cylinders hired off that supply boat on the seventeenth of November. He was in the water about fifty minutes and on the bottom about twenty-nine of them and came up at about ten to eleven, and he came up with two bundles and no figure of any kind against him and nobody thanked him.

---

**And a man of about thirty-six named Corwin Tace was on that floor, and he had been on it since before that party came out here in October, and he was setting marks.**

He came up at about eleven o'clock with his roll under his arm and about four inches of iron in his hand, and he went down again at about half past two with the same conditions that a man of that crew works to, and he was on the bottom about thirty of them and came up at about a quarter past three, and nobody asked him a question about the room, the racks, the paper, the frames, the wall or the man of about thirty-eight who came up out of it on the day before, and the one question he was asked that day was about his own work.

"How many are you putting in on that side."

"Four," he said. "Two at the ends and two a fathom in, and they go in at the ends and the ends are about four inches apart and I do not set them any other way because there is a paper that says four inches and I have it in the roll and you do not." He put the roll down on the deck and did not open it. "That is my answer and it is the only one I have got and you can measure them yourself if you have a rule and half an hour."

Nobody gave him anything. Nobody thanked him, he was not argued with, nothing was settled by anything he said, and nobody on that boat said the word Crown out loud to him or about him on that day, and the word that would name the seal on his warrant is in no mouth in that boat and is in no mouth in that harbor.

---

**And three people said one thing each about what is on that floor, on that after deck, between about four and about five in the afternoon, and each of them said it once and nothing was explained a second time and nothing was settled by any of them and nobody voted.**

**The man of about thirty-seven said the first, and it was a thing he had not said before and he said that he had not known it was there on the Sunday.**

"On the far side of the last frame there is a bar set into the floor at each end, about the height of my own chest, running about nine foot, and it is worn bright along the top for about two foot in the middle and dull at both ends. **I have been in that room four times since the Saturday and I am telling you that is a rail.** I have not told anybody before because I did not see it on the Sunday, when I went down to count the racks and stood at the front of the fifth one, and I do not know how long it has been under that silt, and a man who counts things for a living can see a rail and can be wrong about what a rail is for, and that is all I have got."

**The pump hand of about forty-four said the second, with nineteen years of compressors behind it and no rattle in it whatever.**

"A floor is for a man to stand on while he does a thing that takes a long time. That is what a floor is. That floor has fifteen long things on it and no roof over them and nowhere on it to sit down, and if a floor is for a man then a man was standing on it for years, and **I have worked that out and I worked it out on the Wednesday, after the three of them had said what they said and I had listened to all of it, and I would not want to be told by anybody on this deck that I have not.** And a rail on a floor like that is not there for a man to hold on to. It is there for a man to stand beside while something heavy comes past him. I have run air into twenty holds on this coast. I know what a rail is for and it is not for hanging on to."

**And the master of that boat of about nineteen tons said the third, and he said it with his hand flat on the crate, and he said it once.**

"A rail is for moving a thing, and there are rails on my boat under her forepeak where a man cannot get at all, and the reason a man cannot get at is that there is something on top of it that gets moved, and it gets moved often, and by two men who cannot get their hands round it. I have sanded up over a bar off the Point exactly the way that floor is sanded up, two inches on one side of a thing and not on the other, and that is a current, and a current does that to everything it goes over for long enough."

**And that is three accounts of a floor and of a bar nine foot long, and the three of them do not agree, and the man of about thirty-seven is not asked to say his again and the pump hand is not asked to say his again and the master is not asked to say his again, and nobody on that deck picked one of the three and nobody wrote down which of the three he thought was right, and the man who keeps the leaf put the three in the second column in the order they were said and wrote at the foot of them that the party does not know.**

**And at about half past five, without being asked by anybody, the master said one more thing on that deck and it was about his own boat and it was nine words long.**

"I said on Thursday what I am doing with my people. I am not saying it again."

Nobody asked him for a date. Nobody asked him to stay. Nobody thanked him.

**A MAN OF ABOUT THIRTY-SEVEN FOUND A BAR ABOUT THE HEIGHT OF HIS OWN CHEST RUNNING ABOUT NINE FOOT ON THE FAR SIDE OF THE LAST OF THOSE FIFTEEN FRAMES, WORN BRIGHT ALONG THE TOP FOR ABOUT TWO FOOT IN THE MIDDLE AND DULL AT BOTH ENDS. A PUMP HAND OF ABOUT FORTY-FOUR SAID IT IS THERE FOR A MAN TO STAND BESIDE WHILE SOMETHING HEAVY COMES PAST. THE MASTER OF THAT BOAT SAID A RAIL IS FOR MOVING A THING. THE THREE OF THEM DO NOT AGREE AND NONE OF THEM WAS ASKED TO SAY IT AGAIN AND NOBODY VOTED AND NOTHING WAS SETTLED. A MAN OF ABOUT THIRTY-SIX NAMED CORWIN TACE WAS STILL SETTING MARKS ON THAT FLOOR, FOUR IN AT THE ENDS AND FOUR IN AND FOUR INCHES APART, AND WAS GIVEN NOTHING AND WAS THANKED BY NOBODY. A SECOND PAIR OF HANDS FOR A DAY WENT ON THAT SHEET AT ONE HUNDRED AND SIXTY-EIGHT PENCE. AND THAT SHEET IS THIRTEEN THOUSAND NINE HUNDRED AND SEVENTY-SIX PENCE SHORT AT THE CLOSE OF THIS DAY, BEING EIGHTY-SEVEN MARKS, FOUR SHILLINGS AND EIGHTPENCE, AND NO FIGURE HAS BEEN ASKED OF ANYBODY IN THAT HARBOR TO MAKE IT OTHERWISE.**

*Second column, not checkable from a mole, entered on the said Saturday at about half past nine in the evening on that deck, and no name is against it: that the reason three people were asked on that deck for one thing each about what is on that floor is in the yard's own mouth, and is that a party which is looking at a floor it has not explained to anybody is entitled to three plain descriptions of it from three people who work for their living, and that a man who has stood at the front of a rack and counted it, a man who has run air into twenty holds on this coast, and a man with a boat of about nineteen tons of his own have all three got the use of their hands on different things and all three of them are worth listening to and not one of them is worth more than the other two; and that the reason the three of them were not asked to say it again is the man who keeps the leaf's own, and is that a thing explained twice on a deck becomes the deck's opinion, and that three voices that do not agree with each other are worth three times as much on a page as one voice that has been talked into agreement with the other two, and that the one that carries a roll in his own hand is a working man under a paper and the other two are working men off their own hands, and that three of those are not the same thing and a page that says they are has thrown one of them away; and that the reason the setter of marks was asked one question about how many he was putting in and not one question about the room is his own, and is that a man who is asked what he thinks about somebody else's work gets an answer about somebody else's work, and that a man who is asked how many is being asked how many, and that a warrant is a paper and the man who carries it works under it and is not obliged to hold an opinion about anything underneath it; and that the reason the master of that boat said nine words about his own people and would not say them a tenth time is his own, and is that a man who has said on Thursday what he is going to do with a boat and two men and a boy has said it, and that a man who repeats a number is showing a room that it has not moved, and that a man repeating a thing is showing a room that he wants to be asked again, and that this yard is not going to be a room that asks; and that the reason the air of that Saturday was the last of the four that were bought, and that nothing was opened under any other line, is the man who keeps the leaf's own, and is that a man who buys air for four days on a Wednesday is describing four days and not a week, and that a man who opens a fifth in front of nine people has to say out loud what the fifth is for, and that the party is going to be off that floor on the Sunday and that is a different sentence and not an accounting; and that the reason every one of the standing items of that harbor was where it had been since the spring is the yard's own mouth, and is that nineteen winters are disputed on that coast and are disputed on a Saturday, and that a woman of about fifty-eight who sweeps out a room at the top of a street every Sunday of that year was not sent for on that Saturday and is not going to be sent for, and that the nineteen winters and the woman of about fifty-eight are both on that same page of that same book and neither of them is on any sheet in that boat.*
