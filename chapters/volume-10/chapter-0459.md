# Chapter 459

*Thursday the Twenty-First Day of October, a Rota Day, and the Ninety-Second Time That Cistern Was Charged and Walked on by None of It, the Mouth of a Trench in the Floor of the Unlit Shelf Found at About Ten Past Nine in the Morning in About Twenty-Two Fathoms, the Water at That Mouth Coming Up About Two Feet in the Twenty Minutes After the Flood and Not Going Down Again Inside the Working Day, a Man Down in a Bell at About Half Past Ten in the Morning With a Sheet of About Four Feet in an Oilcloth Fold in the Inside Pocket of a Coat Inside a Bell, Something in That Trench Answering It, and That Answer Written Into the Second Column of a Leaf at About Six in the Evening With the Hour Against It and No Name Against It, and a Line of Marks Set Out on the Floor of That Mouth About Three Weeks Old With a Piece of Worked Lead Beside Them About the Size of a Man's Palm, Nine Notches in It and Three of Them Deeper Than the Other Six and a Place About the Size of a Thumb Left Out of It on Purpose and No Figure and No Number of Any Kind, and a Man of About Thirty-Six Named Corwin Tace Who Says He Is Setting Marks and That Setting Marks Is What He Does*

Thursday the twenty-first day of October, in about twenty-two fathoms, and in that shed two days behind them.

**The said Thursday was a rota day and was not a wage Tuesday and was not a wipe, and the cistern at the back of that market was charged for the ninety-second time at about eight in the morning and was walked on by none of it, and the top name on that rota board is still off it. Three hundred and thirty-six pence came into that yard at about ten in the morning, being two days at the figure in chalk, and three hundred and thirty-six pence went out of it at about four in the afternoon, being two days asked for by name, and there are twenty-eight pence in the bag at the close of the said day, being two shillings and fourpence, and the figure that bag is short of the wage of four marks is six hundred and twelve pence, being three marks eleven shillings.**

**And the far bench was occupied from ten until four and was not approached by anybody. Mara Venn was in that harbor and going nowhere, of no office, not on the crew, not restored, not forgiven, forty marks first after food, neither reduced nor paid. Tobias Wren was on the crown of that mole from ten until four, being sixty-one, and did not move the eleventh piece. The notice of the seventeenth of May past is on its wall in a different street for one hundred and fifty-seven days. The bellwright of that harbor has not been asked for one hundred and fifty-nine days, the chest is shut, and the eight marks and a day of a boat have not been argued. The three nots standing against the eighth of June past are a fifteenth week. The hand of a boy of twenty-two is at the head of the third column and that boy's surname is on no page of this harbor. The two objects of nobody's on that crown are four hundred and ninety-seven days old. The bench under that pump is bare for the two hundred and sixty-third night. Two lights at about a mile off that point have not moved in eighty-one days. A man of about fifty-one has said nothing to anybody about a page of six names for ninety-nine days. The sheet of six lines at the back of that end stall is on that wall for the thirty-sixth day, the paper of four lines in Fish Street is on that door for the fifty-first day and has been answered by nobody, and the nine-line sheet with a seal on it is in that front street for the sixty-eighth day.**

---

The trench was found at about ten past nine in the morning on the ebb, in about twenty-two fathoms, and it is a mouth and not an object.

**It is about nine feet across at the top of it and it goes down into a shelf that has not been measured, and the water at that mouth comes up about two feet in the twenty minutes after the flood begins, and it does not go down again inside the working day.** The party found that out the hard way, once, and wrote it on a slate in the yard's own mouth, and the slate says that the working day at that mouth is the ebb after the flood and that a man who is going to be in that mouth at the top of a flood is a man who is going to be there when the mouth fills.

The wall of that trench is basalt and old worked stone in courses, and the courses are not coursed, they are laid, and about four feet down on the port side there are the two sockets of a keel cord that carried something out of that mouth once and about six hundred years ago.

He went down at about half past ten in the morning with the sheet of about four feet in an oilcloth fold in the inside pocket of a coat, and Teodor Vask on the surface with his hand on the chain weight and the air limit written on the slate at about a quarter to ten and the reason against it, and the reason was, in his own words, *to see how far the mouth goes and to come back with a number of fathoms and nothing else.*

He was in the water about fifty minutes and on the bottom about twenty-three of them and he came up at about ten to eleven with a cold in his teeth and a taste of iron like a nail and one new thing.

**He did not describe it in the boat. He wrote nothing on the slate and he said nothing on the deck except that he was not going to say what had happened until somebody else had been in the mouth, and that he wanted Amos Trewin, who could not hear out of the left side of his head and had been in that water the day before, to go down next and be asked the same question and to be asked it before anybody else on that boat was told anything at all.**

Amos Trewin went down at about half past eleven and was in the water about forty minutes and came up with the same two things a body's got and with his left side still about nine feet off, and he was asked one question in the boat at about ten past twelve, which was what he heard, and he said the same thing he said on the deck the day before, and then he said a second thing.

"And I could tell you where it came from, sort of. It was ahead and low and to the left, and the ledge you are stood on is not the bottom of the mouth, it is a step, and there is more of it under your feet on the left than there is on the right, and I have been in a lot of holds and I know a step when I am stood on one."

Then Calder Marr said his own account, which did not agree with that, in his own words, and both of them were entered.

**And at about six in the evening on the said day, in that shed two days behind them, in the yard's own mouth, with the hour against it and no name against it, this went into the second column of that leaf: that a man in a bell at about twenty-two fathoms with a sheet of about four feet in an oilcloth fold in the inside pocket of his coat felt a thing in that trench answer the paper, and that it was a pressure and not a sound and that it was there before anything that makes a noise got there, and that a hand in the pattern of the water changed angle across a cut. And that a paper cannot answer, and that whatever answered was answering a description, and that a record of a man holding a paper and feeling something answer is a record of a man holding a paper and is not a record of the thing, and that this does not make the description into a key and does not make what came up out of that bell true, and that the two of the party who were in that mouth are both honest and one of them heard a thing and one of them did not, and that it is written down here and not anywhere else.**

---

**They found the marks at about four o'clock in the afternoon on the said day, in a line, on the ledge to the left of that mouth, and they are square-ended cuts about four inches long and there are eleven of them and no two of them are the same length.**

And on a flat stone at the end of that line, about nine inches from the eleventh cut, there was a piece of worked lead about the size of a man's palm.

**Nine notches were cut into it, and the second, the fifth and the eighth of them were deeper than the other six, and a place about the size of a thumb had been left out of the line on purpose, and there is no figure and no number of any kind on it, and there is a letter at the head of it that is an office's mark and is not a number, and there is a hand the size of a thumbnail.**

The man who kept the leaf did not pick it up for about a minute and then he did not pick it up at all, and he said one sentence out loud on the deck of a boat of about nineteen tons in about twenty-two fathoms of water, and the sentence was this:

"That is a cut. That is a bellwright's cut, that is a line with notches in it, and somebody has left a place out of it on purpose, and I have had that sentence said in a room on a hill about four hundred yards from that chair I am sitting in and I did not understand a word of it until this minute."

Nobody in that boat told him that the piece of lead on that hill had been cut by a woman of about thirty-four off the western reef line who was paid one hundred and sixty-eight pence in coin on the flat of a bench stone for a day's work on the twelfth of October past, and that she said out loud once that that figure is a caulker's figure and not hers.

**A man of about thirty-six came up the ladder at about a quarter past four in the afternoon and put a tool roll on the deck and said his name was Corwin Tace and that he was a setter of marks and that setting marks was what he did.**

"How long have you been on this shelf."

"About three weeks," he said. "I have a warrant. It has a seal on it. It has no figure on it, and I have had it eight months and I have not asked what office it belongs to, because a man who sets marks does not ask what the marks are for, and I have been setting marks for nineteen years and I have never once asked, and there is a word for a man who asks and it is not a good word."

"Are you working for the Crown."

"I am working for whoever pays for the coal," he said. "That is what a warrant is. It is a piece of paper that says somebody has paid for the coal."

Nobody thanked him. Nobody gave him anything. Nobody in that party said one word to him about the lead on that stone, and he did not say one word to them about it, and he put his hand flat on it once, the way a man puts a hand flat on a tool he has not picked up, and then he picked it up and put it inside his coat, and the man who keeps the leaf let him do it and said nothing, and that nothing is in the minute with the hour against it.

**A PAPER IN AN INSIDE POCKET WAS PUT AGAINST A THING AT ABOUT TWENTY-TWO FATHOMS AND SOMETHING ANSWERED IT, AND WHAT ANSWERED WAS ANSWERING A DESCRIPTION, AND A RECORD OF A MAN HOLDING A PAPER IS NOT A RECORD OF THE THING, AND THAT IS IN THE SECOND COLUMN OF A LEAF AT ABOUT SIX IN THE ELEVENING ON THE SAID DAY AND IS IN NO OTHER PLACE. ELEVEN SQUARE-ENDED MARKS IN A LINE ON A LEDGE TO THE LEFT OF THAT MOUTH ARE ABOUT THREE WEEKS OLD AND NO TWO OF THEM ARE THE SAME LENGTH, AND BESIDE THEM ON A FLAT STONE IS A PIECE OF WORKED LEAD CUT IN THE SAME CUT AS A PIECE OF WORKED LEAD ON A HILL FOUR DAYS AND FOUR HUNDRED YARDS FROM THAT BOAT, WITH THE SAME NINE NOTCHES AND THE SAME THREE DEEPER AND THE SAME THUMB-SIZED PLACE LEFT OUT ON PURPOSE AND NO FIGURE AND NO NUMBER OF ANY KIND ON IT, AND A MAN OF ABOUT THIRTY-SIX PUT IT INSIDE HIS COAT AND WAS NOT STOPPED AND WAS NOT THANKED AND WAS ASKED ONE QUESTION ABOUT HIMSELF AND NONE ABOUT THE LEAD, AND NOBODY IN THAT HARBOR HAS BEEN TOLD ANY OF IT YET.**

*First column, entered on Thursday the twenty-first day of October next at about half past nine in the evening in that shed, and entered by the ship's hand in a harbor two days behind the party: that the said day was a rota day and was not a wage Tuesday and was not a wipe, and that the cistern at the back of that market was charged for the ninety-second time and was walked on by none of it; and that three hundred and thirty-six pence came into that yard and three hundred and thirty-six went out of it, and that twenty-eight plus three hundred and thirty-six less three hundred and thirty-six is twenty-eight pence at the close of the said day, being two shillings and fourpence, being short of the wage of four marks by six hundred and twelve pence, being three marks eleven shillings; and that a trench was found at about ten past nine in the morning in about twenty-two fathoms, being about nine feet across at the top of it, and that the water at that mouth comes up about two feet in the twenty minutes after the flood and does not go down again inside the working day, and that the working day at that mouth is the ebb after the flood; and that about four feet down the port side of that mouth there are two sockets of a keel cord; and that eleven square-ended marks about four inches long are set out in a line on a ledge to the left of that mouth, no two of them the same length, about three weeks old; and that on a flat stone about nine inches from the eleventh of them is a piece of worked lead about the size of a man's palm with nine notches cut into it, the second, the fifth and the eighth deeper than the other six, and a place about the size of a thumb left out of it on purpose, and no figure and no number of any kind on it.*

*Second column, not checkable from a mole, entered on the said Thursday at about half past nine in the evening in that shed two days behind the party, and no name is against it: that the reason a man in a bell at about twenty-two fathoms with a sheet of about four feet in an oilcloth fold in his inside pocket felt a thing in that trench answer the paper is entered here at about six in the evening on the said day and is in no other place, and is that it was a pressure and not a sound, and that a paper cannot answer, and that whatever answered was answering a description, and that a record of a man holding a paper and feeling something answer is a record of a man holding a paper and is not a record of the thing, and that this does not make the description into a key and does not make what came up out of that bell true, and that both of the men who were in that mouth are honest and one of them heard a thing and one of them did not; and that the reason the second of those two was put down second is that the man who felt it said he would not describe it until a man who had been in that water the day before and who could not hear out of the left side of his head had been down and been asked the same question, and that a man who keeps the only account of a thing because he was in it first is a man who will end up with a record of himself; and that the reason nobody said one word to a setter of marks of about thirty-six about a piece of lead on a flat stone is that he is a working man with a tool roll and a warrant with a seal on it and no figure on it, and that a warrant is a piece of paper that says somebody has paid for the coal, and that a man who sets marks does not ask what the marks are for, and that asking him would have been the first thing in nine days that anybody in that party did on that water for a reason of its own; and that the reason a woman of about thirty-four off the western reef line is not named in that minute is that nothing in that boat was told about the hill that is not on the face of the thing in that boat, and that a party that tells a stranger about a person in a room a hundred miles away has bought that stranger something; and that the standing list entered in full on the fourteenth day of August past is not entered in full on the said Thursday and stands, and no part of it was paid, reduced, advanced or improved on that day.*
