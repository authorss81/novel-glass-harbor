# Chapter 491

*Tuesday the Twenty-Third Day of November, the Fifty-Seventh Wage Tuesday, the Wage Column Not Moved and Fifty-Seven Weeks Standing Fallen Due at Its Close, Bread and Salt Beef for Nine Men for Ten Days Going onto the Fitting-Out Sheet at About Ten in the Morning at One Thousand Six Hundred Pence, Being Ten Marks, and a Day Apiece in Advance to the Same Four Men at Six Hundred and Seventy-Two Pence, Being Four Marks, Two Shillings and Eightpence, and Nothing Going into That Sheet in Any Hand on Any Line, Three Descents into a Bell on the Air out of That Boat's Own Bottles and Not on Any Line and Not on Any Figure, with a Figure of Forty Minutes on Each Slate and a Named Tender on the Surface and Not in the Bell and a Cutoff He Could Pull Without Asking, a Man of About Thirty-Four of the Second Lay Going Down at About Ten Past Nine and Coming Up at About Ten to Ten with a Hole in the Middle of a Tuesday in Him That He Said Out Loud Once and That Nobody Asked Him About, Nobody Thanked, the Shortfall Said Out Loud at Sixteen Thousand Two Hundred and Forty-Eight Pence, Being One Hundred and One Marks, Seven Shillings and Fourpence, and the Bag at Twenty-Eight Pence, Being Two Shillings and Fourpence, Against a Wage of Six Hundred and Forty*

Tuesday the twenty-third day of November, in about twenty-two fathoms, on the floor of a trench, and on a boat of about nineteen tons that is two days' sail from that harbor.

**The said Tuesday was the fifty-seventh wage Tuesday and was not a rota day and was not a wipe. Bread and salt beef for nine men for ten days went onto the sheet that carries the money at about ten in the morning at one thousand six hundred pence, being ten marks, and a day apiece in advance to the same four men went on at about the same hour at six hundred and seventy-two pence, being four marks, two shillings and eightpence, and nothing went onto that sheet in any hand in any line on that day and nobody was asked for anything. One hundred and sixty-eight pence came into that yard at about ten in the morning, being a day's work with a boat and two men at a berth at the figure in chalk, and one hundred and sixty-eight pence went out of it at about four in the afternoon, being a day asked for by name, and there are twenty-eight pence in the bag at the close of the said day, being two shillings and fourpence, and the figure that bag is short of the wage of four marks is six hundred and twelve pence, being three marks eleven shillings. That sheet stands at twenty-six thousand eight hundred and eighty-eight pence out, being one hundred and sixty-eight marks and eightpence, and ten thousand six hundred and forty pence in, being sixty-six marks, six shillings and eightpence, and it is sixteen thousand two hundred and forty-eight pence short, being one hundred and one marks, seven shillings and fourpence, and that figure is against that yard's own book and is nobody's else. Nothing has been in a tin in that shed since the twenty-ninth day of April past, and not one penny of the whole of that trip came out of that bag, out of the owner of any boat, out of the man with two papers, or out of the Crown.**

**The nine that a stranger in that harbor could have read off any wall are the nine below, and not one of them was found by looking: the notice of the seventeenth of May past, one hundred and eighty-nine days; the bellwright of that harbor, not asked for one hundred and ninety-one; the two objects of nobody's on that crown, five hundred and twenty-nine days; the bench under that pump, bare for the two hundred and ninety-fifth night; the two lights at about a mile off that point, one hundred and thirteen days; the six-line sheet at the back of that end stall, its sixty-eighth day; the paper of four lines in Fish Street, its eighty-third; the nine-line sheet with a seal on it in that front street, its hundredth; and a man of about fifty-one who has said nothing to anybody about a page of six names for a hundred and thirty-one days. The mole-head light in that harbor is out and its lens has been out since the seventh of May, and the figure of four hundred and fifty pence is still on a board at an end stall of that market with February's figure standing visible above it and both dates on that board. A man of about twenty-nine on that boat cannot hear out of the left side of his head, and no figure is set against it anywhere and it is not a motive in anything he does, and it is not going to lift this trip.**

---

**A bell went down onto that floor at about half past eight in the morning, and it is not the chamber with the stove in it.** That chamber stands about ten foot off the last of those fifteen frames on four pads of sawn fir with its stove drawn and its hatch shut, and it was shut from the bank until about eleven o'clock that night, and nobody was in it at any hour of that day. The bell that went down is a plain riveted thing about five foot across and four foot high, open at the bottom, that has been slung under that boat's davit since the first of October and has been down that trench eleven times on that trip, and it goes down to a ring on that floor beside the last of those frames and it is held there by a shackle off its own head.

**The thing it goes down beside is fifteen frames in a row along that floor, and the last of them stands about nine foot past the fourteenth.** Each of them is about the height of a man's own chest and is made of one piece of something that is not wood and has not the grain of wood, and there is a sheet of iron let into the base of each of them about two foot by one foot, and there is a ring standing in nine of the fifteen, and the ring in the eleventh is bright all round the base where something has been set down on it a great many times, and there is silt lying in the top of that brightness, and the man of about thirty-four who said on the sixth day of November that the fittings were about three weeks old has not been asked for that figure again and has not put it up again. There is a mark struck into the corner of the frame about the size of a thumbnail and under it a figure cut the same way, and nobody on that boat has said out loud what either of them is. On the far side of the last of those frames is a bar about the height of a man's own chest and about nine foot long, worn bright along the top for about two foot in the middle and dull at both ends.

---

**A slate was written before anybody was off that deck and it was written on the man who was going down and not by him, and the air limit on it was a figure of forty minutes, and the reason on it was in his own words because he had said the words out loud the evening before and the man who keeps the leaf wrote them down as they came out of him.** Ivo Sark, of about twenty-four, one of the master's two men, was the tender on the surface for the whole of the three with his hand on the chain weight and a cutoff he could pull without asking anybody's leave, and he was not in that bell at any hour of that morning, and the pump hand of about forty-four was on the compressor with him and neither of those two men went down at all.

**The air on those three slates was out of bottles that boat carried off her own coast and filled off her own compressor, and the master of that boat stood on the surface with the tender and named it as his own out loud, and nobody asked him for it a second time and no figure of it is on any sheet in that boat or in that harbor.** The man who keeps the leaf said out loud before the bell was off the chocks that five fresh days had been ordered off that same supply boat and were not aboard yet, and that the twenty-third was worked on what that boat carried, and that he was not going to open a line for an order that was not in his hand.

---

**Ollum Trevask, of about thirty-four, of the second lay, went down at about ten past nine in the morning on that ebb, and he put his hand flat on that bar on the way past without stopping and nobody asked him what it was for.**

He was in the water about ten minutes and in that bell and on that floor about thirty of them, and he came up at about ten to ten, and nobody went into that mouth at the top of a flood and no man went in twice and no two men were in it at once at any hour.

**He came up and he put a hand on the gunwale of that boat and kept it there, and the man who keeps the leaf wrote the hour of the coming up against his name and did not write anything else against it.**

"I will say this once and I am not going to be asked about it," he said. "I went down at ten past nine and I got back at ten to ten and there is a hole in the middle of a Tuesday. Not a day we were on. An ordinary Tuesday in that shed in the spring, and I know the two ends of it and I can put my hand on either one of them and there is about a minute and a half in the middle of it that is not there. It is not a sweet and it is not a sound. It is just not there, and it was there this morning when I got up, because I was going to do a thing at about eleven o'clock on that Tuesday and I know I was going to do it."

Nobody asked him. Nobody was asked for a year and nobody was asked for his book and he did not offer either and nobody looked in his coat, and no figure is set against him anywhere and he is not a casualty and nobody has named him one.

**And the machine took from him, and it is not a thing anybody on that boat has to be persuaded is dangerous.** Ivo Sark, on the surface, said out loud over the wire at about twelve minutes past nine that the man in that bell was not answering, and the man who keeps the leaf wrote that hour against the line, and nobody pulled the cutoff, because a man who has stopped answering has not asked to come up and the whole of the house's own rule is that a man asks.

He began answering again at about thirty-one minutes and he asked to come up, and the tender pulled the cutoff without asking anybody anything, and that bell was up in about nine minutes and he was out of it at about ten to ten.

---

**Sabra Loach, of about thirty-one, who crews nothing and sells nothing, went down at about ten past ten under the same air and the same slate form and the same tender and the same cutoff, and she was in the water about ten minutes and in that bell and on that floor about thirty of them and came up at about twenty to eleven.** Nobody followed her into that bell and nobody stood over her on that deck while she was on that floor.

She said one thing about her own body and not one thing about what was in the room.

"It did not hurt and I want that said first, because I have watched people wait to be told it hurts. It took about two minutes off me and I know the two minutes were mine because I know what my hands were doing when it started. I was holding the edge of the eleventh one. And when I came to, my hands were flat on the iron and my knees were further along than I had put them, and my mouth was full of a taste like a penny that had been in a pipe, and that taste is still here at about eleven o'clock and I have not been asked what I gave it and I am not going to be told."

Nobody asked her. Nobody thanked her. Nobody wrote a figure against her and nobody wrote what she gave it in any column.

---

**And a man of about thirty-seven went down at about ten past eleven under a slate of his own, and he was in the water about ten minutes and in that bell and on that floor about thirty of them, and he came up at about twenty to twelve with a headache and a cold in the back of his neck, and he worked the pump that afternoon and was not thanked.**

He was asked one question at the top of the day and it was the question on the slate, and it was his own, and nobody asked him a second question. He said out loud that he had been in that room four times since the Saturday and had stood at the front of the fifth rack, and that he was not going to say anything about that bar because three men had already said three things about it and the three of them do not agree.

**And at about half past nine in the evening on that deck, in the open, in the yard's own mouth, all four columns including the empty one, the leaf was read back, and it was read twice because about four men of that party cannot read.**

"There are two hundred and twenty-three entries in the first column at this hour and two hundred and twenty-four at the close of the day, and there were one hundred and eighty-three of them at the close of the twelfth day of October past and forty-one have gone in on the forty-one days from Wednesday the thirteenth of October to this day, one against each of them, and not one day in two of them, there being one day in between that is the twelfth day of November and that is a day nobody is in. A day of the first column carries one entry against it whatever else that day printed, so that a stranger with a leaf and a pencil can count it.

The second column has nine hundred and ninety-two lines in it at this hour and nine hundred and ninety-four at the close, and seven hundred and eighty-nine of them were in it at the close of the twelfth and five have gone in on each of those same forty-one days since, three of that day's five before the hour of that day's reading and two of them after it, so that of the two hundred and five that have gone in, two hundred have gone in on the forty days before this one and three went in this morning before this hour, and two hundred and five less two is two hundred and three, and seven hundred and eighty-nine and two hundred and three is nine hundred and ninety-two. That figure is that book of that yard's own and it is not a count of anything anybody can check from a mole. The first column's two hundred and twenty-three and the fourth column's one hundred and twenty-five at this hour are a count of entries and a count of lines, and both of them can be taken with a pencil. **Those are the two definitions and they are said out loud in the same words and in the same order every time this leaf is read back, so that a man with a pencil taking either figure off a page knows which of the two he is holding and at which of the two moments.**

The third column is empty and nobody has been appointed to it, and the hand of a boy of twenty-two is at the head of it with a name beside it that is not that boy's and is not the holder's, and that name was said out loud once in a boat before that yard ever put a column on a page and has not been said since.

And the fourth column has one hundred and twenty-five lines in it at this hour and one hundred and twenty-five at the close, and it has taken no line in these forty-one days and it has taken no line in the ten days before them either, and there is no hundred and twenty-sixth line in it. That is not a closure and it is not a strike-out and nobody has closed that column and nobody in this yard is going to."

---

**And the sheet that carries the money was read back after that, in full, with the day against every line, and it carries no count of persons, and the three who said no stand on it at the same size as the figures, and none of the three has been asked a second time in any of these forty-one days and nobody in that harbor has been asked for the rest of it.**

"Ten thousand six hundred and forty pence in, being sixty-six marks, six shillings and eightpence. Twenty-six thousand eight hundred and eighty-eight pence out, being one hundred and sixty-eight marks and eightpence. **Sixteen thousand two hundred and forty-eight pence short, being one hundred and one marks, seven shillings and fourpence, and that figure is against this yard's own book and is nobody's else, and it is said out loud in this yard's own mouth and it has not been answered by anybody and no figure has been asked of any person in that harbor to make it otherwise.** Two lines went out on that day and nothing went in, being one thousand six hundred and seventy-two pence together, and a stranger with a leaf and a pencil can take all three of those figures off that sheet in about a minute and find that they agree.

And on every day of this trip so far one hundred and sixty-eight pence has come into that yard and one hundred and sixty-eight pence has gone out of it, and the whole of the forty-one days is six thousand eight hundred and eighty-eight pence each way, being forty-three marks and eightpence each way, and twenty-eight plus six thousand eight hundred and eighty-eight less six thousand eight hundred and eighty-eight is twenty-eight pence, being two shillings and fourpence."

Nobody argued with him. Nobody was asked for the rest of it. The four columns at the close of that day stand at two hundred and twenty-four, nine hundred and ninety-four, none and one hundred and twenty-five.

**A BELL WENT DOWN ONTO THAT FLOOR AT ABOUT HALF PAST EIGHT IN THE MORNING AND THREE MEN WENT DOWN IN IT ONE AT A TIME, ON THE AIR OUT OF THAT BOAT'S OWN BOTTLES, UNDER A FIGURE OF FORTY MINUTES ON EACH SLATE, WITH IVO SARK OF ABOUT TWENTY-FOUR ON THE SURFACE AND NOT IN THAT BELL AND A CUTOFF HE PULLED WITHOUT ASKING. THE THING ON THAT FLOOR TOOK FROM EACH OF THEM AND IT IS NOT A THING ANYBODY ON THAT BOAT HAS TO BE PERSUADED IS DANGEROUS. A MAN OF ABOUT THIRTY-FOUR CAME UP AT ABOUT TEN TO TEN WITH A HOLE IN THE MIDDLE OF A TUESDAY IN HIM AND SAID IT ONCE AND WAS NOT ASKED. A WOMAN OF ABOUT THIRTY-ONE CAME UP WITH A TASTE LIKE A PENNY IN A PIPE IN HER MOUTH AND WAS NOT TOLD WHAT SHE HAD GIVEN IT. A MAN OF ABOUT THIRTY-SEVEN CAME UP WITH A HEADACHE AND WORKED THE PUMP. NOBODY WAS THANKED. THAT SHEET IS SIXTEEN THOUSAND TWO HUNDRED AND FORTY-EIGHT PENCE SHORT AT THE CLOSE OF THIS DAY, BEING ONE HUNDRED AND ONE MARKS, SEVEN SHILLINGS AND FOURPENCE, AND NO FIGURE HAS BEEN ASKED OF ANYBODY IN THAT HARBOR TO MAKE IT OTHERWISE.**

*First column, entered on Tuesday the twenty-third day of November at about half past nine in the evening on that deck: that the said day was the fifty-seventh wage Tuesday and was not a rota day and was not a wipe, and that in that harbor the wage column stood at twenty-six lines and one hundred and four marks with twenty envelopes in a chest at ten in the morning and at four in the afternoon, and that the column did not move, and that fifty-seven weeks stand fallen due at its close, and that a wage Tuesday is the day a harbor finds out what it owes and the finding out is not a payment; and that bread and salt beef for nine men for ten days at one thousand six hundred pence, being ten marks, and a day apiece in advance to the same four men at six hundred and seventy-two pence, being four marks, two shillings and eightpence, went onto the sheet that carries the money at about ten in the morning, being two thousand two hundred and seventy-two pence together, and that nothing went onto that sheet in any hand in any line on that day and nobody was asked for anything; and that one hundred and sixty-eight pence came into that yard and one hundred and sixty-eight went out of it, and that twenty-eight plus one hundred and sixty-eight less one hundred and sixty-eight is twenty-eight pence at the close of the said day, being two shillings and fourpence, being short of the wage of four marks by six hundred and twelve pence, being three marks eleven shillings; and that the chamber with a stove in it stood about ten foot off the last of the fifteen frames on four pads of sawn fir with its stove drawn and its hatch shut from the bank until about eleven o'clock at night, and that nobody was in it at any hour of that day; and that a man of about twenty-four, one of the master's two men, was the tender on the surface for the whole of the three with his hand on the chain weight and a cutoff he could pull without asking anybody's leave, and that he was not in that bell at any hour of that morning, and that the pump hand of about forty-four was on the compressor with him and neither of those two men went down at all; and that a man of about thirty-four of the second lay went down at about ten past nine in the morning on that ebb, and put his hand flat on a bar about the height of his own chest and about nine foot long on the far side of the last of those frames without stopping, and that nobody asked him what that bar was for; and that he was in the water about ten minutes and in that bell and on that floor about thirty of them and came up at about ten to ten, and that the tender said out loud over the wire at about twelve minutes past nine that the man in that bell was not answering, and that nobody pulled the cutoff, and that he began answering again at about thirty-one minutes and asked to come up and the tender pulled the cutoff without asking anybody anything and that bell was up in about nine minutes; and that he said out loud once on that deck that there is a hole in the middle of an ordinary Tuesday in a shed in the spring of about a minute and a half, and that he knows the two ends of it and can put his hand on either one, and that nobody asked him and nobody was asked for a year and nobody was asked for his book and he did not offer either and no figure is set against him anywhere and he is not a casualty and nobody has named him one; and that a woman of about thirty-one who crews nothing and sells nothing went down at about ten past ten under the same air and the same tender and the same cutoff and was in the water about ten minutes and in that bell and on that floor about thirty of them and came up at about twenty to eleven, and that nobody stood nine feet off her on that deck while she was on that floor, and that she said out loud that it did not hurt and that it took about two minutes off her and that she was holding the edge of the eleventh one when it started and that her hands were flat on the iron and her knees further along when she came to, and that she had a taste like a penny that had been in a pipe and that she was not going to be told what she gave it; and that a man of about thirty-seven went down at about ten past eleven under a slate of his own and was in the water about ten minutes and in that bell and on that floor about thirty of them and came up at about twenty to twelve with a headache and a cold in the back of his neck, and that he worked the pump that afternoon and was not thanked, and that he said out loud that he had been in that room four times since the Saturday and had stood at the front of the fifth rack and that he was not going to say anything about that bar because three men had already said three things about it and the three of them do not agree; *Second column, not checkable from a mole, entered on the said Tuesday at about half past nine in the evening on that deck, and no name is against it: that the reason the air of the twenty-third was out of that boat's own bottles and not off the hired line is the man who keeps the leaf's own, and is that a line is a figure a stranger with a pencil can take off a page, and that an order that is five days off and not in his hand is not a figure, and that a yard which opens a line for air it has not got spends a week's work on a boat's honour; and that the reason nobody pulled the cutoff at twelve minutes past nine on that first descent of the day is the yard's own mouth, and is that a man who has stopped answering has not asked for anything and that the house's whole of this is a man asking, and that a cutoff pulled at a man who has not asked is a decision taken away from the man who is in it; and that the reason a man of about thirty-four was let say a hole in the middle of a Tuesday once and was not asked a second question about it is his own, and is that the second question is the one that makes it a hole instead of a Tuesday, and that a man who has been asked twice about a missing minute will begin to look for it in places it is not; and that the reason a woman of about thirty-one was not told what she gave that floor is the yard's own mouth, and is that a woman standing on a deck with the taste of a penny in her mouth has been made the owner of a thing nobody can price, and that this yard does not put a name on a loss and call it a description; and that the reason the three of them were put down one at a time on the first morning of that movement is the yard's own mouth, and is that the twenty-third is the first of three days in which a thing on that floor is used instead of being argued about, and that a rule of one at a time is only worth anything on a morning when the man in the bell would be in the way of the next one; and that the reason the proof of that fit is in the leaf and not on the sheet that carries the money is in the yard's own mouth, and is that a bell going down at half past eight in the morning is a cost and a cost belongs on the second of those two sheets and not on the first, and that the two sheets are named separately here on purpose and are not to be put in one column, one room or one mouth without both being named.*
