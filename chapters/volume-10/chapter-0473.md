# Chapter 473

*Thursday the Fourth Day of November, a Rota Day, and the Ninety-Fifth Time That Cistern at the Back of That Market Was Charged at About Eight in the Morning and Walked on by None of It, the Top Name on That Rota Board Still off It, a Slate Written Before Anybody Went Down With a Figure of Forty Minutes of Air and a Named Tender on the Surface and Not in the Bell and a Cutoff That Tender Could Pull Without Asking, a Man of About Twenty-Nine Going Down on the Ebb and Past the Last Row of Racks to a Place Where the Floor Was Not Swept and Coming Up at About Ten to Eleven With His Hands Shaking for About Two Hours and No Figure Set Against That and Nothing Written, a Rectangle of Worked Iron About Nine Feet Long and Four Wide Bar and Not Plate With Three Rivets in It and Two Inches of Silt Over It Banked Along One Long Side, Uprights Going Down Into That Floor About Two Feet and a Frame Under Nine Short Lengths of New Metal, and a Figure Cut Beside a Builder's Mark That Is Six Hundred and Eleven and Is Not a Year and Is Not a Curse, a Man of About Thirty-Four Saying Out Loud That a Scar Does Not Have Square Ends and That He Has Been in Holds for Nineteen Years and Has Never Once Been Wrong About How Long a Cut Has Been Under and Giving Two Figures and Not One, Being a Very Old Cut and a Cut of About Three Weeks, and Nobody Arguing With Him About Either, the Third Column Empty and the Fourth Standing at One Hundred and Twenty-Five Where It Stood, the Shortfall Unmoved at Four Thousand Seven Hundred and Twenty-Two Pence, Being Twenty-Nine Marks, Six Shillings and Tenpence, and the Bag at Twenty-Eight Pence, Being Two Shillings and Fourpence, Against a Wage of Six Hundred and Forty*

Thursday the fourth day of November, in about twenty-two fathoms, on the floor of a trench, and on a boat of about nineteen tons that is two days' sail from that harbor.

**The said Thursday was a rota day and was not a wage Tuesday and was not a wipe. The cistern at the back of that market was charged for the ninety-fifth time at about eight in the morning and was walked on by none of it, and the top name on that rota board is still off it. One hundred and sixty-eight pence came into that yard at about ten in the morning, being a day's work with a boat and two men at a berth at the figure in chalk, and one hundred and sixty-eight pence went out of it at about four in the afternoon, being a day asked for by name, and there are twenty-eight pence in the bag at the close of the said day, being two shillings and fourpence, and the figure that bag is short of the wage of four marks is six hundred and twelve pence, being three marks eleven shillings. Nothing has been in a tin in that shed since the twenty-eighth day of April past, and not one penny of the whole of that trip came out of that bag, out of the owner of any boat in that harbor, or out of the Crown.**

**And the far bench was occupied from ten until four in that harbor and was not approached by anybody. Mara Venn was on that deck and going nowhere, of no office, not on the crew, not restored, not forgiven, forty marks first after food, neither reduced nor paid, and the leaf was under her own coat. Tobias Wren was on the crown of that mole from ten until four, being sixty-one. The notice of the seventeenth of May past is on its wall in a different street for one hundred and seventy-one days. The bellwright of that harbor has not been asked for one hundred and seventy-three days, the chest is shut, and the eight marks and a day of a boat have not been argued. The three nots standing against the eighth of June past are a seventeenth week and nobody in that crew has said one word about them. The hand of a boy of twenty-two is at the head of the third column and that boy's surname is on no page of this harbor. The two objects of nobody's on that crown are five hundred and eleven days old. The bench under that pump is bare for the two hundred and seventy-seventh night. Two lights at about a mile off that point have not moved in ninety-five days. A man of about fifty-one in that harbor has said nothing to anybody about a page of six names for a hundred and thirteen days. The sheet of six lines at the back of that end stall is on that wall for the fiftieth day, the paper of four lines in Fish Street is on that door for the sixty-fifth day, and the nine-line sheet with a seal on it is in that front street for the eighty-second day. A man of about twenty-nine on that boat cannot hear out of the left side of his head and it is not a motive in anything he does and no figure is set against it anywhere.**

---

**The party had been working the swept part of that floor since the Sunday, and there are forty racks in rows on the swept part, and the man who keeps the leaf wrote on the Monday that a later phase might not strike one of them off that page and nobody in that party has any intention of doing so.**

On the Thursday they went past the last row.

The floor changes about nine feet beyond it and nobody marked where it changes, because marking it would have meant putting a thing on that floor that was not already there, and at about two hundred and forty yards out from the trench the party had stopped putting things on floors. Past the last row the silt is not swept and the hand of a board has never been on it, and it lies flat and unbroken for about nine feet and then it stops.

Amos Trewin, of about twenty-nine, went down at about ten in the morning on that ebb under a slate with his reason in his own words and the hour against it, being that the floor past the last row had not been looked at and that a thing a man has heard once in one place is not a thing he has heard twice, and there was a figure of forty minutes of air on that slate and a named tender on the surface of that boat and not in the bell with his hand on the chain weight and a cutoff he could pull without asking anybody's leave.

He was in the water about fifty minutes and on the bottom about thirty-four of them and came up at about ten to eleven, and he came up with his hands shaking and they went on shaking for about two hours, and he was not asked to write anything in that time and wrote nothing, and nobody thanked him and no figure is set against it anywhere.

---

**What is past the last row is a frame, and the frame is a great deal older than what is standing on it, and both of those facts are in the same place on the same afternoon.**

The frame itself is about nine feet long and about four wide, and it is made of bar and not of rolled metal, and its corners are cut and not welded, and it is riveted in three places, and the silt lies over it about two inches deep and is banked along the north long side and not along the south one.

Standing on that frame are nine short lengths of something newer, each of them about the width of two fingers and about a foot and a half long, each of them set into a hole in the frame with a collar and a nut that are also newer than the bar, and each of the nine has been in that hole long enough to have put a bright ring around its base and not long enough to have put anything else there at all.

**Nobody in that bell put a hand on any of the nine.**

---

**Ollum Trevask went down onto that floor again at about two in the afternoon under a slate of his own with his reason in his own words and the hour against it, being that a thing which is under a fitting cannot be read while the fitting is on it and the fitting is going nowhere, and the air limit was the same figure of forty minutes and the tender was the same man, on the surface and not in the bell, and the cutoff was the same one he could pull without asking. He was in the water about forty-five minutes and on the bottom about thirty-one of them and came up at about a quarter to three. He looked at the frame and at the nine with a slate held out over it and gave two figures and not one, and nobody in that party argued with him about either of them.**

"A scar does not have square ends," he said. "I have been in holds nineteen years and I have never once been wrong about how long a cut has been under. That bar there has been under a great deal longer than a man's life and I am not going to put a number of centuries on it off a slate in twenty-two fathoms, and I will tell you why, and it is not modesty. It is that a man who has been in holds nineteen years knows what a hundred years looks like and he does not know what six hundred looks like, and nobody on this boat does, and the number that would go in that column this afternoon would be a guess wearing a coat."

"And the nine?" the man of about fifty-one asked.

"About three weeks." He did not look up from the slate. "Three weeks and not three days, because the bright ring round the base of one of them has silt in the top of it and silt does not lie on a thing that has been there since Sunday. Three weeks is what I would say and I would say it again in the same words on the Friday and I will say it again on the Saturday, and it does not matter to me in the least whether anybody believes me, because you can put a finger on that collar and find the same answer I found and I have not asked anybody to."

---

**There is a mark on that frame, and it is a builder's mark, and there is a figure cut beside it, and the figure is six hundred and eleven.**

The mark is a hammer and a square crossed with a chisel, cut with a cold chisel and not stamped and not pressed, and it is about two inches across, and it is cut on the flat of the bar where it would be seen by a man standing on the frame.

Beside it is a figure of six hundred and eleven cut to the same depth with the same chisel, and beside the figure is a word that is not a name and is the word people cut when they were counting, and **no man in that boat can turn six hundred and eleven into a year, because there is no year cut on that frame to turn it into and there is no name cut beside it either.**

The man of about fifty-one out of the coast town four days down said the one thing he said about the mark, and it went into the second column in the yard's own mouth with the hour against it.

"That is a builder's mark and not a seal. A seal goes on a thing to say somebody has taken responsibility for that thing and it is pressed and it has a rim and a die in it and it is round or it is square and it is pressed. That has been cut with a chisel by a man who was not standing at a desk. I have seen a hundred and forty of them on the heads of gates and lintels and end rails in houses four days up this coast and not one of them was ever on a thing that anybody had taken out of the water and claimed."

Mara Venn wrote down two things that day and both of them were in her own mouth and one of them was about the figure and not about the mark.

"That is a date," she said. "It is a figure and a cut and it is the day somebody stood here and finished a thing, and it is a date and it is not a curse and it is not a warning, and I am going to write it down as the first thing I have put in a column in a long while that I am writing down because I know what it is and not because I know what it means."

---

**And at about half past four in the afternoon on the said Thursday a pump hand of about forty-four with nineteen years of compressors, who is in nobody's crew and is a member of nothing, was asked by the man who keeps the leaf what a thing of that shape was for, and he answered it in the working words of his own trade, and gave the yard a word for it, and the word is in that one mouth once.**

He did not say it twice and nobody asked him to and it was not written on any page of that yard's own that day.

"There is no horn on that boat and there is no horn in this harbor and there is not one man in nine who could tell you what it is called," he said. "I will give you the word because you will get it out of somebody in a month anyway and I would rather you had it from a man who has held one. It is chorus. It is not a church and it is not singing and it is not anybody's name. It is the name of a thing that was built to be worked by more than one person at a time so that one man's account can be set against another's while it is being given, and not after. I have never been in one. I have been in three compressor houses and a fish hall and a shed with a stove in it, and I am telling you the word and nothing else, and do not write it down as mine."

---

**A FRAME OF BAR AND NOT OF PLATE STANDS PAST THE LAST ROW OF RACKS WITH NINE SHORT LENGTHS OF NEWER METAL SET INTO HOLES IN IT, AND THE NINE HAVE BEEN IN THOSE HOLES ABOUT THREE WEEKS AND THE BAR HAS BEEN UNDER A GREAT DEAL LONGER THAN ANY MAN'S LIFE. A FIGURE OF SIX HUNDRED AND ELEVEN IS CUT BESIDE A BUILDER'S MARK THAT IS A HAMMER AND A SQUARE AND A CHISEL AND NOT A SEAL, AND IT IS NOT A YEAR BECAUSE THERE IS NO YEAR CUT ON THAT FRAME AND NO NAME BESIDE IT, AND IT IS A DATE AND NOT A CURSE. A MAN OF ABOUT THIRTY-FOUR GAVE TWO FIGURES AND NOT ONE AND WAS NOT ARGUED WITH ABOUT EITHER, AND THE COUNT OF SIX HUNDRED AND ELEVEN IS IN ONE COLUMN OF ONE LEAF IN THE YARD'S OWN MOUTH AND ON NO PAGE OF THAT HARBOR. NOTHING WENT ONTO THAT SHEET ON THIS DAY AND IT IS FOUR THOUSAND SEVEN HUNDRED AND TWENTY-TWO PENCE SHORT, BEING TWENTY-NINE MARKS, SIX SHILLINGS AND TENPENCE, AND NO FIGURE HAS BEEN ASKED OF ANYBODY TO MAKE IT OTHERWISE.**

*First column, entered on Thursday the fourth day of November next at about half past nine in the evening on that deck: that the said day was a rota day and was not a wage Tuesday and was not a wipe, and that the cistern at the back of that market was charged for the ninety-fifth time at about eight in the morning and was walked on by none of it, and that the top name on that rota board is still off it; and that one hundred and sixty-eight pence came into that yard and one hundred and sixty-eight went out of it, and that twenty-eight plus one hundred and sixty-eight less one hundred and sixty-eight is twenty-eight pence at the close of the said day, being two shillings and fourpence, being short of the wage of four marks by six hundred and twelve pence, being three marks eleven shillings; and that the party had been working the swept part of that floor since the Sunday, and that there are forty racks in rows on the swept part and none of them was struck off any page that day; and that the floor changes about nine feet past the last row, and that the silt past it is unswept and lies flat and unbroken for about nine feet and then stops; and that a man of about twenty-nine went down at about ten in the morning on that ebb in about twenty-two fathoms under a slate with his reason in his own words and the hour against it, with an air limit of forty minutes written on that slate and a named tender on the surface and not in the bell and a cutoff that tender could pull without asking, and that he was in the water about fifty minutes and on the bottom about thirty-four of them and came up at about ten to eleven; and that he came up with his hands shaking and they went on shaking for about two hours, and that he was not asked to write anything in that time and wrote nothing, and that no figure is set against it; and that past the last row there is a frame about nine feet long and about four wide made of bar and not of rolled metal, its corners cut and not welded, riveted in three places, with about two inches of silt over it banked along the north long side and not along the south one; and that standing on that frame are nine short lengths of newer metal, each set into a hole with a collar and a nut, and that nobody in that bell put a hand on any of the nine; and that the same man went down onto that floor again at about two in the afternoon under a slate of his own with the reason in his own words and the hour against it, with the same figure of forty minutes of air and the same named tender and the same cutoff, that he was in the water about forty-five minutes and on the bottom about thirty-one of them and came up at about a quarter to three, and gave two figures and not one, being a very old cut and a cut of about three weeks, and that he said the second of them twice in the same words on the Thursday and that nobody in that party argued with him about either; and that there is a figure of six hundred and eleven cut beside a mark that is a hammer and a square crossed with a chisel, cut with a cold chisel and not stamped and not pressed, and that there is no year cut on that frame and no name beside it; and that nothing went onto the fitting-out sheet on that day and that it stands at ten thousand six hundred and forty pence in and fifteen thousand three hundred and sixty-two pence out and is four thousand seven hundred and twenty-two pence short, being twenty-nine marks, six shillings and tenpence.*

*Second column, not checkable from a mole, entered on the said Thursday at about half past nine in the evening on that deck, and no name is against it: that the reason nobody marked where the swept floor changed into the unswept one is in the yard's own mouth, and is that a mark on a floor is a thing put where there was nothing, and that at about two hundred and forty yards out from that trench this party had stopped putting things on floors, and that a man can carry a floor away in his head and a mark stays; and that the reason a man of about thirty-four would not put a number of centuries on that bar is his own and he gave it out loud, and is that a man who has been in holds nineteen years knows what a hundred years looks like and does not know what six hundred looks like, and that a guess wearing a coat is worse than no figure because it gets copied; and that the reason he gave three weeks and not three days is that there is silt lying in the top of the bright ring round the base of one of the nine, and that a man who has not been told a thing will not improve it; and that the reason nobody in that bell put a hand on any of the nine is the yard's own mouth, and is that the whole of what this party came for is that a hand is a thing that makes a decision, and that a decision taken in twenty-two fathoms about nine pieces of metal with collars on them is a decision that has to be paid for out of somebody's own hands and there is not enough on that sheet to pay for it yet; and that the reason a pump hand of about forty-four was asked one question and gave the yard a word for what a thing of that shape was for is his own, and is that he has held one of them and has been in three compressor houses and a fish hall and a shed with a stove in it, and that he gave the word once in one mouth and would not say it twice and would not have it written as his; and that the standing list entered in full on the fourteenth day of August past is not entered in full on the said Thursday and stands, and no part of it was paid, reduced, advanced or improved on that day.*
