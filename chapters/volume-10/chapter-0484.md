# Chapter 484

*Tuesday the Sixteenth Day of November, the Fifty-Sixth Wage Tuesday, a Day on Which No Charge Was Placed on That Cistern and on Which the Wall Behind a Reeve's Bench Did Not Come Down at All, Four Days of Coal for That Pump off the Same Supply Boat Going onto the Sheet That Carries the Money at About Ten in the Morning at Two Thousand Three Hundred and Fifty-Two Pence, Being Fourteen Marks, Nine Shillings and Fourpence, and Nothing Going into That Sheet in Any Hand, a Man of About Fifty-Four Out of a Supply Boat off That Reef Saying One Thing Out Loud for the Third Time This Trip and Saying It in the Same Words and This Time Saying That Coal off That Boat Goes Up at the End of the Month, One of the Four Men Paid a Day Apiece Asking Out Loud What a Man Is Owed if the Boat Goes Home Early and Being Told the Same Answer He Was Given Twice Before and Thanking Him Again and Being Told Again That He Is Not to Be Thanked for It, the Wage Column in That Harbor Not Moving and Fifty-Six Weeks Standing Fallen Due at the Close of That Day, the Shortfall at Eleven Thousand Six Hundred and Thirty-Eight Pence, Being Seventy-Two Marks, Nine Shillings and Tenpence, and the Bag at Twenty-Eight Pence, Being Two Shillings and Fourpence, Against a Wage of Six Hundred and Forty*

Tuesday the sixteenth day of November, in about twenty-two fathoms, on the floor of a trench, and on a boat of about nineteen tons that is two days' sail from that harbor.

**The said Tuesday was a wage Tuesday and was not a rota day and was not a wipe, and no charge was placed on that cistern on it and the wall behind a reeve's bench in that harbor did not come down on it at all. Four days of coal for that pump came off the same supply boat and went onto the sheet that carries the money at about ten in the morning at two thousand three hundred and fifty-two pence, being fourteen marks, nine shillings and fourpence, and that is the same figure as the last two loads and nothing went into that sheet in any hand. One hundred and sixty-eight pence came into that yard at about ten in the morning, being a day's work with a boat and two men at a berth at the figure in chalk, and one hundred and sixty-eight pence went out of it at about four in the afternoon, being a day asked for by name, and there are twenty-eight pence in the bag at the close of the said day, being two shillings and fourpence, and the figure that bag is short of the wage of four marks is six hundred and twelve pence, being three marks eleven shillings. That sheet stands at twenty-two thousand two hundred and seventy-eight pence out, being one hundred and thirty-nine marks, three shillings and twopence, and ten thousand six hundred and forty pence in, being sixty-six marks, six shillings and eightpence, and it is eleven thousand six hundred and thirty-eight pence short, being seventy-two marks, nine shillings and tenpence. Nothing has been in a tin in that shed since the twenty-ninth day of April past, and not one penny of the whole of that trip came out of that bag, out of the owner of any boat, or out of the Crown.**

**The nine that a stranger in that harbor could have read off any wall are the nine below, and not one of them was found by looking: the notice of the seventeenth of May past, one hundred and eighty-two days; the bellwright of that harbor, not asked for one hundred and eighty-four; the two objects of nobody's on that crown, five hundred and twenty-two days; the bench under that pump, bare for the two hundred and eighty-eighth night; the two lights at about a mile off that point, one hundred and six days; the six-line sheet at the back of that end stall, its sixty-first day; the paper of four lines in Fish Street, its seventy-sixth; the nine-line sheet with a seal on it in that front street, its ninety-third; and a man of about fifty-one who has said nothing to anybody about a page of six names for a hundred and twenty-four days. The north berth in that harbor is out and the hose there is dead until the spring, and the tide gang is four men short, and a company of seven boats and about thirty men on that coast is worth about a hundred and five minutes of ebb in twenty-four hours and about two hours of usable in it, and no chapter of that trip improved any of those three things.**

**And the wage column in that harbor stood where it stood at ten o'clock that morning and stood where it stood at four in the afternoon. It is at twenty-six lines and one hundred and four marks, and there are twenty envelopes in a chest, and the fifty-sixth wage Tuesday came and went at the close of that day and the column did not move, and fifty-six weeks stand fallen due. That is the whole of what a wage Tuesday is.**

---

**The supply boat came off the reef at about half past eight in the morning with four days of coal in her and put it over the side in a hand-basket, and one man in her, of about fifty-four, who has run that run for nineteen years, put the last of it down and said the thing he says.**

"Coal off this boat goes up at the end of the month."

He said it standing on that deck with a basket in his hand and he said it in the same words, and he has said it on this trip twice before, once off the reef on the third of November and once on the seventh, and both of those times it was the same sentence with the same figures in it, and nobody on that deck argued with him then and nobody argued with him now.

**And the man who keeps the leaf did not open a line for it, and said out loud that he was not going to.**

"There is no line opened for the end of the month and there is not going to be one and that figure is not going onto any sheet in this boat. It is a thing a man in a supply boat said standing on a deck with a basket in his hand and it is true and it is not ours and I am not writing it down and I am not paying for it. The four days of coal are on the sheet at the figure they are at and the figure is the same as the last two loads and the last two loads are on the sheet at the same figure, and a stranger with a pencil can take all three off that sheet in about a minute and find that they agree."

---

**At about half past ten in the morning one of the four men who is paid a day apiece in advance asked the master of that boat a question in front of the whole of that party, and it was a question he had clearly been holding since the Thursday of the eleventh.**

"What is a man owed if the boat goes home early?"

**And the master gave him the answer he had given him twice before, in the same words, and it was that the day in advance is a day in advance and is not taken back, and that it is not a promise of a day after it, and that his boat is not this party and the man was never in it, and that he cannot be asked to stay by anybody on that deck and is not going to be asked.**

"Thank you," said Ruben Skay, of about thirty-eight, and he said it out loud in front of the rest of them, as he had said it twice before.

"You are not to be thanked for it," said the master. "I told you that the second time and I am telling you the third time for the same reason. A man who is thanked for an answer has been thanked for an answer and the answer is what I owe you, and it is not a favour and it does not get better because I said it kindly. **I said on Thursday what I am going to do with my own people and I am not going to say it a fourth time this week and nobody on this deck is going to ask me for it.**"

Nobody asked him. Nobody thanked him. Nobody put a date on it and nobody wrote one down.

---

**A slate was written before anybody went down on that Tuesday and the man of about thirty-seven wrote his own reason on it and the reason was that the long rack down the side of that room has four shelves and the other four racks have three, and that a rack with four shelves in it is a different thing from a rack with three and that he wanted to stand in front of it and not go past it.**

He went down at about ten o'clock in the morning on that ebb under that slate, and the air limit on it was a figure of forty minutes, and the air was out of bottles that boat carried off her own coast and filled off her own compressor, and the tender was named and was on the surface and not in the bell with his hand on the chain weight and a cutoff he could pull without asking anybody's leave.

He was in the water about fifty minutes and on the bottom about thirty-four of them and came up at about ten to eleven. **He came up and said that the long rack has four shelves and the four against the back wall have three each, and that the fourth shelf of the long rack is about a foot lower than the other three, and that he did not go into it past the second shelf and nobody asked him to and he said he was not going to on his own account and not because he was told.**

Nobody thanked him and no figure is set against him anywhere. Nobody went into that mouth at the top of a flood on that day and nobody went in twice.

---

**At about half past five in the afternoon, in the yard's own mouth, the position was said out loud, and a wage Tuesday was said out loud with it, and neither of the two was answered.**

"The sheet that carries the money is eleven thousand six hundred and thirty-eight pence short, being seventy-two marks, nine shillings and tenpence, and it is against this yard's own book and not against anybody's, and no figure has been asked of any person in that harbor to make it otherwise.

And the wage column in that harbor is at twenty-six lines and one hundred and four marks with twenty envelopes in a chest, and the fifty-sixth wage Tuesday came at the close of this day and the column did not move, and fifty-six weeks stand fallen due, and that figure is fifty-six and it is one more than it was seven days ago and it will be one more than it is next Tuesday. **A wage Tuesday does not pay a wage. A wage Tuesday is the day a harbor finds out what it owes, and this harbor has found out, and the finding out is not a payment and nobody in that harbor is going to be told that it is one.**"

---

**FOUR DAYS OF COAL FOR THAT PUMP WENT ONTO THE SHEET THAT CARRIES THE MONEY AT ABOUT TEN IN THE MORNING ON THAT TUESDAY AT TWO THOUSAND THREE HUNDRED AND FIFTY-TWO PENCE, BEING FOURTEEN MARKS, NINE SHILLINGS AND FOURPENCE, AT THE SAME FIGURE AS THE LAST TWO LOADS. A MAN OF ABOUT FIFTY-FOUR OUT OF A SUPPLY BOAT OFF THAT REEF SAID OUT LOUD FOR THE THIRD TIME THIS TRIP THAT COAL OFF THAT BOAT GOES UP AT THE END OF THE MONTH AND NO LINE WAS OPENED FOR IT. ONE OF THE FOUR MEN PAID A DAY APIECE ASKED WHAT A MAN IS OWED IF THE BOAT GOES HOME EARLY AND WAS TOLD THE ANSWER HE HAD BEEN GIVEN TWICE BEFORE AND THANKED HIM AGAIN AND WAS TOLD AGAIN THAT HE WAS NOT TO BE THANKED FOR IT. THE FIFTY-SIXTH WAGE TUESDAY CAME AND WENT AND THE COLUMN DID NOT MOVE AND FIFTY-SIX WEEKS STAND FALLEN DUE. AND THAT SHEET IS ELEVEN THOUSAND SIX HUNDRED AND THIRTY-EIGHT PENCE SHORT AT THE CLOSE OF THIS DAY, BEING SEVENTY-TWO MARKS, NINE SHILLINGS AND TENPENCE, AND NO FIGURE HAS BEEN ASKED OF ANYBODY IN THAT HARBOR TO MAKE IT OTHERWISE.**

*First column, entered on Tuesday the sixteenth day of November at about half past nine in the evening on that deck: that the said day was a wage Tuesday and was not a rota day and was not a wipe, and that no charge was placed on that cistern on it and that the wall behind a reeve's bench in that harbor did not come down on it at all; and that four days of coal for that pump came off the same supply boat and went onto the sheet that carries the money at about ten in the morning at two thousand three hundred and fifty-two pence, being fourteen marks, nine shillings and fourpence, at the same figure as the last two loads, and that nothing went into that sheet in any hand; and that one hundred and sixty-eight pence came into that yard and one hundred and sixty-eight went out of it, and that twenty-eight plus one hundred and sixty-eight less one hundred and sixty-eight is twenty-eight pence at the close of the said day, being two shillings and fourpence, being short of the wage of four marks by six hundred and twelve pence, being three marks eleven shillings; and that the running out on that sheet moved from nineteen thousand nine hundred and twenty-six pence to twenty-two thousand two hundred and seventy-eight pence, being one hundred and thirty-nine marks, three shillings and twopence; and that a man of about fifty-four out of a supply boat off that reef said out loud, standing on that deck with a basket in his hand, that coal off that boat goes up at the end of the month, in the same words, for the third time this trip, having said it off the reef on the third day of November and again on the seventh, and that nobody argued with him then and nobody argued with him on that day; and that the man who keeps the leaf said out loud that no line was opened for the end of the month and none is going to be and that the figure a man in a supply boat said on a deck is not on any sheet in that boat, and that the four days of coal are on the sheet at the figure they are at and that figure is the same as the last two loads; and that one of the four men paid a day apiece, of about thirty-eight, asked the master of that boat at about half past ten in the morning what a man is owed if the boat goes home early, in front of the whole of that party, and that the master gave him the answer he had given him twice before in the same words, being that the day in advance is a day in advance and is not taken back, and that it is not a promise of a day after it, and that his boat is not this party and that man was never in it, and that he cannot be asked to stay by anybody on that deck and is not going to be asked; and that the man thanked him out loud as he had thanked him twice before and the master told him again that he was not to be thanked for it, that a man thanked for an answer has been thanked for an answer, and that the answer is what he owes and is not a favour; and that the master said that he said on the eleventh what he is going to do with his own people and is not going to say it a fourth time that week, and that nobody asked him and nobody thanked him and no date was put on it and none was written down; and that a man of about thirty-seven went down at about ten o'clock in the morning on that ebb under a slate with his reason in his own words and the hour against it, being that the long rack down the side of that room has four shelves and the four against the back wall have three each, with an air limit of forty minutes written on that slate and the air out of bottles that boat carried off her own coast, and a named tender on the surface and not in the bell and a cutoff that tender could pull without asking, and that he was in the water about fifty minutes and on the bottom about thirty-four of them and came up at about ten to eleven; and that the wage column in that harbor stood at twenty-six lines and one hundred and four marks with twenty envelopes in a chest at ten o'clock that morning and at four in the afternoon, and that the fifty-sixth wage Tuesday came and went at the close of that day and the column did not move and fifty-six weeks stand fallen due, and that a wage Tuesday does not pay a wage and is the day a harbor finds out what it owes; and that nobody thanked anybody on that day.*

*Second column, not checkable from a mole, entered on the said Tuesday at about half past nine in the evening on that deck, and no name is against it: that the reason a figure a man in a supply boat said out loud on a deck was not opened as a line on that sheet is the man who keeps the leaf's own, and is that a figure which has not been asked for by anybody has not been priced by anybody, and that a line on that sheet is a line somebody has asked for and somebody else has agreed to pay, and that the end of the month is not a thing anybody on that boat has agreed to pay for; and that the reason that man said the same three sentences for the third time in nine days is his own, and is that he has been saying the same thing on that run for nineteen years and that nobody ever acted on it in the middle and that a man who says a thing once and then stops saying it has been talked out of it, and that a number can be said twice to show that it has not moved and a thing that is not a number is said once, and that he is a working man and not a page; and that the reason the master of that boat gave the same answer the third time and would not take a word of thanks for it is his own, and is that a man who is thanked for an answer has been given a thing on top of the thing he was owed, and that the two words a man says for a thing he has been given are not a wage and are not a line on a sheet, and that the whole of what he owes a man on a day is the answer about that day, and that the declaration he made in front of all nine of them stands exactly where he put it and this yard has not touched it since and is not going to; and that the reason a man of about thirty-eight had been holding that question since the Thursday of the eleventh is the yard's own mouth, and is that a man paid a day apiece is not asked what he thinks about it and is allowed to think about it, and that a man who asks a question out loud in front of nine people on a deck has done the only thing a deck is for; and that the reason the long rack was left past the second shelf on that man's own account is his own, and is that a party which goes into a room on what one of its own found on the Saturday past and does not come out of it again in front of anybody is a party that will be known for it, and that a man of about thirty-seven counted four racks off the front of the fifth on the fourteenth and did not go into the long one past its second shelf on the sixteenth and nobody asked him to do either and he has not been asked to give a figure for either; and that the reason no standing figure in that harbor moved on the said Tuesday is the yard's own mouth, and is that a wage Tuesday is the day a harbor counts what it owes and that the count came to fifty-six weeks and one hundred and four marks in twenty-six lines and did not go down one mark because a boat of about nineteen tons was lying over a trench two days' sail off, and that a man who is four men short in a tide gang is four men short on a Tuesday as well.*
