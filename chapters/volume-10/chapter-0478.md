# Chapter 478

*Tuesday the Ninth Day of November, the Fifty-Fifth Wage Tuesday, That Column Not Moving on It and Fifty-Five Weeks Standing Fallen Due With No Envelope Against Any of Them, a Wage Column at Twenty-Six Lines and One Hundred and Four Marks With Twenty Envelopes in a Chest in a Front Room in a Harbor the Party Was Not In, a Day Apiece to the Same Four Men Going onto the Fitting-Out Sheet at Six Hundred and Seventy-Two Pence and One of the Four Asking a Third Question and Being Given the Honest Answer That Nothing Has Been Promised Beyond the Day and Nothing Is Owed, a Slate Written Before Anybody Went Down With a Figure of Forty Minutes of Air and a Named Tender on the Surface and Not in the Bell and a Cutoff That Tender Could Pull Without Asking, the Party Going Through That Doorway on the Ebb and Into a Long Hall With a Foot of Silt on the Floor of It and Nothing Fallen In From Above and Thirty Feet of Hull Planking Set Into the North Wall on Brackets About Five Feet Deep and Curving, and on the Third Plank From the Head of It a Mark of Nine Short Lines Joined at One Point Cut About an Inch and a Half Long Each and All the Same Depth and With No Figure Beside It and No Year Beside It and No Name Beside It, a Woman of Thirty of No Office Saying Five Things About That Mark Out Loud Before Anybody Could Say Them About Her and Not One Word of It Written in Her Own Mouth Before She Said It, and Nobody Thanked, the Shortfall at Six Thousand Six Hundred and Twenty-Two Pence, Being Forty-One Marks, Five Shillings and Twopence, and the Bag at Twenty-Eight Pence, Being Two Shillings and Fourpence, Against a Wage of Six Hundred and Forty*

Tuesday the ninth day of November, in about nine fathoms off a line, inside a built thing under the water, and on a boat of about nineteen tons that is two days' sail from that harbor.

**The said Tuesday was not a rota day and was the fifty-fifth wage Tuesday and was not a wipe, and a wage Tuesday in this harbor is a day the column is counted on and not a day it is moved on. The column is at twenty-six lines and one hundred and four marks with twenty envelopes in a chest in a front room, and it did not move, and fifty-five weeks stand fallen due with no envelope against any of them. One hundred and sixty-eight pence came into that yard at about ten in the morning, being a day's work with a boat and two men at a berth at the figure in chalk, and one hundred and sixty-eight pence went out of it at about four in the afternoon, being a day asked for by name, and there are twenty-eight pence in the bag at the close of the said day, being two shillings and fourpence, and the figure that bag is short of the wage of four marks is six hundred and twelve pence, being three marks eleven shillings. Nothing has been in a tin in that shed since the twenty-eighth day of April past, and not one penny of the whole of that trip came out of that bag, out of the owner of any boat in that harbor, or out of the Crown.**

**And the far bench was occupied from ten until four in that harbor and was not approached by anybody. Mara Venn was on that deck and going nowhere, of no office, not on the crew, not restored, not forgiven, forty marks first after food, neither reduced nor paid, and the leaf was under her own coat. Tobias Wren was on the crown of that mole from ten until four, being sixty-one. The notice of the seventeenth of May past is on its wall in a different street for one hundred and seventy-six days. The bellwright of that harbor has not been asked for one hundred and seventy-eight days, the chest is shut, and the eight marks and a day of a boat have not been argued. The three nots standing against the eighth of June past are an eighteenth week and nobody in that crew has said one word about them. The hand of a boy of twenty-two is at the head of the third column and that boy's surname is on no page of this harbor. The two objects of nobody's on that crown are five hundred and sixteen days old. The bench under that pump is bare for the two hundred and eighty-second night. Two lights at about a mile off that point have not moved in a hundred days. A man of about fifty-one in that harbor has said nothing to anybody about a page of six names for a hundred and eighteen days. The sheet of six lines at the back of that end stall is on that wall for the fifty-fifth day, the paper of four lines in Fish Street is on that door for the seventieth day, and the nine-line sheet with a seal on it is in that front street for the eighty-seventh day.**

---

A day apiece went to the same four men at about two in the afternoon, being six hundred and seventy-two pence, being four marks, two shillings and eightpence, and each of the four wrote his own name against his own line.

**One of the four asked a third question and he asked it in front of the other three, and the other three did not ask it for him and did not stop him, and he was answered in the yard's own mouth and the answer was not a comfort.**

He asked it in the terms a man asks it in when he has asked it twice before.

"If this boat goes back up that coast before the four days are up, do I get the day."

"No," the man who keeps the leaf said. "You get the days that have been paid and no more, and no promise stands behind them, and nothing has been said to you about the others and nothing is going to be said to you about the others until there is a day to say it about. That sheet is short by six thousand six hundred and twenty-two pence this afternoon and the man who asked it a fortnight ago asked it twice because it was a good question and it is still a good question and the answer is the same one both times."

The man who asked it said thank you, which nobody else on that deck has said out loud on any day of that trip, and the man who keeps the leaf said that he was not to be thanked for it, and he was not thanked for it after that.

---

**They went through that doorway at about ten in the morning on that ebb under a slate with the reason in his own words and the hour against it, with an air limit of a figure of forty minutes written on that slate and a named tender on the surface of that boat and not in the bell with his hand on the chain weight and a cutoff he could pull without asking anybody's leave. They were in the water about fifty minutes and on the bottom about thirty-two of them and came up at about ten to eleven, and nobody went into that mouth at the top of a flood on that day and nobody went in twice.**

Behind the doorway is a long hall. The floor of it carries about a foot of silt and the silt is level from the doorway to about forty feet in, and nothing has fallen into it from above and nothing is leaning on either wall. There are two rows of square-ended holes running the length of both side walls at about the height of a man's shoulder, spaced the same distance apart, and the holes are the same shape and the same size, and they are not new.

Set into the north wall on iron brackets about four feet off that floor is **about thirty feet of hull planking**, and it is curving, and it is five feet deep at the middle and tapers at both ends, and it is fastened with metal and not with glass, and it is a piece of a boat's bottom kept indoors in a building, the way a house keeps a stone.

**On the third plank from the head of it there is a mark, and it is nine short lines joined at one point, and each of the nine is about an inch and a half long, and they are cut to the same depth with a graver, and they meet in the middle and they do not cross, and there is no figure beside it and no year beside it and no name beside it.**

Ollum Trevask looked at it for a long time and gave no number and said why, and he said the same reason twice that week and it is not the same reason as the one he gave about the bar.

"I have been putting my hand on other people's cuts for nineteen years and I have never had my hand on a cut like that one. It is a graver and it is not a stamp and it is not a die and there is nothing pressed into anything. I am not going to give it a figure and I told you on the fourth why I do not give figures to things I cannot put my life beside, and you have heard me once this week and you can have it twice."

---

**And at about a quarter to eleven in the morning a woman of thirty of no office, who has been reading things out of glass for eleven years, said five things about that mark out loud on that deck before anybody could say any of them about her, and she said them in the order they are written here and she did not soften one of them and she did not put a face on while she said it.**

"That is a builder's mark. It was cut by whoever made that boat and by nobody else and it was cut on her and not on her paper and it is not a name.

"It was cut before any claim was laid over that ground. Whatever is standing on that shelf with a seal on it arrived six hundred years and more after a man with a graver was at work on a plank in a hall with a silt on the floor of it, and no office has ever had anything to do with that plank before this morning.

"It is not a seal. A seal is pressed and it has a rim and a die in it and it is put on a thing by somebody saying out loud that they are answerable for it. That mark is cut by a builder and a builder is answerable to the person who paid him and to nobody else and not from any distance.

"It is not a limit of anybody's consent. No person put it there and no person can take it off and it says nothing about what any living person agreed to, and a thing like that standing in a room is not permission for anything.

"And it is not proof that anybody agreed to anything. Nine lines in a point means a maker and a yard and a contract of some kind between a man who wanted a boat and a man who built one, and it means nothing whatever about the people who were in the boat or about anybody who ever heard of it."

Nobody argued with her and nobody agreed with her and nobody thanked her and no word of it was written down before she said it, and the whole of the five went into the second column of that leaf that evening in her own mouth and in the order she said them, with the hour against them, and the reason they are in the second column is the reason given at the foot of that column on that day.

Nobody in that boat compared it with anything, because there is a drawing of a mark in a book in a shed two days' sail off and a drawing is not a mark.

---

**THE OLDEST THING ANYBODY ON THAT SHELF HAS PUT A HAND ON IS A MARK CUT WITH A GRAVER ON A PLANK IN A ROOM, AND IT IS NINE SHORT LINES JOINED AT ONE POINT, AND IT CARRIES NO FIGURE AND NO YEAR AND NO NAME. A WOMAN OF THIRTY OF NO OFFICE SAID FIVE THINGS ABOUT IT OUT LOUD BEFORE ANYBODY COULD SAY THEM ABOUT HER, AND THE FIVE ARE THAT IT IS A BUILDER'S MARK, THAT IT WAS CUT BEFORE ANY CLAIM WAS LAID OVER THAT GROUND, THAT IT IS NOT A SEAL, THAT IT IS NOT A LIMIT OF ANYBODY'S CONSENT, AND THAT IT IS NOT PROOF THAT ANYBODY AGREED TO ANYTHING. NOBODY THANKED HER. THE FIFTY-FIFTH WAGE TUESDAY CAME AND WENT AND THAT COLUMN DID NOT MOVE AND FIFTY-FIVE WEEKS STAND FALLEN DUE WITH NO ENVELOPE AGAINST ANY OF THEM, AND A DAY APIECE WENT TO THE SAME FOUR MEN AT SIX HUNDRED AND SEVENTY-TWO PENCE AND ONE OF THEM ASKED A THIRD QUESTION AND WAS TOLD THAT HE GETS THE DAYS THAT HAVE BEEN PAID AND NO MORE AND THAT NOTHING HAS BEEN PROMISED BEYOND THE DAY. AND THAT SHEET IS SIX THOUSAND SIX HUNDRED AND TWENTY-TWO PENCE SHORT AT THE CLOSE OF THIS DAY, BEING FORTY-ONE MARKS, FIVE SHILLINGS AND TWOPENCE, AND NO FIGURE HAS BEEN ASKED OF ANYBODY TO MAKE IT OTHERWISE.**

*First column, entered on Tuesday the ninth day of November next at about half past nine in the evening on that deck: that the said day was not a rota day and was the fifty-fifth wage Tuesday and was not a wipe, and that a wage Tuesday in that harbor is a day the column is counted on and not a day it is moved on; and that the column is at twenty-six lines and one hundred and four marks with twenty envelopes in a chest in a front room and did not move on that day, and that fifty-five weeks stand fallen due with no envelope against any of them; and that one hundred and sixty-eight pence came into that yard and one hundred and sixty-eight went out of it, and that twenty-eight plus one hundred and sixty-eight less one hundred and sixty-eight is twenty-eight pence at the close of the said day, being two shillings and fourpence, being short of the wage of four marks by six hundred and twelve pence, being three marks eleven shillings; and that a day apiece went to the same four men at about two in the afternoon, being six hundred and seventy-two pence, being four marks, two shillings and eightpence, and each of the four wrote his own name against his own line; and that one of the four asked a third question in front of the other three, and that the other three did not ask it for him and did not stop him, and that he was answered in the yard's own mouth that he gets the days that have been paid and no more and that no promise stands behind them and that nothing is going to be said to him about the others until there is a day to say it about, and that he said thank you and the man who keeps the leaf said he was not to be thanked for it; and that the party went through that doorway at about ten in the morning on that ebb under a slate with the reason in his own words and the hour against it, with an air limit of forty minutes written on that slate and a named tender on the surface and not in the bell and a cutoff that tender could pull without asking, that they were in the water about fifty minutes and on the bottom about thirty-two of them and came up at about ten to eleven; and that nobody went into that mouth at the top of a flood on that day and nobody went in twice; and that behind that doorway is a long hall carrying about a foot of level silt with nothing fallen into it from above and nothing leaning on either wall, and that there are two rows of square-ended holes running the length of both side walls at about the height of a man's shoulder, spaced the same distance apart, the same shape and the same size; and that about thirty feet of curving hull planking is set into the north wall on iron brackets about four feet off that floor, five feet deep at the middle and tapering at both ends, fastened with metal and not with glass; and that on the third plank from the head of it there is a mark of nine short lines joined at one point, each of the nine about an inch and a half long, cut to the same depth with a graver, meeting in the middle and not crossing, with no figure beside it and no year beside it and no name beside it; and that a man of about thirty-four looked at it for a long time and gave no number and said he had never had his hand on a cut like it in nineteen years; and that a woman of thirty of no office said five things about that mark out loud on that deck at about a quarter to eleven in the morning before anybody could say them about her, and that the five are on the face of that column; and that nobody argued with her, nobody agreed with her, nobody thanked her, and that no word of it was written down before she said it; and that nobody in that boat compared it with anything, because there is a drawing of a mark in a book in a shed two days' sail off and a drawing is not a mark; and that the fitting-out sheet stands at ten thousand six hundred and forty pence in and seventeen thousand two hundred and sixty-two pence out and is six thousand six hundred and twenty-two pence short, being forty-one marks, five shillings and twopence.*

*Second column, not checkable from a mole, entered on the said Tuesday at about half past nine in the evening on that deck, and no name is against it: that the five things a woman of thirty said about that mark are in this column and not in the first because a stranger with a pencil can check the first column and cannot check whether a person consented to anything, and because the whole of the argument that this party is having with three offices is about what a mark on a thing does and does not do, and if the yard itself wrote one of those two things into a column a man can check, then the yard has agreed that a mark does what an office says it does; and that the reason she said them out loud before anybody could say them about her is her own, and is that a person who has not said a thing first has had it said for her, and that everything she said that morning had a face on that nobody in that boat had put there; and that the reason nobody thanked her is that thanking a person for saying out loud what a mark is not is a way of thanking a person for helping, and she was not helping and did not want to be thanked and said so on the sixth day of November in another column; and that the reason the four men were paid a day apiece on that day is that they are on the water and a day is a day and the sheet is short and being short is not a reason to hold back a day that has been earned; and that the standing list entered in full on the fourteenth day of August past is not entered in full on the said Tuesday and stands, and no part of it was paid, reduced, advanced or improved on that day.*
