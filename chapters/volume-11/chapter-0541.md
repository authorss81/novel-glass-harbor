# Chapter 541

*Wednesday the Twelfth Day of January, Not a Rota Day and Not a Wage Tuesday and Not a Wipe, a Cart Going Down That Coast Road on the Monday the Ninth of December Past With a Page Carrying Four Names and Five Marks and Coming Back Up at About Half Past Eleven in the Morning on the Wednesday With Three Windows and Three Bells Written on the Back of It in Three Different Hands, a Sheet of Paper Put on That Bench Stone at About Ten in the Morning and Read Out Loud Before a Word of It Was Written, Three Crews Asked One at a Time in the Same Eighteen Words and Three Answers, and the Man of About Thirty-Two Answering for Himself and Being the Third Name Down on the First of the Three and Not at the Head of Any of Them, Eight Slates Written at About Two in the Afternoon, Three of Them in That Shed and Five at the Boats, Each Carrying a Reason in That Man's Own Words and the Hour Against It and a Figure of Twenty-Five Minutes of Air, a Man of About Thirty-Three Who Hires Hands Asking Out Loud at About Half Past Three Who Is In Charge and Being Answered in About Sixty Words, Nothing Going Onto the Sheet That Carries the Money in Any Hand and Nothing Going Into It in Any Hand and Nobody Asked for Anything, the Shortfall Said Out Loud at About Half Past Five Unmoved at Sixty-Five Thousand Eight Hundred and Fifty Pence, Being Four Hundred and Eleven Marks, Seven Shillings and Sixpence, the Four Heads on That Wall Not Changed and No Fifth Head Added on Account of a Night, the Bag at Twenty-Eight Pence, Being Two Shillings and Fourpence*

Wednesday the twelfth day of January, in that harbor, in about four hundred yards of water and along a stone quay, and in a shed with a roof on it, a bench stone, and a door that stands open on a slip.

**The said Wednesday was not a rota day and was not a wage Tuesday and was not a wipe, and no charge was placed on that cistern on it. Nothing went out on any line of the sheet that carries the money in any hand on that day and nothing went into it in any hand, and nobody was asked for anything. One hundred and sixty-eight pence came into that yard at about ten in the morning and one hundred and sixty-eight pence went out of it at about four in the afternoon, and there are twenty-eight pence in the bag at the close of the said day, being two shillings and fourpence, and the figure that bag is short of the wage of four marks is six hundred and twelve pence, being three marks eleven shillings. That sheet stands at seventy-six thousand four hundred and ninety pence out, being four hundred and seventy-eight marks and tenpence, and ten thousand six hundred and forty pence in, being sixty-six marks, six shillings and eightpence, and it is sixty-five thousand eight hundred and fifty pence short, being four hundred and eleven marks, seven shillings and sixpence, and that figure is against that yard's own book and was said out loud in that yard's own mouth and was not answered by anybody. Nothing has been in a tin in that shed since the twenty-ninth day of April past, and no penny of the whole of that day came out of that bag, out of the owner of any boat, out of the man with two papers, or out of the Crown.**

**The nine that a stranger in that harbor could have read off any wall are the nine below, and not one of them was found by looking: the notice of the seventeenth of May past, two hundred and thirty-nine days; the bellwright of that harbor, not asked for two hundred and forty-one; the two objects of nobody's on that crown, five hundred and seventy-nine days; the bench under that pump, bare for the three hundred and forty-fifth night; the two lights at about a mile off that point, one hundred and sixty-three days; the six-line sheet at the back of that end stall, its hundred and eighteenth day; the paper of four lines in Fish Street, its hundred and thirty-third; the nine-line sheet with a seal on it in that front street, its hundred and fiftieth; and a man of about fifty-one who has said nothing to anybody about a page of six names for a hundred and eighty-one days. The paper with a seal on it and no figure on it that was nailed to the board at the head of that mole on the third of December is still nailed to that board, and it has not been taken off it, and it was not opened then and it has not been opened since, and no man in that harbor has a key to it. The ninth hull of that line about two sea miles off the head of that mole was counted once at about two o'clock in the afternoon on Friday by a man with a glass, and he told one other person, and that person has told nobody. A man of about sixty-one who repairs boats sat on that crown of the mole from ten until four with a boat's hook upright between his boots and did not look up at anybody and was not sent for and was not thanked. A man of about twenty-nine who came home on that boat can hear out of the left side of his head and it has been thirty-nine days and he is into his second week at that listening station and nobody in either harbor has said a word to him about it and he has not asked anybody for anything.**

---

At about ten in the morning the man who keeps the leaf put a clean sheet on that bench stone, and he did not write on it until he had read out loud what he was going to put on it, and about nine people were on that slip and heard all of it.

"Three points, three windows and three bells. That is the whole of it. **The whole of the coordination of Friday night is a piece of paper with three hours on it and a bell hanging under a board on each of the three marks, and there is not one word on this piece of paper that is an order, and there is no man whose name is at the top of it.**"

He wrote it then, and it is this.

1. *First point, the one off the stone with the chain in it, in about four fathoms, and the mark at the top of it is the first mark on the page on the boards of that market. Over the rail at about half past eleven. Last helmet off at about one o'clock.*
2. *Second point, the one off the broken ground, in about four fathoms, and the mark at the top of it is the second mark on the same page. Over the rail at about twenty past one. Last helmet off at about half past two.*
3. *Third point, the outer one, in about four fathoms, and the mark at the top of it is the third mark on the same page, and that one is a bell and not a float. Over the rail at about twenty to three. Last helmet off at about five to four.*
4. *And a bell on each of the three, rung at the hour written against that point by the crew that answers for that mark, and not one of the three bells is rung by anybody else, and no bell is rung twice.*

He said out loud, before he put the pen down, why there is no name at the head of it, and he said it once.

"If I put a name at the top of that, then on Friday night there is a man in three places at once, and he cannot be in three places at once, and every man on that slip will have worked out inside about a minute of watching me write it what that means. **A thing that works because three men who do not know each other turned up at three different hours is a different thing from a thing that works because somebody stood in the middle of it, and I know which one of those I would rather have at four o'clock in the morning on a tide.** And a page a stranger can take three hours off is a page worth having, and a page with my name at the top of it is a page that a stranger has to take my word for."

---

And at about half past eleven in the morning a cart came back up that coast road from that port four days down that coast, and it brought back the page that had gone down it on the Monday, and on the back of that page there are three windows and three bells written in three different hands, and the four names and the five marks are on the front of it and were not touched.

Hobb Lee, of about forty-six, who mends nets on the third lay, is the one who put that page up on four tacks on the boards of the floor of that market on the Sunday the twenty-sixth, and he was the one who stood in the room on the Saturday of that market and read out the crossing of the thirtieth word for word with his own name and the hour under it, and he put the cart's tally on the bench stone at about half past twelve and said out loud, in about fifty words, what that tally was.

"Three hands and three answers. **Nobody in that port has been asked for anything and nobody in that port has written a name in the second of those three spaces, and that is not a fault in a page, that is the page being honest about who is in it.**"

The front of the page still carries the four names, and the marks each of them answers for, and the name of the man of about thirty-two is not on it and is not under it and is not on the back of it either, and it never was. What is on the back of it are the three windows, and each of the three is in a different hand, and the first of the three has three names in it and the second two and the third three, and there are eight names on the three of them and eight slates, and the man of about thirty-two is the third of the three on the first window and is at the bottom of them and not at the top.

---

And at about two in the afternoon, in three separate corners of that slip and about forty feet apart from each other, eight slates were written and there are eight of them, and three of them are here and a man can go and read the other five at the boats.

Each of the eight carries a reason in the words of the man it was written for, and the hour against it, and a figure of twenty-five minutes of air. None of them carries a name, and each of them is signed with a boat, and a date, and an hour, and the date on all eight is the twelfth of this month at about half past one in the afternoon, being the afternoon they were said out loud on that slip.

1. *Because there is a stone under that first mark with something in it that came in on a Monday night and has been in the way of two lines ever since, and it is four fathoms and I would rather know what is in it than be told. Twenty-five minutes. If I say nothing for two minutes, pull it. — the first point, the twelfth of January at about half past one.*
2. *Because I have run the second lay inside that line for nineteen winters and the ground under the broken ground comes up in a net three times in ten and I have never been told what is under it. Twenty-five minutes. If I say nothing for two minutes, pull it. — the second point, the twelfth of January at about half past one.*
3. *Because that outer one is the only one of the three that a boat of any size can get round, and if it is not put in properly on Friday it will be the same in March, and I have a girl of fifteen and I would like her to have a coast to come into. Twenty-five minutes. If I say nothing for two minutes, pull it. — the third point, the twelfth of January at about half past one.*

**Ivo Sark, of about twenty-four, one of a master's two men, is the named tender on the surface at the first point, in a dinghy with a chain weight in his own hand and a cutoff he can pull without asking anybody's leave, and he is not in that water at any hour of Friday night. A pump hand of about forty-four with nineteen years of compressors is the named tender on the surface at the second point and is in nobody's crew and is a member of nothing. A woman of about thirty-four of that commonwealth is the named tender on the surface at the third point and is not in that yard and is not its mouth, and her condition is still on the face of the record four hundred yards from that bench stone and is still not settled and is still not agreeable.** The man who keeps the leaf said out loud that he was not the tender at any of the three, and that a man who writes the hours is not the man with the cutoff, and that a page with three tenders on it is a page with three men in it.

---

And at about half past three in the afternoon a man of about thirty-three who hires hands stood at the corner of that slip and asked out loud, in front of about eleven people, who was in charge, and he was answered in about sixty words and did not ask it again.

"Three men in three boats and no man in the middle. **If that mark is not put in on Friday, nobody rings that bell on Saturday, and if a bell does not go, a boat stays out, and if a boat stays out then nobody on that coast finds out anything before Sunday, and I am the man who finds things out before Sunday.**"

He said that was worth knowing and went off up the quay and he has not asked it a second time.

A woman of about fifty-two who keeps a fish stall at the head of that quay said one thing about the page of hours and nobody asked her for a second thing.

"**Three bells in one night, and the three of them are on a piece of paper, and I cannot read the piece of paper, and I do not need to, because the bells are the only part of it I would have been able to hear anyway.**"

---

And at about half past five in the afternoon the man who keeps the leaf said the shortfall out loud at that bench stone with the door of that shed open on the slip, and said that the day had not touched it, and nobody in that harbor was asked for the rest of it.

"Sixty-five thousand eight hundred and fifty pence short, being four hundred and eleven marks, seven shillings and sixpence. Unmoved since two o'clock on Saturday morning and it is going to move in the morning, because tomorrow is a Thursday and a Thursday is victual and coal and air, and those three things are for a night on the water and not for a man standing on a slip. Seventy-six thousand four hundred and ninety out and ten thousand six hundred and forty in, and a stranger with a leaf and a pencil can take all three of those figures off that sheet in about a minute and find that they agree."

"And **I have arranged three points for Friday night and I have not bought one foot of rope for any of them, and I am not going to buy one foot of rope for any of them out of that bag, and there is a woman on that slip who has just told me that the bells are the only part of the page she could have heard, and she is right, and it took me nine words of argument with a man of about thirty-three to get to the same place, and I have not said that out loud twice.**"

Nobody in that harbor was asked for the rest of it, no figure has been asked of any person in that harbor to make it otherwise, and nobody was thanked for anything on that Wednesday, and nobody was thanked for the page and nobody was thanked for the three answers and nobody was thanked for the eight slates.

---

**A PAGE CARRYING THREE WINDOWS AND THREE BELLS WAS PUT ON A BENCH STONE IN THAT SHED AT ABOUT TEN IN THE MORNING AND WAS READ OUT LOUD BEFORE A WORD OF IT WAS WRITTEN, AND THERE IS NO NAME AT THE HEAD OF IT. THREE CREWS WERE ASKED ONE AT A TIME IN THE SAME EIGHTEEN WORDS AND GAVE THREE ANSWERS, AND THE MAN OF ABOUT THIRTY-TWO ANSWERED FOR HIMSELF AND IS THE BOTTOM NAME OF THREE ON THE FIRST OF THE THREE AND IS NOT THE HEAD OF ANY OF THEM. EIGHT SLATES CARRY A REASON IN EACH MAN'S OWN WORDS AND THE HOUR AND A FIGURE OF TWENTY-FIVE MINUTES, AND THREE NAMED TENDERS ARE ON THE SURFACE AND NOT IN THE WATER AND ONE OF THE THREE OF THEM IS A WOMAN OF THAT COMMONWEALTH WHOSE CONDITION IS STILL UNSETTLED. NOTHING WENT ONTO THAT SHEET IN ANY HAND AND NOTHING WENT INTO IT AND NOBODY WAS ASKED FOR ANYTHING. THE FOUR HEADS ON THAT WALL DID NOT CHANGE AND NO FIFTH HEAD WENT ON IT ON ACCOUNT OF A NIGHT. THE SHEET THAT CARRIES THE MONEY IS SIXTY-FIVE THOUSAND EIGHT HUNDRED AND FIFTY PENCE SHORT AGAINST THAT YARD'S OWN BOOK, BEING FOUR HUNDRED AND ELEVEN MARKS, SEVEN SHILLINGS AND SIXPENCE, AND THERE ARE TWENTY-EIGHT PENCE IN THE BAG UNDER THE SLATE.**

*First column, entered on Wednesday the twelfth day of January at about half past nine in the evening in that shed: that the said day was not a rota day and was not a wage Tuesday and was not a wipe, and that no charge was placed on that cistern on it; and that a cart went down that coast road on Monday the ninth of December past with a page carrying four names and five marks on it and came back up that road at about half past eleven in the morning on the said Wednesday, and that the three windows and the three bells on the back of that page are in three different hands, and that the first of the three carries three names and the second two and the third three, and that the man of about thirty-two is the bottom of the three on the first of them and is not at the top of any of them and his name is not on the front of that page and was never on it; and that a clean sheet was put on that bench stone at about ten in the morning with three windows and three bells on it and was read out loud in full before a word of it was written; and that eight slates were written at about two in the afternoon, three of them read out in that shed and the other five at the boats, each carrying a reason in that man's own words and the hour and a figure of twenty-five minutes of air, and that Ivo Sark, of about twenty-four, is the named tender on the surface at the first point, that a pump hand of about forty-four with nineteen years of compressors is the named tender on the surface at the second and is in nobody's crew, and that a woman of about thirty-four of that commonwealth is the named tender on the surface at the third and is not in that yard; and that the man who keeps the leaf said out loud that he was not the tender at any of the three and that a man who writes the hours is not the man with the cutoff; and that a man of about thirty-three who hires hands asked out loud who was in charge and was answered in about sixty words and did not ask it again; and that a woman of about fifty-two who keeps a fish stall at the head of that quay said out loud that the three bells are the only part of the page she would have been able to hear, and was asked nothing further; and that nothing went onto that sheet in any hand in any line on that day and nothing went into it and nobody was asked for anything, and that one hundred and sixty-eight pence came into that yard and one hundred and sixty-eight pence went out of it, and that twenty-eight plus one hundred and sixty-eight less one hundred and sixty-eight is twenty-eight pence at the close of the said day, being two shillings and fourpence, being short of the wage of four marks by six hundred and twelve pence, being three marks eleven shillings; and that that sheet stood at seventy-six thousand four hundred and ninety pence out and ten thousand six hundred and forty pence in and is sixty-five thousand eight hundred and fifty pence short, being four hundred and eleven marks, seven shillings and sixpence, and that the running out on it did not move from the close of the second of this month; and that the four columns at the close of that day stand at two hundred and seventy-four, one thousand two hundred and forty-four, none and one hundred and twenty-five, and at the hour of that day's reading at two hundred and seventy-three, one thousand two hundred and forty-two; and that the third column is empty and nobody has been put in it and the hand of a boy of twenty-two is at the head of it and his surname is on no page of this, and that the fourth column stands at one hundred and twenty-five and took no line and has no hundred and twenty-sixth line in it and is neither closed nor struck; and that the paper with a seal on it and no figure on it from the third of December is still on the board at the head of that mole and was not opened.*

*Second column, not checkable from a mole, entered on the said Wednesday at about half past nine in the evening in that shed, and no name is against it: that the reason there is no name at the top of that page of hours is his own, and is that a man at the top of three things is a man who is in none of them by the small hours, and that the page has to still be true on the Saturday morning when the people who wrote it are asleep and the only things awake on that coast are three boats and three bells; and that the reason the three crews were asked in the same eighteen words is Hobb Lee's, and is that a question put three ways is three questions and a question put one way is one question with three answers to it, and that the whole difference between a page and a list is whether anybody can tell afterwards what was asked; and that the reason the man of about thirty-two's name is at the bottom of three and not at the top of any of them is his own, and is that he has been at the top of three things since the middle of December and it has not once been the reason anything worked, and that a name at the bottom of three names on a piece of paper is a thing a stranger can take off and check, and a name at the top of three is a thing a stranger has to believe; and that the reason the eight slates carry no name is his own again, and is that a slate with a name on it is a slate a man's employer can read, and a slate with a boat and an hour on it is a slate a man can be held to, and the second of those is worth something and the first of those is worth nothing at three in the morning; and that the reason the tender at the third point is a woman whose condition is still on that record four hundred yards from that bench stone and is not settled is his own and is not a settlement of it, and is that a condition that has been stated out loud in a room and has not been agreed to is still a condition, and that a person who brought it is owed the first refusal on a thing that is dangerous and not the first thanks, and that a man who gives the first refusal of a night on the water to a woman who has asked for a boat's work is not doing her a favour and must not be described as doing her one; and that the reason nothing standing in that harbor was paid, advanced, cut back, bettered, struck or closed on that Wednesday is his own, and is that the whole of the four heads on that wall was decided before the paper of hours was written and nothing on that slip this afternoon had the standing to move any of it, and that the three bells on that page are the only thing this yard has produced this month that a person who cannot read can check by going outside.*
