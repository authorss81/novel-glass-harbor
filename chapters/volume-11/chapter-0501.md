# Chapter 501

*Friday the Third Day of December, Not a Rota Day and Not a Wage Tuesday and Not a Wipe, a Line of Boats Standing About Two Sea Miles Off the Mole Head at About Ten Past Six in the Morning on Which There Are Nine Hulls and a Man About Fifty With a Boat of About Twenty-Two Tons Saw It First Off the Third Lay and Did Not Go and Tell Anybody Until About Eight o'Clock, a Paper With a Seal on It and No Figure on It Nailed to the Board at the Head of That Mole at About a Quarter Past One in the Afternoon and Not Opened and Not Copied and No Key to It Given to Anybody in That Harbor, About Eleven People Standing at It in the Dark and About Eleven of Them Who Cannot Read It, a Victual of Bread and Salt Beef for Nine Men for Ten Days Going Onto the Fitting-Out Sheet at About Ten in the Morning at One Thousand Six Hundred Pence, Being Ten Marks, and a Day Apiece in Advance to the Same Four Men at Six Hundred and Seventy-Two Pence, Being Four Marks, Two Shillings and Eightpence, and Nothing Going into That Sheet in Any Hand, One Hundred and Sixty-Eight Pence Coming Into That Yard at About Ten in the Morning and One Hundred and Sixty-Eight Going Out of It at About Four in the Afternoon, the Shortfall Said Out Loud at About Half Past Five in the Afternoon at Twenty-Four Thousand One Hundred and Eighty-Two Pence, Being One Hundred and Fifty-One Marks, One Shilling and Tenpence, the Bag at Twenty-Eight Pence, Being Two Shillings and Fourpence, Against a Wage of Six Hundred and Forty*

Friday the third day of December, in that harbor, in about four hundred yards of water and along a stone quay, and in about two sea miles of open water off the head of that mole.

**The said Friday was not a rota day and was not a wage Tuesday and was not a wipe, and no charge was placed on that cistern on it. Bread and salt beef for nine men for ten days went onto the sheet that carries the money at about ten in the morning at one thousand six hundred pence, being ten marks, and a day apiece in advance to the same four men went on at the same hour at six hundred and seventy-two pence, being four marks, two shillings and eightpence, and nothing went into that sheet in any hand in any line on that day and nobody was asked for anything. One hundred and sixty-eight pence came into that yard at about ten in the morning, being a day's work with a boat and two men at a berth at the figure in chalk on a slate at an end stall, and one hundred and sixty-eight pence went out of it at about four in the afternoon, being a day asked for by name, and there are twenty-eight pence in the bag at the close of the said day, being two shillings and fourpence, and the figure that bag is short of the wage of four marks is six hundred and twelve pence, being three marks eleven shillings. That sheet stands at thirty-four thousand eight hundred and twenty-two pence out, being two hundred and seventeen marks, eight shillings and sixpence, and ten thousand six hundred and forty pence in, being sixty-six marks, six shillings and eightpence, and it is twenty-four thousand one hundred and eighty-two pence short, being one hundred and fifty-one marks, one shilling and tenpence, and that figure is against that yard's own book and was said out loud in that yard's own mouth and was not answered by anybody. Nothing has been in a tin in that shed since the twenty-ninth day of April past, and no penny of the whole of that trip came out of that bag, out of the owner of any boat, out of the man with two papers, or out of the Crown.**

**The nine that a stranger in that harbor could have read off any wall are the nine below, and not one of them was found by looking: the notice of the seventeenth of May past, one hundred and ninety-nine days; the bellwright of that harbor, not asked for two hundred and one; the two objects of nobody's on that crown, five hundred and thirty-nine days; the bench under that pump, bare for the three hundred and fifth night; the two lights at about a mile off that point, one hundred and twenty-three days; the six-line sheet at the back of that end stall, its seventy-eighth day; the paper of four lines in Fish Street, its ninety-third; the nine-line sheet with a seal on it in that front street, its hundred and tenth; and a man of about fifty-one who has said nothing to anybody about a page of six names for a hundred and forty-one days. The mole-head light in that harbor is out and its lens has been out since the seventh day of May. The north berth is out and has been out since the autumn. There is no flour in that market and none is coming this month. A man of about twenty-nine who came home on that boat cannot hear out of the left side of his head, and it is still gone on that Friday, and he has said nothing about it and nobody in that harbor has asked him.**

---

**The first man in that harbor to see it was a man of about fifty who owns a boat of about twenty-two tons, and he was on the third lay at about ten past six in the morning with his own boat's painter half in and a coil of light line over his forearm, and he stood there with his hand on the ring and did not call out, and it was three quarters of an hour later before he went up to the shed at the head of that lay and said it out loud.**

What he said, before he said anything else, was the shape of it and not what he thought it was.

"Nine hulls," he said. "Two of them are big enough to have a gunwale you could land a plank on and the other seven are not. They are standing off the mole head about two sea miles and they are lying the same way and they are not swinging. The tide is half past the ebb and they have not swung. A boat that is lying does not swing with the tide and a boat that is under way does, and there has not been a hull under way in that line since the light went."

Nessa Pike, of about twenty-seven of that yard, came out of the shed door with a basket on her hip and did not ask him what he thought it was.

"How long."

"I was on that lay at about ten past six and the water was making and there was nothing on it," he said. "And the woman at the end stall with the slate was on her step at half past seven and there was nothing on it while she was on that step. So it came in the night. I did not see it come in and I am not going to say I did."

---

**And two people in that harbor gave two accounts of what it was on that morning, and they do not agree, and both of them are standing a fortnight later exactly as they stood that morning.**

The woman with the end stall and the slate said it in about one sentence, at about ten minutes to nine, to nobody in particular, in the way she says a price.

"That is what it looks like when two boats of a supply do not come in."

A man of about thirty-three who hires hands at that market for whoever will pay him said the other thing, at about a quarter to ten, and he said it to the woman, and he was not laughing.

"That is a line put where it can be seen. There is a difference and you know there is a difference. A shortage is somebody else's problem. Somebody has drawn a mark at about two sea miles and put boats on it, and a shortage does not need a mark, because a shortage arrives at your own gate by itself."

**Nobody in that market answered either of them, and both of them were still standing in the same places on the twelfth day of December, and neither of them was asked again what they had said on that Friday, and the two accounts have never been agreed with and have never been taken back.**

---

**And at about a quarter past one in the afternoon a boat's boat came up inside that mole with two men in it, and one of them got out at the head of that mole with a bag and a maul and a sheet of paper rolled in the bag, and he nailed that sheet to the board at the head of that mole and he did not put the maul down while he did it and he did not say one word to anybody while he did it, and he was back in that boat's boat and out through the harbor inside a quarter of an hour.**

It is about two feet by a foot and a half. There is a seal pressed into the paper at the foot of it and there is no figure on it anywhere, and the head of it carries a date and a place, and the second line of it sets down about seven things, and under that there are about nine lines of rule. **It went on that board and it has not been taken off it and it was not opened and nobody in that harbor has a key to it and nobody in that harbor is going to be given one.**

The light that is meant to stand over that board is out and its lens has been out since the seventh of May, so the people who stood at that mole head between two and four in the afternoon were standing in the dark in front of a board with a sheet of paper nailed to it, and about eleven of them could not read what was on it.

The man who keeps the leaf went up and read it. He read it standing up with his hands behind his back and he did not take it off that board, and he read it about four times, and then he came back down that mole to his own shed and said two things about it out loud, and neither of the two things was about what it means.

"I am not copying that," he said. "There is no line on that sheet in this yard and there is not going to be one, and the reason is not a figure and the reason is that this yard is not answerable for one word of it, and a page this yard keeps is a page this yard is answerable for. That paper is nailed to a board out there where any man in this harbor can walk up and read it, and there is nothing on this slip that I could put in front of a man in an office, and I would not know where to put it."

"And can anybody say what is on it."

"No," he said. "I can tell you what is standing on it. There is a date at the head of it and a seal at the foot of it and about seven things set down in the second line and about nine lines of rule under that, and there is not one person's name on the whole of it that I could see standing where I was standing. That is the whole of what I know about it."

Nobody asked him a second question about it. A man of about fifty-eight of the second lay asked one other person at that mole head at about half past two in the afternoon whether it was about the boats, and the person he asked said he did not know, and that was the end of it, and nobody wrote the answer down because there was not one.

---

**The two lines went onto that sheet at about ten in the morning and the man who keeps the leaf said the whole of each of them out loud before he wrote a figure against it, in that yard's own mouth, and nobody argued with him about any part of either of them.**

"Bread and salt beef for nine men for ten days. One thousand six hundred pence, being ten marks. That is the figure I paid the same victual at on the twenty-third day of November and it is the figure I have paid it at every other time and I am not carrying it anywhere else."

"And then four days apiece to the same four men in advance, being six hundred and seventy-two pence, being four marks, two shillings and eightpence. That is four men at one hundred and sixty-eight pence apiece, being a day apiece at the figure in chalk, and the four of them are not on this sheet and are not counted on it and their names are in the book the days are paid from and not here."

He wrote the third day of December against both of them and put the pen down flat.

**And then he said one thing out loud, once, and he said it to the four men standing there and not to anybody in Fish Street, and it is the whole of what he said about a day on which no boat in that harbor went out.**

"You are not losing a day because of anything you did," he said. "I am not going to put that on you and I am not going to put it on anybody. The figure in chalk has not moved and it is not going to move this week whatever is standing off that mole, and a man who loses a day's work because of a thing he did not decide is still owed the hour he worked, and I will pay the same four of you the same one hundred and sixty-eight pence at four o'clock this afternoon with the water as flat as it was on Tuesday."

The four men heard him. Nobody thanked him. One of them asked whether the figure in chalk would be the same on Monday and he said it would be the same on Monday and he was not going to say what it would be in a week.

---

**And at about half past five in the afternoon he said the shortfall out loud, standing at the bench stone with the door open on the slip, and nobody in that harbor was asked for the rest of it and nobody was asked to put a mark on anything.**

"Twenty-four thousand one hundred and eighty-two pence short, being one hundred and fifty-one marks, one shilling and tenpence. That is against this yard's own book and it is nobody's else and I am not going to ask anybody in this harbor for it."

---

**THERE ARE NINE HULLS LYING IN A LINE ABOUT TWO SEA MILES OFF THE HEAD OF THAT MOLE AND THEY HAVE NOT SWUNG WITH THE HALF EBB AND THERE WERE NONE ON THAT WATER AT HALF PAST SEVEN WHEN A WOMAN WITH AN END STALL AND A SLATE WAS STANDING ON HER OWN STEP. TWO PEOPLE IN THAT MARKET HAVE SAID TWO THINGS ABOUT IT THAT DO NOT AGREE AND BOTH OF THEM HAVE STOOD SINCE. A SHEET OF PAPER WITH A SEAL ON IT AND NO FIGURE ON IT IS NAILED TO THE BOARD AT THE HEAD OF THAT MOLE UNDER A LIGHT THAT IS OUT, AND IT HAS NOT BEEN TAKEN OFF THAT BOARD AND IT WAS NOT OPENED. A BOAT OF ABOUT TWENTY-TWO TONS LIES ON THE THIRD LAY WITH HER PAINTER HALF IN AND A COIL OF LIGHT LINE OVER HER OWNER'S FOREARM. A SLATE LEANS ON THAT BENCH STONE WITH A FIGURE OF TWENTY-EIGHT PENCE UNDER A FIGURE OF SIX HUNDRED AND FORTY. THE CISTERN AT THE BACK OF THAT MARKET IS CHARGED ON A MONDAY AND A THURSDAY AND WAS NOT CHARGED ON THAT FRIDAY. THE WAGE COLUMN IN THAT HARBOR STANDS AT TWENTY-SIX LINES AND ONE HUNDRED AND FOUR MARKS WITH TWENTY ENVELOPES IN A CHEST AND FIFTY-EIGHT WEEKS OF IT STAND FALLEN DUE AND IT DID NOT MOVE ON THAT DAY. THE MOLE-HEAD LIGHT IS OUT AND ITS LENS HAS BEEN OUT SINCE THE SEVENTH DAY OF MAY. NOBODY IN THAT HARBOR HAS BEEN ASKED FOR ANYTHING AND NOBODY IN THAT HARBOR WAS PAID FOR ANYTHING ON THAT DAY.**

*First column, entered on Friday the third day of December at about half past nine in the evening in that shed: that the said day was not a rota day and was not a wage Tuesday and was not a wipe, and that no charge was placed on that cistern on it, and that there were nine hulls lying in a line about two sea miles off the head of that mole at about ten past six in the morning with two of them carrying a gunwale a plank could be landed on and seven of them not, and that they had not swung at the half past the ebb, and that the woman with the end stall and the slate stood on her own step at half past seven and there was nothing on that water while she was on it, and that the man of about fifty who owns a boat of about twenty-two tons was the first man in that harbor to see it and did not go and tell anybody until about eight o'clock; and that two people in that market gave two accounts of what it was on that morning that do not agree and neither of them was asked again; and that a sheet of paper carrying a seal at the foot of it and no figure of any kind on it was nailed to the board at the head of that mole at about a quarter past one in the afternoon by a man out of a boat's boat who said nothing while he did it, and that the light over that board is out and its lens has been out since the seventh of May and that about eleven people stood in front of that board in the dark between two and four in the afternoon and about eleven of them could not read what was on it; and that the man who keeps the leaf read it standing up about four times and did not take it off that board and did not copy it and put no line against it on any sheet in that yard, and that he said out loud that this yard is not answerable for one word of it and that there is no key to it in that harbor and that nobody is going to be given one, and that a man of about fifty-eight of the second lay asked one other person at that mole head whether it was about the boats and was told that the person he asked did not know; and that bread and salt beef for nine men for ten days at one thousand six hundred pence, being ten marks, and a day apiece in advance to the same four men at six hundred and seventy-two pence, being four marks, two shillings and eightpence, went onto the sheet that carries the money at about ten in the morning with the third of December against both of them, and that nothing went onto that sheet in any hand in any line on that day and nobody was asked for anything; and that one hundred and sixty-eight pence came into that yard and one hundred and sixty-eight pence went out of it, and that twenty-eight plus one hundred and sixty-eight less one hundred and sixty-eight is twenty-eight pence at the close of the said day, being two shillings and fourpence, being short of the wage of four marks by six hundred and twelve pence, being three marks eleven shillings; and that that sheet stood at thirty-four thousand eight hundred and twenty-two pence out and ten thousand six hundred and forty pence in and is twenty-four thousand one hundred and eighty-two pence short, being one hundred and fifty-one marks, one shilling and tenpence, and that the running out on it moved by two thousand two hundred and seventy-two pence on that day and nothing came in against it; and that the man who keeps the leaf said out loud once, in that yard's own mouth, that the four men were not losing a day because of anything they had done and that the figure in chalk had not moved and was not going to move that week, and that nobody thanked him for it.*

*Second column, not checkable from a mole, entered on the said Friday at about half past nine in the evening in that shed, and no name is against it: that the reason a man who owns a boat of about twenty-two tons stood out at that ring for three quarters of an hour with his hand on it before he went up to that shed is his own and not the yard's, and is that a man who owns a boat has to be the first one to be wrong in that harbor or he will spend the rest of his life being told he was not looking hard enough, and that this yard did not send anybody up that lay at six o'clock because nobody in this shed was going to be the man who went up it; and that the reason no word of that paper went onto any sheet in this yard is the man who keeps the leaf's own, and is that a sheet this yard keeps is a sheet this yard is answerable for, and that a paper nailed to a board out there is answerable to any man in that harbor who walks up and reads it and is answerable to this yard for nothing, and that a copy of it in a shed four hundred yards from that mole would be a thing one man could produce in a room and could not produce it in a room with about eleven people in it who cannot read it; and that the reason the two accounts in that market were both left standing is the yard's own mouth, and is that a line of boats put at two sea miles and a shortage of two boats of a supply look the same from a quay and are not the same thing, and that a harbor that agrees with itself about a thing it cannot see is a harbor that will agree with the office as well in about a fortnight; and that the reason the increase that is coming on that sheet is not carried inside another line is the man who keeps the leaf's own, and is that a figure a man can take off a page is worth more to a stranger than a figure that is correct and buried in another figure, and that nobody in this yard has ever been able to check what a victual cost and has been able to check what this yard paid for it; and that the reason no figure standing in that harbor moved on that Friday is the yard's own mouth, and is that a line of boats standing off a mole is not a creditor and does not become one by being nailed to a board, and that a day is paid out of a fund that pays days and not out of an old account, and that this yard did not touch one standing figure in that harbor on that day and has not touched one on any day of this week;  and that the reason the four men were paid at the figure in chalk on a Friday with nothing on the water is in that yard's own mouth, and is that the money for a day is in the bag on the morning of that day before anybody knows what the day is going to be, and that taking it back on the Friday would be taking back a man's Saturday.*
