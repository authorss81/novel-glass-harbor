# Chapter 512

*Tuesday the Fourteenth Day of December, the Sixtieth Wage Tuesday, the Wage Column in That Harbor Standing at Twenty-Six Lines and One Hundred and Four Marks With Twenty Envelopes in a Chest and Not Moving and a Further Week Standing Fallen Due at Its Close, Being the Sixtieth, and Nobody Paid a Penny Out of That Chest, a Supply Boat of About Two Hundred Tons Coming Inside the Line of Nine Hulls Off That Mole Head at About Half Past Ten in the Morning and Selling at a Figure This Yard Has Not Been Quoted Before and Higher Again, the Same Victual at Two Thousand Two Hundred and Forty Pence for Nine Men for Ten Days, Being Fourteen Marks, Written in Chalk on the Slate of a Woman With an End Stall and Not on Any Sheet, Because a Price Is a Price and Not a Cost, Coal for the Pump for Eight Days off That Supply Boat Going Onto the Sheet at About Two in the Afternoon at Two Thousand and Eighty Pence, Being Thirteen Marks, With the Fourteenth of December Against It, the Shortfall Said Out Loud at Thirty-Four Thousand and Fifty Pence, Being Two Hundred and Twelve Marks, Ten Shillings and Tenpence, the Bag at Twenty-Eight Pence, Nobody Thanked*

Tuesday the fourteenth day of December, in that harbor, in about four hundred yards of water and along a stone quay, and in a room at the back of that market and in a shed at the head of that third lay.

**The said Tuesday was not a rota day and was not a wipe, and no charge was placed on that cistern on it, and it was the sixtieth wage Tuesday. The wage column in that harbor stands at twenty-six lines and one hundred and four marks with twenty envelopes in a chest and did not move on that day, and nobody was paid a penny out of that chest, and a further week stands fallen due at its close, being the sixtieth, and a wage Tuesday is the day a harbor finds out what it owes and the finding out is not a payment. Coal for the pump for eight days off a supply boat of about two hundred tons went onto the sheet that carries the money at about two in the afternoon at two thousand and eighty pence, being thirteen marks, with the fourteenth of December against it, and nothing went into that sheet in any hand in any line on that day and nobody was asked for anything. One hundred and sixty-eight pence came into that yard at about ten in the morning and one hundred and sixty-eight pence went out of it at about four in the afternoon, and there are twenty-eight pence in the bag at the close of the said day, being two shillings and fourpence, and the figure that bag is short of the wage of four marks is six hundred and twelve pence, being three marks eleven shillings. That sheet stands at forty-four thousand six hundred and ninety pence out, being two hundred and seventy-nine marks, four shillings and twopence, and ten thousand six hundred and forty pence in, being sixty-six marks, six shillings and eightpence, and it is thirty-four thousand and fifty pence short, being two hundred and twelve marks, ten shillings and tenpence, and that figure is against that yard's own book and was said out loud in that yard's own mouth and was not answered by anybody. Nothing has been in a tin in that shed since the twenty-ninth day of April past, and no penny of the whole of that day came out of that bag, out of the owner of any boat, out of the man with two papers, or out of the Crown.**

**The nine that a stranger in that harbor could have read off any wall are the nine below, and not one of them was found by looking: the notice of the seventeenth of May past, two hundred and ten days; the bellwright of that harbor, not asked for two hundred and twelve; the two objects of nobody's on that crown, five hundred and fifty days; the bench under that pump, bare for the three hundred and sixteenth night; the two lights at about a mile off that point, one hundred and thirty-four days; the six-line sheet at the back of that end stall, its eighty-ninth day; the paper of four lines in Fish Street, its hundred and fourth; the nine-line sheet with a seal on it in that front street, its hundred and twenty-first; and a man of about fifty-one who has said nothing to anybody about a page of six names for a hundred and fifty-two days. There are nine hulls lying about two sea miles off the head of that mole and they have not swung since the morning of the third and the number of them has not changed and nobody in that harbor has counted them twice. There is no flour in that market and none is coming this month. The wage column in that harbor is twenty-six lines and one hundred and four marks with twenty envelopes in a chest. A man of about twenty-nine who came home on that boat can hear out of the left side of his head, and nobody in that harbor has made it into anything and no figure is set against it anywhere.**

---

**At about ten in the morning a clerk of the harbor authority came over the west basin and read the column out on the step at the back of that market, and about forty people were on the boards in front of that step and about twenty of them cannot read the column on the lid.**

He read it as it stands and he read the number of weeks that stand fallen due at the foot of it, being the sixtieth, and he said the day of the week and the date, and he put his own name at the foot of the reading and the hour, being ten o'clock in the morning, and he said that a wage Tuesday was the day a harbor found out what it owed and that nobody was paid on that step and that the finding out was not a payment.

The chest with the twenty envelopes in it is in a room at the back of that market and the lid of it is shut and the key of it is on a nail behind the desk of the man whose trade it is, and it was shut at ten o'clock and it was shut at four o'clock and it will be shut at ten o'clock next Tuesday, and nobody in that harbor has opened that chest this year and nobody in that harbor has been paid out of it this year.

**A woman at an end stall said one thing out loud to nobody in particular, in the way she says a price, and she said it on the third and she said it on this day and both times it has been the same.**

"That is what it looks like when two boats of a supply do not come in, and it is what it looks like on a Tuesday as well as on a Friday."

The man of about thirty-three who hires hands at that market for whoever will pay him said the other thing to her at about half past ten, and he was not laughing, and he has not said it since and he is not going to be asked about it again.

"That is a line put where it can be seen. A shortage arrives at your own gate by itself and a mark does not need boats, though I will grant you the boats are cheaper to keep than an argument is."

---

**And at about half past ten in the morning the same water that a supply boat has been running since before the third of December put a boat of about two hundred tons inside the line, and nobody in that harbor stopped her and nobody in that harbor could have.**

She was not the boat that came in on the fourth. She was longer by about nine feet and she had a new stern and her master was a different man and she came in on the last of the flood and lay against the north quay at about eleven with her moorings on in about four minutes, and the nine hulls about two sea miles off that mole head lay where they had lain and did not turn a board.

About nine people in that harbor watched her come through and about nine people counted her out loud and nobody in that harbor has a figure of how many of that kind have come in this winter, and nobody asked for one.

---

**And at about a quarter past eleven in the morning the man who keeps the leaf went down to that end stall with a piece of chalk and asked the woman whose stall it is to let him have the back of that slate for a minute, and she let him have it, and he wrote a figure on it in chalk and stood it back up on her step where anybody in that market could read it.**

The figure is a price and not a cost.

**BREAD AND SALT BEEF — NINE MEN — TEN DAYS — 2,240 — FOURTEEN MARKS.**

He stood there and said the whole of it out loud twice, once for himself and once for anybody in that market who wanted it.

"That is what she is selling it at and that is a price. This yard bought the same article on Monday for one thousand nine hundred and twenty pence and this is three hundred and twenty more than Monday and six hundred and forty more than the third of December, and it is not going on that sheet because a price is a price and not a cost. **A sheet that carries the money is a sheet of what this yard paid. If a man chalks up what a market is charging and calls it a cost, then in four weeks that sheet will be a page of what other people thought things were worth, and a stranger with a pencil could not take one figure off it and know what it meant.**"

The woman with that end stall said, without turning round, that four hundred and fifty pence was still on the front of that board with February's figure visible above it and both dates on it, and that it had been on that board since the twenty-ninth day of August, and the man who keeps the leaf said he knew it was and that he had not come to take it down and was not going to.

He left the chalk on the step and the figure standing on it and the woman did not wipe it off and it was still standing there on the twentieth day of December and it was still standing there on the twenty-second.

---

**And at about two in the afternoon he said the whole of the coal line out loud before he wrote a figure against it, and nobody argued with him about any part of it, and the woman of about twenty-seven of that yard was standing at the bench stone while he said it.**

"Coal for the pump, eight days, off that boat that came in this morning. Two thousand and eighty pence, being thirteen marks, with the fourteenth of December against it, being the day I agreed what it would cost and the day it goes into the total at the foot of that sheet, and those are the same day this once. That is not the coal the man on the reef told us about at the end of the month in November and it is not on that sheet at a price anybody said out loud on a deck, and it is what this yard pays for eight days off a boat that was alongside this quay at eleven o'clock this morning."

He wrote it and put the pen down flat, and the man who keeps the leaf's wage is four marks and there are twenty-eight pence in the bag and no figure of any kind has been asked of any person in that harbor to make the rest of that sheet otherwise.

---

**And at about half past five in the afternoon he said the shortfall out loud, and he said what the day had been, because a wage Tuesday is a day a harbor is told a number and not a day it is paid one.**

"Thirty-four thousand and fifty pence short, being two hundred and twelve marks, ten shillings and tenpence. It moved this afternoon by two thousand and eighty pence and that is the coal. The column in the room at the back of that market did not move and nobody was paid a penny out of that chest and the sixtieth week stands fallen due at the close of this day, and neither of those two numbers has been on that sheet this year and neither of them is going to be."

Nobody in that harbor was asked for the rest of it. Nobody was asked whether they would carry victual from that quay at the figure in chalk, and the four men who are paid a day apiece were paid a day apiece at four o'clock with the water as flat as it has been all week, and nobody thanked anybody on that day.

---

**THE SAME WATER CAME IN TWICE INSIDE A LINE OF NINE HULLS AND WENT OUT AGAIN AND TOOK A BOAT OF ABOUT TWO HUNDRED TONS IN WITH HER AND A CHEST THAT TAIL IS WORTH MORE THAN THE FIGURE CHALKED ON A SLATE AT AN END STALL. THERE ARE TWENTY ENVELOPES IN A CHEST IN A ROOM AT THE BACK OF THAT MARKET AND THE LID OF IT WAS SHUT AT TEN O'CLOCK AND SHUT AT FOUR AND THE KEY OF IT IS ON A NAIL BEHIND A DESK. FOUR HUNDRED AND FIFTY PENCE IS STILL ON THE FRONT OF THAT BOARD AND FEBRUARY'S FIGURE IS VISIBLE ABOVE IT AND BOTH DATES ARE ON IT AND NOBODY TOOK EITHER DOWN. THERE IS NO FLOUR IN THAT MARKET. A BERTH FUND PAID ONE HUNDRED AND SIXTY-EIGHT PENCE INTO A YARD THAT MORNING AND ONE HUNDRED AND SIXTY-EIGHT PENCE OUT OF IT AT FOUR AND THE BAG UNDER THE SLATE IS TWENTY-EIGHT PENCE. THE SHEET THAT CARRIES THE MONEY IS THIRTY-FOUR THOUSAND AND FIFTY PENCE SHORT AGAINST THAT YARD'S OWN BOOK.**

---

*First column, entered on Tuesday the fourteenth day of December at about half past nine in the evening in that shed: that the said day was not a rota day and was not a wipe, and that no charge was placed on that cistern on it, and that it was the sixtieth wage Tuesday, and that the wage column in that harbor stood at twenty-six lines and one hundred and four marks with twenty envelopes in a chest and did not move and that nobody was paid a penny out of that chest and that a further week stands fallen due at its close, being the sixtieth, and that the column was read out on the step at the back of that market at ten o'clock in the morning by a clerk of the harbor authority with his own name and the hour at the foot of the reading in front of about forty people of whom about twenty cannot read the column on the lid; and that a supply boat of about two hundred tons, and not the boat that came in on the fourth, came inside the line of nine hulls off that mole head on the last of the flood and was moored against the north quay at about eleven and was not stopped by anybody in that harbor; and that the same victual was offered in that market on that day at two thousand two hundred and forty pence for nine men for ten days, being fourteen marks, being six hundred and forty pence more than the third of December, and that the man who keeps the leaf wrote that figure in chalk on the slate at an end stall at about a quarter past eleven in the morning and it went on no sheet in that yard because a price is a price and not a cost; and that coal for the pump for eight days off that supply boat went onto the sheet that carries the money at about two in the afternoon at two thousand and eighty pence, being thirteen marks, with the fourteenth of December against it, and that the man who keeps the leaf said the whole of that line out loud before he wrote a figure against it and nobody argued with him about any part of it; and that nothing went onto that sheet in any hand in any line on that day and nobody was asked for anything, and that one hundred and sixty-eight pence came into that yard and one hundred and sixty-eight pence went out of it, and that twenty-eight plus one hundred and sixty-eight less one hundred and sixty-eight is twenty-eight pence at the close of the said day, being two shillings and fourpence, being short of the wage of four marks by six hundred and twelve pence, being three marks eleven shillings; and that that sheet stood at forty-four thousand six hundred and ninety pence out and ten thousand six hundred and forty pence in and is thirty-four thousand and fifty pence short, being two hundred and twelve marks, ten shillings and tenpence; and that the two accounts given in that market of what a line of boats is were given again on that day in the same words as on the third and neither has been agreed with and neither has been taken back; and that the third column is empty and nobody has been appointed to it and the fourth column stands at one hundred and twenty-five and took no line; and that the four columns at the close of that day stand at two hundred and forty-five, one thousand and ninety-nine, none and one hundred and twenty-five, and at the hour of that day's reading at two hundred and forty-four, one thousand and ninety-seven.*

*Second column, not checkable from a mole, entered on the said Tuesday at about half past nine in the evening in that shed, and no name is against it: that the reason a figure a market is charging was put in chalk on a slate and not on the sheet that carries the money is the man who keeps the leaf's own, and is that there are two numbers in that market on that day and only one of them is a fact about this yard, and that a page which carried both would be a page a stranger could not read, because a stranger would be adding a price to a cost and getting a number that is neither; and that the reason he asked a woman with an end stall for the back of her slate instead of a piece of paper is his own, and is that a price in a market belongs on a thing in that market, and that a man who carried a price back to his own shed and put it on a wall there would be telling that harbor what it ought to be charging, and that is not a thing a yard does; and that the reason the coal went on that sheet at the figure this yard agreed with a master alongside that quay and not at a figure a man said out loud on a deck in November is this yard's own mouth, and is that a deck is a place a man says things in and a quay is a place a man is handed things on, and that a page that takes its coal off a deck is a page about the winter instead of a page about the pump; and that the reason the wage column was not touched on that Tuesday, and not mentioned in any line on that sheet, is this yard's own mouth, and is that a column of twenty-six lines and a chest of twenty envelopes are a matter of that harbor and its clerk and its office on the west basin, and that a yard that opened that column on a Tuesday would be a yard that had started doing the harbor's counting instead of its own; and that the reason nothing in that harbor was settled, shortened, rubbed out or put behind a door on that Tuesday is this yard's own mouth, and is that a wage Tuesday is the day the chest is looked at and shut again, and that a harbor which pays anything on a Tuesday pays it out of the chest and not out of a yard, and that this yard moved no old figure on that day and would have been ashamed of moving one for the sake of a chalk mark on somebody else's step.*
