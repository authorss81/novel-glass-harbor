# Chapter 506

*Wednesday the Eighth Day of December, Not a Rota Day and Not a Wage Tuesday and Not a Wipe, the Yard at the Head of That Third Lay Building a Buoy, a Bell and a Line Between About Seven in the Morning and About One in the Afternoon, the Man Who Keeps the Leaf Saying the Whole of That Line Out Loud Before Any Figure Is Written Against It and Nobody Arguing With Him About Any Part of It, the Bellwright of That Harbor Not Asked for a Figure and Giving None and His Chest Shut and the Eight Marks and a Day of a Boat Unargued, One Descent Into About Four Fathoms Off That Third Lay at About Ten Past Two in the Afternoon on That Ebb with a Slate Written Before Anybody Was Off the Boat Carrying the Reason in the Diver's Own Words and the Hour Against It and a Figure of Twenty-Five Minutes on It, a Named Tender on the Surface and Not in the Water and an Independent Line and a Cutoff He Could Pull Without Asking, Down at Ten Past Two and on the Bottom at About a Quarter Past Two and the Word at About Half Past Two and Out of That Water at About Thirty-Five Minutes Past Two, the Second Pair of Hands' Two Days Written Into That Sheet on That Day With the Sixth of December Against It at Three Hundred and Thirty-Six Pence, the Shortfall Said Out Loud at Twenty-Eight Thousand and Sixty-Six Pence, Being One Hundred and Seventy-Five Marks, Five Shillings and Sixpence, the Bag at Twenty-Eight Pence, Nobody Thanked*

Wednesday the eighth day of December, in that harbor, in about four hundred yards of water and along a stone quay, and in about four fathoms of water off the head of that third lay.

**The said Wednesday was not a rota day and was not a wage Tuesday and was not a wipe, and no charge was placed on that cistern on it. A buoy, a bell and a line went onto the sheet that carries the money at about one in the afternoon at six hundred and forty pence, being four marks, with the seventh of December against it, and a second pair of hands for two days, being three hundred and thirty-six pence, being two marks, one shilling and fourpence, was written into the total at the foot of that sheet on that day with the sixth of December against it, being the day those two men were asked for. Nothing went into that sheet in any hand in any line on that day and nobody was asked for anything. One hundred and sixty-eight pence came into that yard at about ten in the morning and one hundred and sixty-eight pence went out of it at about four in the afternoon, and there are twenty-eight pence in the bag at the close of the said day, being two shillings and fourpence, and the figure that bag is short of the wage of four marks is six hundred and twelve pence, being three marks eleven shillings. That sheet stands at thirty-eight thousand seven hundred and six pence out, being two hundred and forty-one marks, twelve shillings and two pence, and ten thousand six hundred and forty pence in, being sixty-six marks, six shillings and eightpence, and it is twenty-eight thousand and sixty-six pence short, being one hundred and seventy-five marks, five shillings and sixpence, and that figure is against that yard's own book and was said out loud in that yard's own mouth and was not answered by anybody. Nothing has been in a tin in that shed since the twenty-ninth day of April past, and no penny of the whole of that trip came out of that bag, out of the owner of any boat, out of the man with two papers, or out of the Crown.**

**The nine that a stranger in that harbor could have read off any wall are the nine below, and not one of them was found by looking: the notice of the seventeenth of May past, two hundred and four days; the bellwright of that harbor, not asked for two hundred and six; the two objects of nobody's on that crown, five hundred and forty-four days; the bench under that pump, bare for the three hundred and tenth night; the two lights at about a mile off that point, one hundred and twenty-eight days; the six-line sheet at the back of that end stall, its eighty-third day; the paper of four lines in Fish Street, its ninety-eighth; the nine-line sheet with a seal on it in that front street, its hundred and fifteenth; and a man of about fifty-one who has said nothing to anybody about a page of six names for a hundred and forty-six days. The hose in that harbor is dead and will be dead until the spring. There is no physic at fourteen pence in that harbor and there is none of it. A man of about twenty-nine who came home on that boat can hear out of the left side of his head, and it has been four days and nobody has made it into anything and no figure is set against it anywhere.**

---

**The yard built it between about seven in the morning and about one in the afternoon, and about nine people were in and about it at one time or another, and nobody was paid for it out of any bag and nobody was thanked for it at any hour of it.**

The float is of tarred fir, about five feet across and shallow underneath, with a ring of old chain round its own waist and a leaded stem through the middle of it. The bell is a cast thing about nine inches across the mouth and it is not the bellwright's and that yard was not asked for a figure and did not give one, and his chest is shut, and the eight marks and a day of a boat that stand against him in that harbor are unargued and were not argued on that day. The frame above the float is four sticks of sawn fir with a board across the top of them, and the bell hangs under that board on a gudgeon, and the line is about forty fathoms of two-inch stuff with a shackle at the head of it and a sinker at the tail.

"Put the water in it now," Nessa Pike said. "Not oil. Water. A bell wants water round it and it will talk to a man in a boat for a year and it will talk to a man in the water for half of that and there is no use in the other half."

---

**At about one in the afternoon the man who keeps the leaf said the whole of that line out loud before he wrote a figure against it, and nobody argued with him about any part of it, and what he said was not about a bell.**

"A buoy, a bell and a line. Six hundred and forty pence, being four marks, with the seventh of December against it, being the day the two men started on it and the day I agreed what it would cost. That figure is timber, chain, lead, two-inch stuff, a cast bell out of a foundry on the far coast, and a boy's two days of wages carrying the float down the slip, and I have not put a figure of mine on any part of it, and I am not going to put my hours on it this month when I cannot pay myself out of it."

"And the second pair of hands."

"Those two days go in the total tonight, being the sixth against them, being the day they were asked for and the first day they worked. I told you on Monday that the line would go in when the days were in and they are in, and you can take the sixth off that line and the day it was written off that line and they are two different days and both of them are on that sheet."

He wrote both lines and put the pen down and the buoy was on a handcart at the head of that slip by half past two with forty fathoms of line coiled on the tail of it and about nine people standing round it and not one of them saying anything about the fact that it was going out into four fathoms of water with a line of boats standing about two sea miles off the mole head.

---

**And at about ten past two in the afternoon a slate was written before anybody was off the boat, and it was written on the man who was going down and not by him, and the air limit on it was a figure of twenty-five minutes, and the reason on it was in his own words because he had said them out loud on the quay at about one o'clock and the man who keeps the leaf wrote them down as they came out of him.**

*To hang the frame and the shackle on the stone and to take the slack out of the line so it does not lie across the channel. The stone is the one the herring boats used to lie on and it has a ring in it. Twenty-five minutes. If I do not say anything for two minutes, pull it.*

Ivo Sark, of about twenty-four, one of the master's two men, was the tender on the surface for the whole of it, in a dinghy about nine feet off that slip, with his own hand on the chain weight and a cutoff he could pull without asking anybody's leave, and he was not in that water at any hour of that afternoon. The pump hand of about forty-four was on the compressor with him. The line was the buoy's own line and there was a second line on the surface going down with him that had nothing to do with the buoy.

**Calder Marr went down at about ten past two in the afternoon on that ebb and put his hand flat on the stone with the ring in it and found the ring and put the shackle through it and took the slack out of forty fathoms of line until it came to hand, and nobody went into that water twice on that day and no two men were in it at once at any hour and nobody went in at the top of a flood.**

He was in the water about six minutes before he was on the bottom and about twenty-two of his twenty-five on it. At about half past two the tender said out loud over the wire that the man on the bottom had not said anything for two minutes and the cutoff was pulled without anybody asking, and the word came about four seconds after it was called for and was not asked for twice.

He was out of that water at about thirty-five minutes past two, being twenty-five minutes and five seconds from the time his helmet went over the rail to the time his helmet came off, and the clock closes at every figure in it, and nobody went in again that day.

---

**And at about half past five in the afternoon the man who keeps the leaf said the shortfall out loud, and then he said the one thing he said that week about what a buoy is, and he said it once, and there were about nine people on that slip and none of them repeated it.**

"Twenty-eight thousand and sixty-six pence short, being one hundred and seventy-five marks, five shillings and sixpence. It moved this morning by three hundred and thirty-six pence and this afternoon by six hundred and forty, and nothing has come into that sheet in six days and nobody in this harbor has been asked for a penny of it."

He put the pen down and looked at the float, which was lying on the water about four fathoms off the head of that slip with the bell under its board and the line going down and away to the stone.

"**A buoy is a thing that is in the wrong place until somebody stands on that quay and says it is in the right one, and that is a thing nine people can do, and that is also a thing nine people can stop doing.**"

Nobody thanked him. Nobody thanked the man in the water or the man in the dinghy or the two men on the slip with the oakum iron. The boy who carried the float down is not on the water and his surname is on no page of that harbor and nobody in that harbor has asked him about it this week.

---

**A BUOY OF TARRED FIR ABOUT FIVE FEET ACROSS WITH A RING OF CHAIN ROUND ITS OWN WAIST IS LYING IN ABOUT FOUR FATHOMS OFF THE HEAD OF THAT THIRD LAY WITH A CAST BELL UNDER A BOARD FOUR STICKS HIGH AND FORTY FATHOMS OF TWO-INCH STUFF RUNNING DOWN AND AWAY TO A STONE WITH A RING IN IT, AND A BOY CARRIED IT DOWN THE SLIP AND IS NOT ON THE WATER. THE BELLWRIGHT OF THAT HARBOR WAS NOT ASKED FOR A FIGURE AND GAVE NONE AND HIS CHEST IS SHUT. A SLATE WITH A FIGURE OF TWENTY-FIVE MINUTES ON IT AND A REASON IN ONE MAN'S OWN WORDS WAS ON THAT BOAT BEFORE ANYBODY WENT OFF IT, AND THE TENDER WAS ON THE SURFACE AND NOT IN THE WATER, AND THE CUTOFF WAS PULLED AT ABOUT HALF PAST TWO WITHOUT ANYBODY ASKING, AND THE MAN CAME OUT AT ABOUT THIRTY-FIVE MINUTES PAST TWO. THAT SHEET IS TWENTY-EIGHT THOUSAND AND SIXTY-SIX PENCE SHORT AGAINST THAT YARD'S OWN BOOK AND NO FIGURE HAS BEEN ASKED OF ANY PERSON IN THAT HARBOR TO MAKE IT OTHERWISE.**

*First column, entered on Wednesday the eighth day of December at about half past nine in the evening in that shed: that the said day was not a rota day and was not a wage Tuesday and was not a wipe, and that no charge was placed on that cistern on it; and that a buoy of tarred fir about five feet across with a ring of chain round its waist, a cast bell about nine inches across the mouth under a board on four sticks, and about forty fathoms of two-inch stuff with a shackle at the head and a sinker at the tail, were built in that yard between about seven in the morning and about one in the afternoon by about nine people; and that the bellwright of that harbor was not asked for a figure on that day and gave none and his chest is shut and the eight marks and a day of a boat that stand against him were not argued; and that the buoy, the bell and the line went onto the sheet that carries the money at about one in the afternoon at six hundred and forty pence, being four marks, with the seventh of December against it, and that the man who keeps the leaf said the whole of that line out loud before he wrote a figure against it and would not put his own hours on it, and that a second pair of hands for two days at three hundred and thirty-six pence, being two marks, one shilling and fourpence, was written into the total at the foot of that sheet on that day with the sixth of December against it; and that nothing went onto that sheet in any hand in any line on that day and nobody was asked for anything, and that one hundred and sixty-eight pence came into that yard and one hundred and sixty-eight pence went out of it, and that twenty-eight plus one hundred and sixty-eight less one hundred and sixty-eight is twenty-eight pence at the close of the said day, being two shillings and fourpence, being short of the wage of four marks by six hundred and twelve pence, being three marks eleven shillings; and that that sheet stood at thirty-eight thousand seven hundred and six pence out and ten thousand six hundred and forty pence in and is twenty-eight thousand and sixty-six pence short, being one hundred and seventy-five marks, five shillings and sixpence, and that the running out on it moved by nine hundred and seventy-six pence on that day and nothing came in against it; and that one man went down into about four fathoms off that third lay at about ten past two in the afternoon on that ebb, on a slate written before anybody was off that boat carrying a figure of twenty-five minutes and the reason in his own words and the hour against it, with a named tender on the surface and not in the water, an independent line, and a cutoff the tender could pull without asking, was about six minutes in the water before he was on the bottom and about twenty-two of his twenty-five minutes on it, the tender said out loud at about half past two that he had not answered for two minutes, the cutoff was pulled without anybody asking, the word came about four seconds after it was called for and was not asked for twice, and he was out of that water at about thirty-five minutes past two, and that nobody went into that water twice on that day and no two men were in it at once at any hour and nobody went in at the top of a flood; and that the man who keeps the leaf said out loud once, in that yard's own mouth and in front of about nine people, that a buoy is a thing that is in the wrong place until somebody stands on that quay and says it is in the right one, and that that is a thing nine people can do and also a thing nine people can stop doing, and that nobody thanked him.*

*Second column, not checkable from a mole, entered on the said Wednesday at about half past nine in the evening in that shed, and no name is against it: that the reason the bell was filled with water and not with oil is the woman of about twenty-seven's own, and is that oil is a thing a bell can be got to do once and water is a thing a bell does to anything that is wet, and that this harbor has no barrel of oil in it since the twenty-ninth of August and is not going to be given one by anything that is standing outside that mole; and that the reason the eighth of December carried one descent and not two is the yard's own mouth, and is that a thing being set for the first time is a thing that wants a man who has set one before and a man who has not, and that two men on a four-fathom stone on the same afternoon is a thing a harbor does for a wreck and not a thing a harbor does for a mark; and that the reason the bellwright of that harbor was not asked for a figure is this yard's own and it is not new, and is that eight marks and a day of a boat have stood unargued in that harbor for longer than that buoy has been in the water, and that a man who has not been asked is a man who can still be asked and a man who has been asked twice cannot; and that the reason the man who keeps the leaf said the thing about a buoy out loud on the quay and wrote nothing against it is his own, and is that it is the shape of the whole of the work and it is checkable by nobody and it is true on about the ninth of every month when the bell is rung, and that a thing worth saying once and worth writing down at two in the morning is not one this harbor has ever been able to get on a page.*
