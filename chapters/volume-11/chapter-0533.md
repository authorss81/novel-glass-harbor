# Chapter 533

*Tuesday the Fourth Day of January, the Sixty-Third Wage Tuesday, the Wage Column in That Harbor Standing at Twenty-Six Lines and One Hundred and Four Marks With Twenty Envelopes in a Chest and Read Out on the Step at the Back of That Market at Ten o'Clock in the Morning in Front of About Forty People of Whom About Twenty Cannot Read the Column on the Lid and Not Moving, Nobody Paid a Penny Out of That Chest, a Further Week Standing Fallen Due at the Close of That Day Being the Sixty-Third, Not a Rota Day and Not a Wipe, a Shipyard on That Quay Priced in Hours and Material at About Half Past Eleven in the Morning in Front of About Nine People at Fifteen Thousand Three Hundred and Twenty Pence, Being Ninety-Five Marks and Ten Shillings, and Nobody Agreeing to Any Part of It and Nothing Going onto That Sheet and Nothing Going into It, the Person Whose Yard It Is Saying Out Loud That a Figure a Yard Is Standing In Is Not a Bill and Is Not Going on That Sheet Today and Is Not Thanked, the Shortfall Said Out Loud at About Half Past Five Unmoved at Forty-Nine Thousand Eight Hundred and Fifty-Eight Pence, Being Three Hundred and Eleven Marks, Eight Shillings and Twopence, the Four Heads Not Changed and the Fitting-Out Sheet on No Wall in Any Room, the Bag at Twenty-Eight Pence, Being Two Shillings and Fourpence*

Tuesday the fourth day of January, in that harbor, in about four hundred yards of water and along a stone quay, and in a shipyard two hundred yards from that bench stone.

**The said Tuesday was not a rota day and was not a wipe and no charge was placed on that cistern on it, and it was the sixty-third wage Tuesday. The wage column in that harbor stands at twenty-six lines and one hundred and four marks with twenty envelopes in a chest and did not move on that day, and nobody was paid a penny out of that chest, and a further week stands fallen due at its close, being the sixty-third. Nothing went out on any line of the sheet that carries the money in any hand on that day and nothing went into it in any hand, and nobody was asked for anything. One hundred and sixty-eight pence came into that yard at about ten in the morning and one hundred and sixty-eight pence went out of it at about four in the afternoon, and there are twenty-eight pence in the bag at the close of the said day, being two shillings and fourpence, and the figure that bag is short of the wage of four marks is six hundred and twelve pence, being three marks eleven shillings. That sheet stands at sixty thousand four hundred and ninety-eight pence out, being three hundred and seventy-eight marks, one shilling and sixpence, and ten thousand six hundred and forty pence in, being sixty-six marks, six shillings and eightpence, and it is forty-nine thousand eight hundred and fifty-eight pence short, being three hundred and eleven marks, eight shillings and twopence, and that figure is against that yard's own book and was said out loud in that yard's own mouth and was not answered by anybody. Nothing has been in a tin in that shed since the twenty-ninth day of April past, and no penny of the whole of that day came out of that bag, out of the owner of any boat, out of the man with two papers, or out of the Crown.**

**The nine that a stranger in that harbor could have read off any wall are the nine below, and not one of them was found by looking: the notice of the seventeenth of May past, two hundred and thirty-one days; the bellwright of that harbor, not asked for two hundred and thirty-three; the two objects of nobody's on that crown, five hundred and seventy-one days; the bench under that pump, bare for the three hundred and thirty-seventh night; the two lights at about a mile off that point, one hundred and fifty-five days; the six-line sheet at the back of that end stall, its hundred and tenth day; the paper of four lines in Fish Street, its hundred and twenty-fifth; the nine-line sheet with a seal on it in that front street, its hundred and forty-second; and a man of about fifty-one who has said nothing to anybody about a page of six names for a hundred and seventy-three days. A mould of a trawlman's shot of about nineteen pounds to the mould stands on a shelf in that market and has not been used since the middle of September and nobody in that market has asked for it back. A boat's boat came inside that mole on the second of this month with a coil of new line in it and nobody in that harbor bought a fathom of it and it went out again on the same tide. A man of about twenty-nine who came home on that boat can hear out of the left side of his head and it has been thirty-one days and this yard has not had him in it since the twenty-ninth of December and nobody has sent for him and he has not come.**

---

**At about ten o'clock in the morning a clerk of that harbor authority read that column out on the step at the back of that market, and about forty people were on the boards in front of that step and about twenty of them cannot read the column on the lid, and he put his own name at the foot of the reading and the hour, and the chest with the twenty envelopes in it stood shut behind that door from ten to four and was not opened between, and the sixty-third week stands fallen due at the close of that day.**

Nobody in that market stopped the clerk at a line. A man of about forty-six who sells ice out of a barrow at the front of that market asked him, out loud and in front of everybody, whether the column had moved since November, and the clerk said that it had not, and the man said *November* in the same tone twice and went back to his barrow.

---

**And at about half past eleven in the morning, in that shipyard on that quay, about nine people were standing in it, and the four men who had come back up that coast road asked Nessa Pike, of about twenty-seven, what a new roof on that shed would come to, and she priced it, and she priced it in hours and material and on the back of a slate, and nobody agreed to any part of it.**

They asked because one of them had said in that yard on the Sunday night that a roof is the first thing a yard loses and the last thing anybody can argue about, and she had heard him say it and had not answered him. That is the whole of why they asked her and they asked her because she is the only person in that harbor anybody has ever asked what a thing costs before anybody agrees to it.

She did not answer them for about a minute. She walked out of the door of that shed, looked up at the underside of the roof from the slates, came back in, and wrote on the back of a slate in chalk, and she said every figure out loud as she wrote it.

"Right. **Forty fathoms of three-inch fir for the rafters and the purlins and the boarding dressed to it. Two hundred and forty pence a fathom, which is the figure I paid at the mill that goes off the coast road before the burn and not the figure I would like to pay at.** That is nine thousand six hundred pence, being sixty marks exactly."

"**The bench. Three days of one man's hours on it, and a man on this yard's own hours is a hundred and sixty-eight pence a day off the berth fund, being the figure in chalk.** That is five hundred and four pence, being three marks and two shillings."

"**The stock. Sixteen fathoms of two-inch for this yard to keep working off, at two hundred pence a fathom, being three thousand two hundred pence, being twenty marks exactly.** That is not the roof. That is the next six months of this yard having timber in it, and it is on the same slate because I put it on the same slate."

"And **three days apiece in advance to the same four men that have been working this yard since the third of December, at the figure in chalk, being two thousand and sixteen pence, being twelve marks and eight shillings.** Those four men are not on that sheet and are not counted on it and their names are in the book the days are paid out of, and I know that because the man who keeps the leaf has said it out loud in that shed in front of nine men twice this month."

She put the chalk down and put the four figures together out loud, and the sum of them closed.

"That is **fifteen thousand three hundred and twenty pence, being ninety-five marks and ten shillings, and that is what a roof on that shed comes to this week, and I have priced it in hours and material and I have not put a penny of contingency on it because a man who puts a tenth on a slate for a roof is a man who does not want to be told the roof cost what it cost.**"

A man of about thirty-three who hires hands, who had come over from the west quay and had been listening at the door, asked her the question that the four of them had not thought to ask.

"Who is paying that."

She looked at him for about two seconds.

"Nobody. That is the answer and you all know it is the answer and you asked me anyway because a man who puts a number on a slate is easier to argue with than a man who does not. **That is a figure for a yard that is standing with a roof on it. It is not a bill, because a bill is money somebody has decided to spend, and no one in this harbor has decided to spend anything this morning and I am not going to sell a roof to a man who does not need one.**"

And then she said the other half of it, once, and did not say it again that month.

"And if it is ever a bill it goes on that sheet with the day the work was done against it and in the total at the foot of that sheet on the day it is settled, and a stranger with a leaf and a pencil can take the days and the figures off it and find that they agree. **The day somebody agrees to that is the day this slate comes off this bench, and not one minute before.**"

Nobody agreed to it. Nobody put a hand on the slate. That slate stayed on that bench stone for the rest of the week with fifteen thousand three hundred and twenty pence on it and not one of them on any sheet in this harbor, and it did not go at the back of that shed, and it came off that bench stone on the Saturday and went into the drawer under it, and that drawer has never been locked in this yard and is not going to be.

Nobody in that harbor was thanked for anything on that Tuesday and the woman whose yard it is was not thanked.

---

**And at about half past five in the afternoon the man who keeps the leaf said the shortfall out loud at that bench stone with the door open on the slip, and said that the day had not touched it, and nobody in that harbor was asked for the rest of it.**

"Forty-nine thousand eight hundred and fifty-eight pence short, being three hundred and eleven marks, eight shillings and twopence. Unmoved since ten o'clock on Monday morning and unmoved at half past five this afternoon, and that is right, because a number said out loud in a shipyard is not a cost. **There are fifteen thousand three hundred and twenty pence on the back of a slate two hundred yards from where I am standing and not one of them is on that sheet, and they will not be on that sheet until somebody has done the work and somebody has settled it, and if either of those two things does not happen then the correct place for that figure is the back of that shed.**"

"And this afternoon the sixty-third wage Tuesday went past in this harbor. Twenty-six lines and one hundred and four marks, twenty envelopes in a chest, and a week standing fallen due at the close of today, and the week before that one fallen due since the twenty-eighth, and nobody in that market read one of those envelopes out loud this afternoon because they are not read out. Nobody in this harbor was asked for a penny of the rest of it and nobody was thanked."

---

**A COLUMN OF WAGES WAS READ OUT ON THE STEP AT THE BACK OF THAT MARKET AT TEN O'CLOCK IN THE MORNING BY A CLERK WITH HIS OWN NAME AND THE HOUR AT THE FOOT OF IT IN FRONT OF ABOUT FORTY PEOPLE AND ABOUT TWENTY OF THEM CANNOT READ THE COLUMN ON THE LID, AND IT DID NOT MOVE AND NOBODY WAS PAID A PENNY OUT OF THE CHEST AND THE SIXTY-THIRD WEEK STANDS FALLEN DUE. FOUR MEN ASKED THE PERSON WHOSE YARD IT IS WHAT A NEW ROOF ON IT WOULD COME TO AND SHE PRICED IT AT ABOUT HALF PAST ELEVEN IN HOURS AND MATERIAL ON THE BACK OF A SLATE AND THE FOUR FIGURES AND THE SUM OF THEM ARE FIFTEEN THOUSAND THREE HUNDRED AND TWENTY PENCE, BEING NINETY-FIVE MARKS AND TEN SHILLINGS, AND NOBODY AGREED TO ANY PART OF IT AND THE SHEET THAT CARRIES THE MONEY IS ON NO WALL IN ANY ROOM AND NOTHING WENT ONTO IT THAT DAY. A MAN OF ABOUT FORTY-SIX WHO SELLS ICE OUT OF A BARROW ASKED OUT LOUD WHETHER THE COLUMN HAD MOVED SINCE NOVEMBER AND WAS TOLD THAT IT HAD NOT AND SAID NOVEMBER TWICE. THE FOUR HEADS ON THAT WALL DID NOT CHANGE AND NO FIFTH HEAD WENT ON IT. THE SHEET THAT CARRIES THE MONEY IS FORTY-NINE THOUSAND EIGHT HUNDRED AND FIFTY-EIGHT PENCE SHORT AGAINST THAT YARD'S OWN BOOK AND THERE ARE TWENTY-EIGHT PENCE IN THE BAG UNDER THE SLATE.**

*First column, entered on Tuesday the fourth day of January at about half past nine in the evening in that shed: that the said day was not a rota day and was not a wipe, and that no charge was placed on that cistern on it, and that it was the sixty-third wage Tuesday, and that the wage column in that harbor stood at twenty-six lines and one hundred and four marks with twenty envelopes in a chest and did not move and that nobody was paid a penny out of that chest and that a further week stands fallen due at the close of that day, being the sixty-third, and that the column was read out on the step at the back of that market at about ten o'clock in the morning by a clerk of that harbor authority with his own name and the hour at the foot of the reading in front of about forty people of whom about twenty cannot read the column on the lid, and that nobody stopped the clerk at a line and that a man of about forty-six who sells ice out of a barrow asked him out loud whether the column had moved since November and was told that it had not; and that four of the men of that yard asked the woman whose yard it is, of about twenty-seven, what a new roof on that shed would come to, and that she priced it at about half past eleven in the morning in about nine people in hours and material on the back of a slate, being forty fathoms of three-inch fir at two hundred and forty pence a fathom, being nine thousand six hundred pence, being sixty marks exactly; three days of one man's hours on a bench at one hundred and sixty-eight pence a day, being five hundred and four pence, being three marks and two shillings; sixteen fathoms of two-inch for that yard's own stock at two hundred pence a fathom, being three thousand two hundred pence, being twenty marks exactly; and three days apiece in advance to the same four men at the figure in chalk, being two thousand and sixteen pence, being twelve marks and eight shillings, and that those four men are not on that sheet and are not counted on it and their names are in the book the days are paid out of; and that the four figures and the sum of them were said out loud and the sum of them is fifteen thousand three hundred and twenty pence, being ninety-five marks and ten shillings, and that she said out loud that a figure for a yard that is standing with a roof on it is not a bill, and that it goes onto that sheet with the day the work was done against it and into the total at the foot of that sheet on the day it is settled, and that nobody agreed to any part of it and nobody put a hand on the slate; and that nothing went out on any line of that sheet in any hand on that day and nothing went into it in any hand and nobody was asked for anything, and that one hundred and sixty-eight pence came into that yard and one hundred and sixty-eight pence went out of it, and that twenty-eight plus one hundred and sixty-eight less one hundred and sixty-eight is twenty-eight pence at the close of the said day, being two shillings and fourpence, being short of the wage of four marks by six hundred and twelve pence, being three marks eleven shillings; and that that sheet stood at sixty thousand four hundred and ninety-eight pence out and ten thousand six hundred and forty pence in and is forty-nine thousand eight hundred and fifty-eight pence short, being three hundred and eleven marks, eight shillings and twopence, and that the running out on it did not move from the close of the Monday; and that a mould of a trawlman's shot of about nineteen pounds to the mould stands on a shelf in that market and has not been used since the middle of September; and that a boat's boat came inside that mole on the second with a coil of new line in it and nobody in that harbor bought a fathom of it.

*Second column, not checkable from a mole, entered on the said Tuesday at about half past nine in the evening in that shed, and no name is against it: that the reason that price was given out loud on a Tuesday morning with no roof missing is the woman whose yard it is and is hers alone, and is that a yard which only knows what a thing costs after it has happened is a yard that has been taught to be surprised, and that the four of them asked her because she is the person in this harbor that a price has ever come out of, and that being that is not an honour and gets a person nothing; and that the reason no figure went onto that sheet on that day is the man who keeps the leaf's own, and is that a number in a yard is not a cost until a day of work has been done against it, and that putting a figure on a sheet for a thing that has not happened would let this yard settle a line for weather, and that a sheet which settles lines for weather is a sheet that cannot be checked; and that the reason that same sum went into that yard's own mouth at the end of that Tuesday as well as into the air at eleven in the morning is the reader's own, and is that a figure nobody has heard is a figure in a room, and that fifteen thousand three hundred and twenty pence going into that yard's own mouth in the evening is not the same number as the same figure going into it in the morning, because in the morning it is a man's guess about a Tuesday and in the evening it is a man warning a market about it; and that the reason not one standing item in that harbor moved on that wage Tuesday is his own, and is that a wage Tuesday is when a harbor finds out what it owes and the twenty envelopes in that chest are still sealed because a man has not asked for anything, and that this yard has put the wage Tuesday thing into three different mouths this month and none of the three mouths had the same words in it; and that the reason that room stayed shut on that Tuesday is hers, and is that those stairs are climbed for a thing that has already happened, and that a slate in a shipyard is not a thing that has happened, and that she would not have read that number out to twenty people and about eleven of them who cannot read it, because a number nobody has agreed to is not a thing a room should be asked to hold.
