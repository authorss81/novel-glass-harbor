# Chapter 528

Thursday the thirtieth day of December, in that harbor, in about four hundred yards of water and along a stone quay, and in about four fathoms of water off the head of that third lay.

**The said Thursday was a rota day and was not a wage Tuesday and was not a wipe. The cistern at the back of that market was charged for the hundred and twelfth time at about eight in the morning and was walked on by none of it, and the name at the head of that rota board is still off it. Air for the day of the three went onto the sheet that carries the money at about ten in the morning at one thousand and one hundred and twenty pence, being seven marks, with the thirtieth of December against it, and nothing went into that sheet in any hand in any line on that day and nobody was asked for anything. One hundred and sixty-eight pence came into that yard at about ten in the morning and one hundred and sixty-eight pence went out of it at about four in the afternoon, and there are twenty-eight pence in the bag at the close of the said day, being two shillings and fourpence, and the figure that bag is short of the wage of four marks is six hundred and twelve pence, being three marks eleven shillings. That sheet stands at fifty-eight thousand four hundred and ninety-eight pence out, being three hundred and sixty-five marks, eight shillings and twopence, and ten thousand six hundred and forty pence in, being sixty-six marks, six shillings and eightpence, and it is forty-seven thousand eight hundred and fifty-eight pence short, being two hundred and ninety-nine marks, one shilling and sixpence, and that figure is against that yard's own book and was said out loud in that yard's own mouth and was not answered by anybody. Nothing has been in a tin in that shed since the twenty-ninth day of April past, and no penny of the whole of that day came out of that bag, out of the owner of any boat, out of the man with two papers, or out of the Crown.**

**The nine that a stranger in that harbor could have read off any wall are the nine below, and not one of them was found by looking: the notice of the seventeenth of May past, two hundred and twenty-six days; the bellwright of that harbor, not asked for two hundred and twenty-eight; the two objects of nobody's on that crown, five hundred and sixty-six days; the bench under that pump, bare for the three hundred and thirty-second night; the two lights at about a mile off that point, one hundred and fifty days; the six-line sheet at the back of that end stall, its hundred and fifth day; the paper of four lines in Fish Street, its hundred and twentieth; the nine-line sheet with a seal on it in that front street, its hundred and thirty-seventh; and a man of about fifty-one who has said nothing to anybody about a page of six names for a hundred and sixty-eight days. A sluice gate at the back of that market has been out of reach to a boat's side since the spring and two men go in on their knees to it and neither of them is paid for it. There are eleven pieces of a boat on the crown of that mole and they have been there since the eleventh and a man of sixty-one sits on that crown from ten until four and nobody sent for him and nobody thanked him. A man of about twenty-nine who came home on that boat can hear out of the left side of his head and it has been twenty-six days and he was in that shed for about an hour that morning and nobody in that shed spoke to him and he did not ask anybody anything.**

**The cistern was charged at about eight in the morning by two men who did not stop walking, and the water went into it out of a hose that is dead until the spring and had to be carried from the butt behind that market in eleven trips, and the hundred and twelfth of them is the last one of them before this yard goes down a coast road tomorrow, and the name at the head of that rota board is still off it and is going to still be off it.**

---

**At about ten in the morning the man who keeps the leaf put one line onto that sheet and said the whole of it out loud before he wrote a figure against it, and about nine people were on that slip and heard all of it.**

"Air for the day of the three. One thousand one hundred and twenty pence, being seven marks, off a boat alongside the west quay that carries her own bottles, with the thirtieth of December against it, being the day the work was done, and it is in the total at the foot of that sheet this afternoon because I am settling it this afternoon. **Every man that goes into that water today goes on that line and on no other line, and there is no other line on this sheet that will pay for a man in that water and there is not going to be one this month.** If a man goes in at half past ten and the air is short then the air was bought short at ten o'clock this morning and that is a figure that stands in the book at the time it was agreed and not at the time it went wrong."

He put the pen down flat and did not say one word more about it.

---

**And at about half past eleven in the morning, before anybody was off that slip, three slates were written and put on the bench stone in that shed, and the reason on each of them was in the man whose name was on it because he had said those words out loud in that shed on Wednesday morning and the man who keeps the leaf wrote them down as they came out of him, and the hour was against each of them and the air on each of them was a figure of twenty-five minutes.**

1. *Because there is a mark of a setters under that float and nobody in this harbor knows what is under it, and I have pulled on a line at four fathoms in the dark on a tide and I would rather know before somebody goes out on it. Twenty-five minutes. If I say nothing for two minutes, pull it. — W. Hesk, first lay, the eleventh of this month at about half past eight.*
2. *Because I have run a lighter of about eleven tons over that water in the dark twice a week for nineteen years off the top of the flood and I have never once been told what the bottom of it does, and I am not going to be the fourth man in this harbor to go over it in the dark without asking. Twenty-five minutes. If I say nothing for two minutes, pull it. — J. Anse, west quay, the eleventh of this month at about half past eight.*
3. *Because the compressor on that quay has run for nineteen years and I have heard that float come up against its own board in the dark on a falling tide and I have never had an answer about it and there is nobody to ask. Twenty-five minutes. If I say nothing for two minutes, pull it. — T. Rieve, second lay, the eleventh of this month at about half past eight.*

**Ivo Sark, of about twenty-four, one of the master's two men, was the named tender on the surface for the whole of it, in a dinghy about nine feet off that slip, with his own hand on the chain weight and a cutoff he could pull without asking anybody's leave, and he was not in that water at any hour of that afternoon. Nessa Pike, of about twenty-seven, who has a shipyard on that quay, was on the compressor with him. The man who keeps the leaf sat on that bench stone with the book and the three slates and the hours and was not the tender and said out loud that the tender was not going to be him and that a man who writes the hours is not the man with the cutoff.**

**There were four lines in that water and only two of them were anybody's: the forty fathoms of two-inch stuff running from the float to a setters mark put into a stone under about four fathoms on the twenty-first of December, which is an object on that floor and is a piece of lead in a hole in a stone and is not a person; a separate thirty feet of three-inch that went down with each of them on its own weight and belonged to each of them and not to the route; and a thirty-foot surface line in the dinghy that was the tender's and nothing else.**

---

**And at about half past twelve in the afternoon, before anybody was off that slip, one of the three asked out loud for a channel to be dropped, and the drop was made, and nobody argued with him about it, and nobody in that boat and nobody on that slip asked him which channel it was.**

The plan on the bench stone was two hundred yards of that water in three channels, one to a man, and a float lying at about the two hundred yard mark with a bell under a board on it, and the mark a Crown man set in a stone under it at about four fathoms on the twenty-first of December at the north end of that run. The man who spoke was the man of about thirty-three of the west quay, and he said it in about nine words, and he said it standing up, and he did not say it to the man of about thirty-two.

"Give one of them up. I am not going to hold all of that and I am not going to be one of the men who makes one of the others hold all of it. **Take a channel off the table before anybody is off the slip and put the water that was on it back to being water nobody has looked at.**"

The man who keeps the leaf took the slate and rubbed out one of the three lines and wrote the hour against the rubbing-out, and nobody asked the man of about thirty-three which of the three he had meant and nobody asked him afterwards and he was not asked twice.

"What is it now."

"A hundred and ten yards held out of two hundred. **Ninety yards at the north end of that water is going to be water that nobody in this harbor has looked at and is going to stay that way until somebody looks at it, and the man who made it so is standing on this slip and is not going to be thanked for it and I am not going to write a figure against him.**"

Somebody at the back of that slip said that was the whole of the use of three men then, and the man of about thirty-three said it was not the whole of the use of three men, and that he had been asked whether he would hold one channel of about a hundred yards and he had said yes and had meant it, and that the ninety yards was not a sacrifice anybody was making to anybody, it was a length of water he did not want to answer for and he would not want to answer for it if the man of about thirty-two was the one asking.

Nobody argued with him. Nobody thanked him.

---

**And then the three went down one at a time, on a falling tide and a making, and the top of that flood was about five o'clock in the afternoon and nobody was in that water at the top of it, and no two of them were in it at once, and none of them was in it twice.**

**The first was the man of about fifty-one who crews four out of the first lay.** He went over the rail at about ten past one in the afternoon and was in that water about five minutes before he was on that bottom, and he was on it about eighteen of his twenty-five, and at about thirty-three minutes past one the tender called for the cutoff because the man on the bottom had said nothing for two minutes, and the cutoff was pulled without anybody asking, and the word came about four seconds after it was called for and was not asked for twice, and his helmet came off at about thirty-five minutes past one, being five minutes down, eighteen on the bottom and two up, which is twenty-five of his twenty-five. He was not hurt and nobody has named him a casualty and no figure is set against him and he was not thanked.

**The second was the man of about thirty-three of the west quay.** He went over the rail at about ten past two in the afternoon, on the making and not on the top of it, and he was in that water about five minutes before he was on that bottom, and he was on it about eighteen of his twenty-five, and he did not ask to come up and the cutoff was not pulled on him, and his helmet came off at about thirty-five minutes past two, being five minutes down, eighteen on the bottom and two up, which is twenty-five of his twenty-five. He came up on the flood that had begun to make under him and he asked, in the boat, for a cup of water and not for anything else, and he was given a cup of water.

**The third was the man of about thirty-eight who works that compressor.** He went over the rail at about ten past three in the afternoon, with about an hour and fifty minutes to run before the top of that flood, and he was in that water about five minutes before he was on that bottom, and he was on it about eighteen of his twenty-five, and at about thirty-three minutes past three the tender called for the cutoff because the man on the bottom had said nothing for two minutes, and the cutoff was pulled without anybody asking, and the word came about four seconds after it was called for, and his helmet came off at about thirty-five minutes past three, being five minutes down, eighteen on the bottom and two up, which is twenty-five of his twenty-five.

**Nobody went into that water twice on that day and nobody went in at the top of that flood and an ascent a man asked for was not argued with, and no man was on that bottom alone for a second of it, because the tender's own line was on that floor with the setters mark the whole time and three other men were standing on a slip on the hard above it in the open with their hands on a boat.**

---

**And at about half past four in the afternoon the three of them were on that bench stone and gave three accounts of what they had had, and each one of them gave his own in his own words, and the three accounts do not agree about one thing and were not made to agree and nobody in that shed asked them to.**

The man of about fifty-one said the chain was moving. He said he had his hand on the stone under that float and that about eleven fathoms of chain lay in a curve under it, curving away to the north, and that it moved against the stone twice while he was down there, both times slow, both times with a space between, and that the space was the length of about four seconds and that he had counted it.

The man of about thirty-three said the water was doing something over that chain. He said that the water came over him in one direction and went back in the other and that the shape of the going-back was the shape of the curve, and that he could not tell anybody what shape a thing has and that he knew the shape of that water, and that the whole of the second part of it was in the first man's mouth and he was not going to take it off him.

The man of about thirty-eight said the chain was in a hole and not on a stone. He said it was not lying on the floor, it was down in something about a foot and a half wide and about a foot deep that runs north under that water, and that he put his whole forearm in it twice and it went in to the elbow both times, and that whatever the first two men had, there is a trough under that float and it is not a rut and it was cut.

**Put together, that is about eleven fathoms of chain in a curve running north into a trough about a foot and a half wide and about a foot deep, with a setters mark in a stone over the north end of it and a float lying in about four fathoms on the top of it, and that is more than any one of those three men had, and it is not a recording and it is not a verdict and it is not a route and it cannot be taken off a page by a stranger with a pencil because it is three men's mouths in a shed and not one page.**

And they do not agree about one thing, and that one thing is whether the float was being held or was loose, and the man of about fifty-one said the line to the setters mark had it and the line was doing it, and the man of about thirty-three said the line had it and the water moved it and the line was doing nothing, and the man of about thirty-eight said he was not watching the float, he was watching the trough, and he does not know.

Nobody settled it. It went into the second column of that leaf in the order the three of them said it and not into the first column, and the man who keeps the leaf said out loud that it was going in in the order it was said and would stay in that order and that he was not going to be asked to give an opinion on it and had not got one and was not going to get one.

---

**And at about half past five in the afternoon the man who keeps the leaf said the shortfall out loud at the bench stone, and it was said before the three of them gave their accounts and it was said once, and nobody was asked for the rest of it.**

"Forty-seven thousand eight hundred and fifty-eight pence short, being two hundred and ninety-nine marks, one shilling and sixpence. It moved this morning by one thousand one hundred and twenty pence and that is air for the day of the three, and every man that went into that water today went on that line and on no other line. Fifty-eight thousand four hundred and ninety-eight out and ten thousand six hundred and forty in, and a stranger with a leaf and a pencil can take all three of those figures off that sheet in about a minute and find that they agree."

"And this is what that line bought and it is not a route and it is not a page. **A hundred and ten yards of that water are in three men's heads and about ninety yards of it are in nobody's, and the man who made it so is standing on this quay and did not ask to be thanked and is not going to be.** There is a trough under that float going north and nobody knows where it stops and I have not written one word of that on that sheet, because a sheet with money on it cannot carry a thing three men said in a shed at half past four, and if I put it on there it would be a different thing and a worse one and it would be mine and not theirs."

Nobody in that harbor was asked for the rest of it, no figure has been asked of any person in that harbor to make it otherwise, and nobody was thanked for anything on that day.

---

**And at about six o'clock in the evening the three of them were in that shed and all three of them had the same thing in their mouths, and it was paid in that shed, and it was not a lesson and it was not a speech and nobody thanked anybody for it.**

A sweetness at the back of the throat and a pressure behind the eyes that was not a headache and a want to be quiet that lasted about ten minutes and then went off of its own accord. All three of them, in three mouths, at about the same time, and one of them said it out loud and the other two looked at each other and one of them said *that is what I have got* and nobody wrote a word of it on that sheet that carries the money and no figure is set against any of it and nobody was thanked and nobody was named a casualty and none of the three is a member of anything.

And at about ten to seven in the evening the man of about thirty-two, who kept the book and the three slates and the hours and who was not the tender and who was not in that water at any hour of that afternoon, said out loud, in that shed, to about nine men, that he was not certain which of the three accounts was his own.

He said it in about twenty words and he did not explain it and nobody asked him to.

"I heard all three of them and I have all three of them and I cannot tell you afterwards which of the three I would have given, and I am not going to stand here and work it out in front of you. **That is not a thing I am going to be able to put down this month, and I am not asking anybody to put it down for me either.**"

No figure is set against it. He is not a casualty and nobody has named him one. He is not a man who has been in the water too often and he has not been in that water at any hour of that day, and the tremor in both hands and the stretch of an afternoon he cannot put in order are exactly where they were on the second of December, and none of it is improved and none of it is a motive in anything he did on that Thursday.

**THE CISTERN AT THE BACK OF THAT MARKET WAS CHARGED FOR THE HUNDRED AND TWELFTH TIME AND IS WALKED ON BY NONE OF IT. THREE SLATES WERE WRITTEN BEFORE ANYBODY WAS OFF THAT SLIP AND EVERY ONE OF THEM CARRIES A REASON IN THAT MAN'S OWN WORDS AND THE HOUR AGAINST IT AND A FIGURE OF TWENTY-FIVE MINUTES. THREE MEN WENT INTO ABOUT FOUR FATHOMS OFF THAT THIRD LAY ONE AT A TIME BETWEEN ABOUT TEN PAST ONE AND ABOUT THIRTY-FIVE MINUTES PAST THREE, ON A FALLING TIDE AND A MAKING, AND NOBODY WENT IN TWICE AND NOBODY WENT IN AT THE TOP OF THAT FLOOD AND THE TENDER WAS IN A DINGHY AND NOT IN THE WATER AT ANY HOUR AND THE CUTOFF WAS PULLED TWICE WITHOUT ANYBODY ASKING. ONE OF THE THREE ASKED OUT LOUD FOR A CHANNEL TO BE DROPPED AND THE DROP WAS MADE AND NOBODY ARGUED WITH HIM ABOUT IT AND NOBODY ASKED HIM WHICH CHANNEL IT WAS. THE HELD WATER IS ABOUT A HUNDRED AND TEN YARDS OUT OF TWO HUNDRED AND NINETY AND THE OTHER NINETY YARDS IS WATER NOBODY IN THAT HARBOR HAS LOOKED AT. *Second column, not checkable from a mole, entered on the said Thursday at about half past nine in the evening in that shed, and no name is against it: that the reason the man of about thirty-three of the west quay was allowed to have one channel dropped off the slate with nobody arguing with him is the man who keeps the leaf's own, and is that a man asked to hold a length of water and finding he cannot hold it has done the only honest thing available to him by saying so in a room before anybody is wet, and that a person who is allowed to say no early is a person whose no costs the rest of the room nothing, and that the ninety yards at the north end of that water is a hole in what this harbor knows and it is going to be a hole until somebody looks at it; and that the reason not one figure in that harbor was settled, advanced, bettered or shut on that Thursday is his own, and is that a cistern was charged for the hundred and twelfth time this morning and a second pressure case is going down a coast road in a wagon tomorrow, and that the wage column stands at twenty-six lines and one hundred and four marks with a week fallen due at the close of yesterday, and that there is no flour in that market, and that the air line is the only line on that sheet which has ever paid for a man in water and the air went on that line and on no other.**
