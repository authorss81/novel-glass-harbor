# Chapter 519

*Tuesday the Twenty-First Day of December, the Sixty-First Wage Tuesday, the Wage Column in That Harbor Standing at Twenty-Six Lines and One Hundred and Four Marks With Twenty Envelopes in a Chest and Not Moving and a Further Week Standing Fallen Due at Its Close, Being the Sixty-First, and Nobody Paid a Penny Out of That Chest, Seven Sentences Written at the Head of a Page at About Half Past Eight in the Morning Before Anybody Was Asked Anything and Not Altered After That, Three Local Crews Asked One at a Time and Not in Front of Each Other Whether They Will Each Hold One Channel of the Same Forty Minutes of Water Off That Third Lay in About Four Fathoms, Two of Them Saying Yes and One of About Thirty-Eight Saying No at About Ten Minutes to Three in the Afternoon, Out Loud, in a Room, in About Three Sentences, and Not Being Argued With and Not Being Asked Why and His Three Sentences Entered in That Room's Own Book as What He Said and Not as Why He Said It, the Other Two Going On Without Him, Three Days of Air for the Crossing Going Onto the Sheet at About Half Past Eleven in the Morning at Eight Hundred and Eighty Pence, Being Five Marks, Six Shillings and Eightpence, With the Twenty-First of December Against It, and Every Descent from That Day on That Line and on Nothing Else, One Man Down at About Ten Past Three in the Afternoon on the Last of the Ebb With a Slate Written Before Anybody Was Off the Boat Carrying the Reason in His Own Words and the Hour and a Figure of Thirty Minutes, a Named Tender on the Surface and Not in the Water and an Independent Line and a Cutoff He Could Pull Without Asking, on the Bottom at About a Quarter Past Three and the Word at About Thirty-Eight Minutes Past Three and Out of That Water at About Forty Minutes Past Three, the Float Taken Up on Its Own Line at About Ten to Five and the Bell Not Struck Twice and a Crossing Made by Four Boats and Not Five, the Man Who Keeps the Leaf Writing the Whole of That Afternoon Into the Second Column With the Hour Against It and Not One Word of It Into the First, the Shortfall Said Out Loud at Thirty-Six Thousand Eight Hundred and Ninety-Eight Pence, Being Two Hundred and Thirty Marks, Eight Shillings and Twopence, the Bag at Twenty-Eight Pence, Nobody Thanked*

Tuesday the twenty-first day of December, in that harbor, in about four hundred yards of water and along a stone quay, and in about four fathoms of water off the head of that third lay, and in a shed at the head of that lay and in about two hundred yards of that water.

**The said Tuesday was not a rota day and was not a wipe, and no charge was placed on that cistern on it, and it was the sixty-first wage Tuesday. The wage column in that harbor stands at twenty-six lines and one hundred and four marks with twenty envelopes in a chest and did not move on that day, and nobody was paid a penny out of that chest, and a further week stands fallen due at its close, being the sixty-first. Three days of air for the crossing went onto the sheet that carries the money at about half past eleven in the morning at eight hundred and eighty pence, being five marks, six shillings and eightpence, with the twenty-first of December against it, being the day the work was done and the day it is written, which are the same day this once, and every descent from that day is on that line and on no other line. Nothing went into that sheet in any hand in any line on that day and nobody was asked for anything. One hundred and sixty-eight pence came into that yard at about ten in the morning and one hundred and sixty-eight pence went out of it at about four in the afternoon, and there are twenty-eight pence in the bag at the close of the said day, being two shillings and fourpence, and the figure that bag is short of the wage of four marks is six hundred and twelve pence, being three marks eleven shillings. That sheet stands at forty-seven thousand five hundred and thirty-eight pence out, being two hundred and ninety-seven marks, one shilling and sixpence, and ten thousand six hundred and forty pence in, being sixty-six marks, six shillings and eightpence, and it is thirty-six thousand eight hundred and ninety-eight pence short, being two hundred and thirty marks, eight shillings and twopence, and that figure is against that yard's own book and was said out loud in that yard's own mouth and was not answered by anybody. Nothing has been in a tin in that shed since the twenty-ninth day of April past, and no penny of the whole of that day came out of that bag, out of the owner of any boat, out of the man with two papers, or out of the Crown.**

**The nine that a stranger in that harbor could have read off any wall are the nine below, and not one of them was found by looking: the notice of the seventeenth of May past, two hundred and seventeen days; the bellwright of that harbor, not asked for two hundred and nineteen; the two objects of nobody's on that crown, five hundred and fifty-seven days; the bench under that pump, bare for the three hundred and twenty-third night; the two lights at about a mile off that point, one hundred and forty-one days; the six-line sheet at the back of that end stall, its ninety-sixth day; the paper of four lines in Fish Street, its hundred and eleventh; the nine-line sheet with a seal on it in that front street, its hundred and twenty-eighth; and a man of about fifty-one who has said nothing to anybody about a page of six names for a hundred and fifty-nine days. A sheet of paper with a seal at the foot of it and no figure on it is still nailed to the board at the head of that mole, and it has not been taken off that board, it has not been opened, it has not been copied, and there is no key to it in that harbor and none is going to be given. There are nine hulls lying about two sea miles off the head of that mole and on that afternoon there were still nine of them and they had not swung at the half past the ebb and the count has not been taken by anybody in that harbor twice. A man of about twenty-nine who came home on that boat can hear out of the left side of his head and it has been thirteen days and he has not said a word about it and nobody in that harbor has asked him.**

---

**At about ten o'clock in the morning a clerk of the harbor authority read the column out on the step at the back of that market, and about forty people were on the boards in front of that step and about twenty of them cannot read the column on the lid, and he put his own name at the foot of the reading and the hour, and the chest with the twenty envelopes in it was shut at ten o'clock and was shut at four o'clock and the sixty-first week stands fallen due at the close of that day.**

**And at about half past eight in the morning, before any of that and before anybody had been asked anything, a page went onto the bench stone in that shed with seven sentences at the head of it, and they were not altered after that hour on that day by anybody.**

1. *This is a stretch of about four fathoms off the head of that third lay, about two hundred yards long, and it is the water a boat takes to come in on the making flood and there is nothing else in it.*
2. *Nobody is being asked to go into it. What is being asked is that a man stand in a boat on that water for forty minutes of the making and hold one end of a line with both hands and not let it go slack.*
3. *There are three of them and there are three ends of it, one end each, and the far end of that line goes into a stone under four fathoms while a man in a case is on the bottom of it, and he is not one of them.*
4. *What a channel is, is two hands on that line for forty minutes. What it costs is forty minutes of a tide, a pair of gloves, and the boat a crew had to bring.*
5. *If an end is let go the mark goes over to the weather and the boats do not come in on it, and it is not the man at that end who decides that, it is the water and the weather and the tide.*
6. *Nobody is paid for this. Nobody in that room can send anybody anywhere. If the answer is no this afternoon nobody will ask again this month or any month after it.*
7. *If the answer is yes it is said in that room, out loud, in front of the man who is asking, and what is said goes into the book on that bench stone with the hour against it and the name of the crew at the top of it.*

---

**Three local crews were asked, one at a time, and not in front of each other, and the seven sentences were read out word for word to each of them before anybody was asked anything else.**

**At about ten o'clock a crew of about six men out of the west quay said yes, and the man who said it was about fifty and he said it in about eleven words and he asked one question before he said it, which was how long, and he was told forty minutes, and he said yes after that.**

**At about half past eleven a crew of five out of the first lay said yes, and the man who said it was about thirty and he said it in about four words and he did not ask anything.**

**At about ten minutes to three in the afternoon, the third of the three was a crew of about four men off the second lay, and the man who spoke for it was a man of about thirty-eight who is one of the four men hired by the day, and he said no, out loud, in that shed, in about three sentences, and nobody argued with him and nobody asked him why.**

"I am not going to hold one."

"I have a thing of my own this afternoon. It is mine, I am not going to be asked about it and I am not going to say what it is, and you asked me nothing and I have told you nothing."

"You have the other two. Go on with the other two."

**Those three sentences went into the book on that bench stone and they went in as what he said and not as why he said it, and nobody has asked him about any of them and nobody is going to, and he is not on any list, and no figure is set against him, and he did not go back up that slip at any hour of that afternoon.**

---

**And the two who had said yes went on without him, and the water did what it does, and the man of about thirty-two did not go back and look for a third.**

Nobody in that shed said anything about the third one at any hour of that afternoon, and the two crews that had said yes did not ask about him, and neither of them asked whether it was still worth it, and it was not a matter that anybody in that harbor put to anybody.

---

**And at about half past eleven in the morning the man who keeps the leaf said the whole of the air line out loud before he wrote a figure against it, and nobody argued with him about any part of it.**

"Three days of air for the crossing. Eight hundred and eighty pence, being five marks, six shillings and eightpence, off a boat alongside the west quay that carries her own bottles, with the twenty-first of December against it. **Every man that goes into that water today goes on that line and on no other line, and there is no other line on this sheet that will pay for a man in that water, and there is not going to be one this month.** If a man goes in on Tuesday and the air is short then the air was bought short on Monday and that is a figure that stands in the book at the time it was agreed and not at the time it went wrong."

---

**A slate was written before anybody was off the boat and it was written on the man who was going down and not by him, and the air limit on it was a figure of thirty minutes, and the reason on it was in his own words because he had said them out loud on that slip at about half past two in the afternoon and the man who keeps the leaf wrote them down as they came out of him.**

*To put an eye on the stone at about four fathoms for the line to be made fast to in the making flood, because a line made fast to a ring in a block of stone is a line that has to be got at in a hurry and a line made fast to nothing is a line that has to be held. Thirty minutes. If I do not say anything for two minutes, pull it.*

Ivo Sark, of about twenty-four, one of the master's two men, was the named tender on the surface for the whole of it, in a dinghy about nine feet off that slip, with his own hand on the chain weight and a cutoff he could pull without asking anybody's leave, and he was not in that water at any hour of that afternoon. The woman of about twenty-seven of that yard was on the compressor with him. **There were two lines in that water and only one of them belonged to the route: the forty fathoms that had been lying off that float since the seventh of December, and a separate thirty feet that went down with him on its own weight and belonged to nobody but him.**

**Calder Marr went down at about ten past three in the afternoon on the last of the ebb, with the water still running off that lay, and he put his hand on the stone, found the place he had dressed on the eighth, and put the eye through it and took the slack out of the line until it came to hand.**

He was in the water about five minutes before he was on the bottom and about twenty-three of his thirty on it. At about thirty-eight minutes past three the man on the bottom asked to come up, and the tender did not argue with it, and the cutoff was pulled without anybody asking, and the word came about four seconds after it was called for and was not asked for twice, and he came up on the flood that was beginning to make under him with his helmet off at about forty minutes past three, being thirty minutes from the time it went over the rail to the time it came off, and the clock closes at every figure in it. Nobody went into that water twice on that day and no two men were in it at once at any hour and nobody went in at the top of a flood, and he was not hurt and nobody has named him a casualty and no figure is set against him.

---

**And then the water made hard at about four o'clock and the float was lying where it had lain since the eighth with its bell under its board and forty fathoms of two-inch stuff running down to that eye, and the tender took the dinghy up to the float and struck the bell once with a hand on the bell rope and did not strike it again.**

A crew of about six men had a boat at the north end of that line with their end of it. A crew of five had a boat at the south end of it with their end of it. **Two ends of a line, two boats, and a man in a dinghy with a cutoff, and the forty minutes of that water were held from about four o'clock until about twenty to five.**

**Five boats of that harbor had come down to the third lay to take that water in. Four of them went.** A boat of about seventeen tons, a boat of about twelve, a lighter of about eleven and a boat of about nine went up that two hundred yards of water between about four o'clock and about half past four with the float lying on the making and the bell's voice going along the stone.

The fifth did not go. Her owner was not asked, at the slip, at the market or in any room in that harbor, at any hour of that evening or since, and he has not been asked and nobody in that harbor is going to ask him, and he is not named on any page in that town. **She is lying at her berth on the south quay this evening with her oars shipped and her warp coiled and there is nothing whatever to report about her, and nobody in that harbor has said a word about her, and there is no list she is on.**

**And at about ten to five the window shut on the tide and not on anybody, and the float was taken up on its own line and on no other, the shackle came off that eye, the forty fathoms came up in the dinghy and were coiled on the thwart, and the bell was not struck twice. The two strokes that mean a thing is coming the other way down that water were not given at about ten to five because nothing was coming the other way down that water, and four boats had gone on the one stroke and that is all that was given.**

---

**And at about half past five in the afternoon the man who keeps the leaf said the shortfall out loud at the bench stone, and the slate was wet and the line was coiled in the dinghy and the bell was dry.**

"Thirty-six thousand eight hundred and ninety-eight pence short, being two hundred and thirty marks, eight shillings and twopence. It moved this morning by eight hundred and eighty pence and that is three days of air, and there is no line on that sheet for the line and the eye and the float and the bell because those went on the seventh of December and I am not putting them on twice."

A woman at the end of that slip asked him, out loud, whether the work would be done again with a different man in the place of the one who had said no, and he said no at once, and did not say it again that month.

"No. **You do not go back into that water and do a thing again with a man in the place of a man who said no**, and I am not going to write it down twice and neither is anybody else."

**Nobody thanked anybody on that day. Not the two crews, not the tender, not the woman on the compressor, not the man in the water and not the man who kept the leaf.**

**And the whole of that afternoon went into the second column of that leaf with the hour against it, and not one word of it went into the first column, and there is a stranger in that harbor who can go and count the first column on that Tuesday and find that it says what every other Tuesday in that book says and nothing about the water at all.**

---

**A CLERK READ THE COLUMN OUT ON THE STEP AT THE BACK OF THAT MARKET AT TEN O'CLOCK AND THE COLUMN DID NOT MOVE AND THE SIXTY-FIRST WEEK STANDS FALLEN DUE AT THE CLOSE OF THAT DAY. SEVEN SENTENCES WERE ON A PAGE AT HALF PAST EIGHT IN THE MORNING AND THREE LOCAL CREWS WERE ASKED IN THEM ONE AT A TIME AND NOT IN FRONT OF EACH OTHER. TWO OF THEM SAID YES. THE THIRD SAID NO IN THREE SENTENCES AT ABOUT TEN MINUTES TO THREE IN THE AFTERNOON AND WAS NOT ARGUED WITH AND WAS NOT ASKED WHY AND HIS THREE SENTENCES ARE IN THE BOOK ON THAT BENCH STONE AS WHAT HE SAID AND NOT AS WHY HE SAID IT. AN EYE WENT INTO A STONE IN ABOUT FOUR FATHOMS OFF THE HEAD OF THAT THIRD LAY AT ABOUT TEN PAST THREE ON THE LAST OF THE EBB, AND THE LINE CAME OFF IT AT ABOUT TEN TO FIVE ON ITS OWN LINE AND ON NO OTHER. FOUR BOATS WENT UP THAT WATER AND A FIFTH DID NOT GO AND NOBODY HAS ASKED HER OWNER WHY. THE BELL WAS STRUCK ONCE AND WAS NOT STRUCK TWICE. THE SHEET THAT CARRIES THE MONEY IS THIRTY-SIX THOUSAND EIGHT HUNDRED AND NINETY-EIGHT PENCE SHORT AGAINST THAT YARD'S OWN BOOK AND THE BAG UNDER THE SLATE IS TWENTY-EIGHT PENCE.**

---

*First column, entered on Tuesday the twenty-first day of December at about half past nine in the evening in that shed: that the said day was not a rota day and was not a wipe, and that no charge was placed on that cistern on it, and that it was the sixty-first wage Tuesday, and that the wage column in that harbor stood at twenty-six lines and one hundred and four marks with twenty envelopes in a chest and did not move and that nobody was paid a penny out of that chest and that a further week stands fallen due at its close, being the sixty-first, and that the column was read out on the step at the back of that market at about ten o'clock in the morning by a clerk of the harbor authority with his own name and the hour at the foot of the reading in front of about forty people of whom about twenty cannot read the column on the lid; and that three days of air for the crossing went onto the sheet that carries the money at about half past eleven in the morning at eight hundred and eighty pence, being five marks, six shillings and eightpence, with the twenty-first of December against it, and that the man who keeps the leaf said the whole of that line out loud before he wrote a figure against it and that every descent from that day is on that line and on no other line on that sheet; and that nothing went onto that sheet in any hand in any line on that day and nobody was asked for anything, and that one hundred and sixty-eight pence came into that yard and one hundred and sixty-eight pence went out of it, and that twenty-eight plus one hundred and sixty-eight less one hundred and sixty-eight is twenty-eight pence at the close of the said day, being two shillings and fourpence, being short of the wage of four marks by six hundred and twelve pence, being three marks eleven shillings; and that that sheet stood at forty-seven thousand five hundred and thirty-eight pence out and ten thousand six hundred and forty pence in and is thirty-six thousand eight hundred and ninety-eight pence short, being two hundred and thirty marks, eight shillings and twopence, and that the running out on it moved by eight hundred and eighty pence on that day; and that seven sentences were written at the head of a page in that shed at about half past eight in the morning before anybody was asked anything and were read out word for word to each of three local crews, one at a time and not in front of each other, at about ten o'clock, at about half past eleven and at about ten minutes to three in the afternoon, and that two of them said yes and that a man of about thirty-eight said no in about three sentences at about ten minutes to three and was not argued with and was not asked why, and that his three sentences went into the book on that bench stone as what he said and not as why he said it; and that the third column is empty and nobody has been appointed to it and the fourth column stands at one hundred and twenty-five and took no line; and that the four columns at the close of that day stand at two hundred and fifty-two, one thousand and one hundred and thirty-four, none and one hundred and twenty-five, and at the hour of that day's reading at two hundred and fifty-one, one thousand and one hundred and thirty-two.*

*Second column, not checkable from a mole, entered on the said Tuesday at about half past nine in the evening in that shed, and no name is against it: that the reason the whole of that afternoon went into this column and not one word of it went into the first is the man who keeps the leaf's own, and is that the first column is a thing a stranger can count and this was not a thing a stranger can count, being one man in four fathoms and two boats at the ends of a line and a weight a man could pull, and that a page which is counted ought to be a page of days and that page was not a day; and that the reason a man of about thirty-eight was asked the same question in the same seven sentences as the other two and his three sentences went in as what he said is this yard's own mouth, and is that a no asked in the same words as a yes is the only kind of no that is worth having, and that a no which had been asked in easier words would have been a different no and might have been about the words; and that the reason the reason was not asked of him is this yard's own, and is that he said he had a thing of his own and that a man who has told a room he has a thing and is not going to say what it is has told that room everything he owes it, and that asking him again would have taken that away; and that the reason the two crews that said yes did not look for a third is his own, and is that a channel held by two men is a channel, and that the page said there were three ends and two of them were taken and the one that was not taken was not filled, and that filling it with somebody else at two o'clock in the afternoon would have been a different page and not this one; and that the reason nothing standing in that harbor was paid, advanced, reduced, improved, struck or closed on that Tuesday is this yard's own mouth, and is that a page in a room and a line in a column are two things that happened on that afternoon and that neither of them is an account, and that this yard put every standing figure in that harbor back where it stood that day and moved none of them for four boats that went or for a boat that did not; and that the reason nobody in that harbor was told that the work had been done and was not going to be done again with a man in the place of the man who said no is this yard's own mouth, and is that a thing a man can do once and did once is not a thing anybody in that harbor needs to be given twice, and that this yard put the whole of it in a column four hundred yards from that water and not on a wall in the market, and that a harbor that reads that column one day will have it a year from now and a harbor that reads it on a wall reads it for a week.*
