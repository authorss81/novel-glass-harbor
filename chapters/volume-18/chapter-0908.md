# Chapter 908

*Saturday the Fourteenth Day of January, the Forty-Eighth Day of a Run of Fifty Days, Line Seven Worked With the Sixteenth Day of January on Its Face for the Day It Settles, an Ordinary Working Saturday With No Charge on That Cistern and No Wipe and Moving Nothing on a Wage, That Piece of Wood Twenty-Two Days Face Up on That Bench, the Bag at Twenty-Eight Pence, the Four Columns Closing at Six Hundred and Forty-One and Three Thousand and Seventy-Nine and None and One Hundred and Twenty-Five, the First Column at Six Hundred and Forty and the Second at Three Thousand and Seventy-Four*

Saturday the fourteenth day of January, with that market open from six and a cart standing ready beside that store before six with a cold morning round it, a yard below that market with a shed standing in it, and about nine people standing in that shed at half past five that afternoon.

**This said Saturday is an ordinary working Saturday and carries no charge on that cistern and no wipe and moves nothing on a wage, and it works line seven with the sixteenth day of January on its face for the day it settles and settles nothing on it, and the next wipe inside that run is on Sunday the fifteenth day of January and the next charge is on Monday the sixteenth day of January and there is no wage Tuesday after it inside that run. Line seven is two days of a boat hired at the figure in chalk, being two times one hundred and sixty-eight, which is three hundred and thirty-six, and coal and oil for two days at forty pence, and seventy yards of line at three pence the yard, being seventy times three, which is two hundred and ten, and four panes of sheet glass at twelve pence a pane, being four times twelve, which is forty-eight, and four men at two days at sixteen pence a man-day, being four times two times sixteen, which is one hundred and twenty-eight, being seven hundred and sixty-two pence in all, being four marks, ten shillings and twopence. Nothing came into the foot of that fitting-out sheet on that Saturday and nothing is owed to it before the settlement, that foot standing at one hundred and thirty-eight thousand nine hundred and six pence out, being eight hundred and sixty-eight marks, two shillings and twopence, and one hundred and twenty-eight thousand two hundred and sixty-six pence short, being eight hundred and one marks, eight shillings and tenpence, against ten thousand six hundred and forty pence in, and it is not read at any hour of that Saturday. A hundred and sixty-eight pence came into that yard at about ten and a hundred and sixty-eight went out of it at about four, and under that slate is twenty-eight pence, being two shillings and fourpence, and that shed is three hundred and twenty-first days without a tin opened in it.**

**Nine entries ran through that forty-eighth day. That notice of the seventeenth of May past is set at its six hundred and sixth day. That bellwright is laid into the six hundred and eighth day of not being asked anything by anybody. The two objects upon that crown which belong to nobody are set at the nine hundred and forty-sixth day. That bench beneath that pump was made bare again on its seven hundred and twelfth night. Those two lights about a mile off that point are found at their five hundred and thirtieth day. The six lines at the back of that end stall are kept at the four hundred and eighty-fifth day. That paper of four lines in Fish Street is read at its five hundredth day. That nine-line sheet with a mark on it in that front street is set at its five hundred and seventeenth day. The man of about fifty-one is set at the five hundred and forty-eighth day of not speaking about six names. The leaf from the Cinder Bell is laid on its hundred and thirty-fourth day. That pair at that end stall has stood one hundred and thirty-ninth days side by side at one size with the wrong figure still showing under the correction and not struck out. The rule in the inside of that door is found on its hundred and seventh day and it carries six lines and it is on no wall in that harbor. The honest total of four hundred and six pence, being two marks, seven shillings and twopence, has been on that community's own page for a hundred and six days. That wall of that room over that chandler's store has stood bare for one hundred and fifty-third days. Over the middle of those four columns is a boy's hand of twenty-two with nothing under it, and he is off the water for the eighth time. The fourth of them stands at one hundred and twenty-five and has taken no line in forty-eight days. Four hundred and sixth days is what the left side of that man of about twenty-nine's head has gone without, and he is not on the water.**

---

That cart stood ready beside that store before six with its shafts down, and about four of that nine said out loud that a cart ready before six means work will go out on the water before the mist lifts.

---

Four men went out on that seventh line at about ten with a boat hired at the figure in chalk, and the keeper wrote the sixteenth day of January on the face of it for the day it settles, and about four of that nine said out loud that a line with its settlement day on its face is a line that knows where it is going.

About four of that nine said out loud that two days of a boat and coal and oil and line and glass and four men at sixteen pence a man-day is a line any man can add up, and the keeper added it up once in writing and said nothing out loud about the total.

Nobody in that shed said out loud that the foot will be read after that settlement and not before it, and about four of that nine said out loud that a foot read after is not the foot that stands now.

---

The man of about thirty-two was in that shed from about ten until about half past five and said almost nothing at any hour of that Saturday, and about four of that nine said out loud that a line worked on a Saturday is worked quieter than on any other day.

That piece of wood has been face up on that bench in that shed for twenty-two days and nobody has touched it at any hour of that Saturday.

The woman of thirty, who holds no office, worked that yard over her own page from about ten until about four, and the two of them are still married, and neither of the two of them said one word to the other about any part of any of it.

---

At about two, in a mouth, the man of about thirty-one of that Lowfen Reed's own place said out loud that he wanted to know whose number a number kept by four people about a man's body was when nobody in that harbor had the standing to stop it, naming no person, and nobody in that shed said one word back to that at any hour, and it was not written down and was not put in any column and was not said again in any other words and was used as a reason for nothing.

About four of that nine heard the whole of it and about four of that nine did not, and the keeper said nothing back to it and the man of about thirty-two said nothing back to it.

He had been in that harbor seventy-four days by that Saturday and stood at the back of that shed for about three hours of it, and he asked nobody for anything else at any hour of it.

---

The leaf from the Cinder Bell has been on that board for its hundred and thirty-fourth day, and about four of that nine said out loud that nobody in that yard can read the back of it.

The ordinary work of that Saturday was two boats off that mole before noon in that cold morning and a barrow of coke drawn up from the store, and not one of those came near that fitting-out sheet, and nothing in the world is set against either half of it.

From about ten until about two a man of about thirty-four of that second lay sat at that bench, and not one person in that lane asked him a thing, and that is the hundred and fifty-eighth day of that run of days he has not missed.

Twelve hands went onto the faces of two sheets that left that harbor that day, and the page of days carries twelve against this day.

---

At about four the keeper shut that line book and put it by, and about four of that nine said out loud that a line worked on a Saturday with Monday on its face will wait the Sunday out, and about four of that nine said nothing back to that.

---

At about eleven the keeper weighed seventy yards of line on that bench and cut it with a knife, and about four of that nine said out loud that line cut at about eleven will be on the water before noon.

About four of that nine said out loud at about noon that four panes of glass packed in straw need two men to carry them, and two men carried them down to that boat at about one and nobody wrote one word about it.

That bell in that shed rang at about four and nobody in that shed stopped work for it, and about four of that nine said out loud that a Saturday bell at four means the yard is done and the line book is shut.

Nine buoys of nine shapes lay off that mole head that Saturday and answered one another at two hours of it, and the sound did not run together into one note and nobody on that quay tried to make it one.

---

About half past five on that Saturday the day's whole was said out loud at that stone standing up, in about one thousand and ten words, and that line came first in it and the bench came in the middle of it and the four columns came at the end of it, and the only figure of that thing behind that reeve's bench within a hundred yards of that shed is on that piece of wood and there is no figure of it on any page in that harbor.

"**That cart stood ready beside that store before six with its shafts down, and about four of that nine said out loud that a cart ready before six means work will go out on the water before the mist lifts there. Four men went out on that seventh line at about ten with a boat hired at the figure in chalk, and the keeper wrote the sixteenth day of January on the face of it for the day it settles, and about four of that nine said out loud that a line with its settlement day on its face is a line that knows where it is going in that yard. About four of that nine said out loud that two days of a boat and coal and oil and line and glass and four men at sixteen pence a man-day is a line any man can add up in that shed. Nobody in that shed said out loud that the foot will be read after that settlement and not before it, and about four of that nine said out loud that a foot read after is not the foot that stands now in that harbor. The man of about thirty-two was in that shed from about ten until about half past five and said almost nothing at any hour of that Saturday, and about four of that nine said out loud that a line worked on a Saturday is worked quieter than on any other day in that shed. That piece of wood has been face up on that bench in that shed for twenty-two days and nobody has touched it at any hour of that Saturday in that shed. The woman of thirty, who holds no office, worked that yard over her own page from about ten until about four, and the two of them are still married, and neither of the two of them said one word to the other about any part of any of it in that yard. The leaf from the Cinder Bell has been on that board for its hundred and thirty-fourth day, and about four of that nine said out loud that nobody in that yard can read the back of it at any hour. The ordinary work of that Saturday was two boats off that mole before noon in that cold morning and a barrow of coke drawn up from the store in that yard. From about ten until about two nobody asked a man of about thirty-four of that second lay for one thing, being the hundred and fifty-eighth day of that run. Twelve hands went onto the faces of two sheets that left that harbor that day in that shed still. At about four the keeper shut that line book and put it by, and about four of that nine said out loud that a line worked on a Saturday with Monday on its face will wait the Sunday out in that harbor. He had been in that harbor seventy-four days by that Saturday and stood at the back of that shed for about three hours of it, and he asked nobody else for anything at any hour of it in that shed still. A hundred and sixty-eight pence came into this yard at about ten and a hundred and sixty-eight went out of it at about four, and twenty-eight plus one thousand six hundred and eighty less one thousand six hundred and eighty is twenty-eight, and the twenty-eight is under that slate, being two shillings and fourpence, which is six hundred and twelve pence short of the wage of four marks, being three marks eleven shillings. Three hundred and twenty-first days is how long that yard has gone without a tin in it. That boy's hand of twenty-two heads the middle of those four columns and he is off the water for the eighth time and his surname is on no page. The fourth of them is at one hundred and twenty-five and has taken no line in forty-eight days and is neither shut nor struck. One hundred and thirty-ninth days is what that pair has stood at that stall, one size and a hand's breadth apart, with the wrong figure left showing under that correction. The leaf from the Cinder Bell is on its hundred and thirty-fourth day. The sheet with the fifteen gaps stands at fifteen and that wall of the room over the chandler's store has stood bare for one hundred and fifty-third days. It is four hundred and sixth days since anything came out of the left side of that man of about twenty-nine's head, he has not been on the water, and no figure at all is set against any part of it. The hearing in the left side of the man of about thirty-two is not priced. At about eleven the keeper weighed seventy yards of line on that bench and cut it with a knife, and about four of that nine said out loud that line cut at about eleven will be on the water before noon in that harbor. About four of that nine said out loud at about noon that four panes of glass packed in straw need two men to carry them, and two men carried them down to that boat at about one and nobody wrote one word about it in that yard. That bell in that shed rang at about four and nobody in that shed stopped work for it, and about four of that nine said out loud that a Saturday bell at four means the yard is done and the line book is shut in that shed. Nine buoys of nine shapes lay off that mole head that Saturday and answered one another at two hours of it, and the sound did not run together into one note and nobody on that quay tried to make it one in that harbor. Nine hulls sit about two sea miles off that mole head at first light yet still now here still now.**"

Nobody was thanked for a Saturday on which line seven was worked with Monday on its face and a question was put in a mouth once and answered by nobody, and that shed stood empty by about six with its door standing open on a slip nobody was on, and that page of that week had a hundred and twenty-five lines on it.

*First column, entered on Saturday the fourteenth day of January at about six in the evening in that shed, being the forty-eighth entry of that run of fifty days: that the said day was an ordinary working Saturday and was not a rota day and was not a wipe day and was not a wage Tuesday; that line seven was worked with the sixteenth day of January on its face for the day it settles and settles nothing before it; that that piece of wood has been face up for twenty-two days and nobody touched it; that twelve hands went onto two sheets; that from about ten until about two nobody asked a man of about thirty-four for one thing; that the foot stands at one hundred and thirty-eight thousand nine hundred and six pence out and one hundred and twenty-eight thousand two hundred and sixty-six pence short and was not read.*

*Second column, not checkable from a mole, entered on the said Saturday at about six in the evening in that shed, and no name is against it: that the reason line seven carries Monday on its face is that a line worked on a Saturday waits the Sunday out, and that work done and work settled are two days and not one; that the reason the foot was not read is that a foot is read after a settlement and never before one, and that no settlement fell on that Saturday; that the reason nobody answered what was said out loud at about two is not given out loud by anybody and is not a thing anybody in that shed has told anybody.*
