# Chapter 865

*Friday the Second Day of December, the Fifth Day of a Run of Fifty Days, Line One Settled at About Four at Seven Hundred and Ninety-Four Pence and the Whole of That Foot Read After That Settlement and Not Before It, the Foot Standing at One Hundred and Thirty-Three Thousand Nine Hundred and Eighty-Two Pence Out and One Hundred and Twenty-Three Thousand Three Hundred and Forty-Two Pence Short, About Four of That Nine Around That Bench Stone, the Man of About Thirty-Two Saying Out Loud While the Reading Is Going On That He Does Not Know Who Puts That Wall Up and Nobody Asking Him, the Bag at Twenty-Eight Pence, the Four Columns Closing at Five Hundred and Ninety-Eight and Two Thousand Eight Hundred and Sixty-Four and None and One Hundred and Twenty-Five, the First Column at Five Hundred and Ninety-Seven and the Second at Two Thousand Eight Hundred and Fifty-Nine*

Friday the second day of December, with that market open from six and a fine hard morning with that frost back in the shade of everything and the air sharp enough off that quay to make two men at the end of it put their collars up, a yard below that market with a shed standing in it, and about nine people standing in that shed at half past five that afternoon.

**This said Friday is an ordinary working Friday and carries no charge on that cistern, no wipe, and nothing on a wage, and the next wipe inside this run is on Sunday the fourth day of December and the next charge is on Monday the fifth day of December and the next wage Tuesday is on Tuesday the sixth day of December. Nothing was worked on that fitting-out sheet today and one line settled on it at about four, and that settlement moved two figures and nothing else, that foot standing after this afternoon's settlement at one hundred and thirty-three thousand nine hundred and eighty-two pence out, being eight hundred and thirty-seven marks, five shillings and twopence, and one hundred and twenty-three thousand three hundred and forty-two pence short, being seven hundred and seventy marks, eleven shillings and tenpence, against ten thousand six hundred and forty pence in. A hundred and sixty-eight pence came into that yard at about ten and a hundred and sixty-eight went out of it at about four, and under that slate is twenty-eight pence, being two shillings and fourpence, and that shed is two hundred and seventy-eighth days without a tin opened in it.**

**Nine counters were carried to the close of this fifth day. That notice of the seventeenth of May past is at its five hundred and sixty-third day and no answer has come back off it. The bellwright of this harbor is into the five hundred and sixty-fifth day of not being asked a question. Those two objects upon that crown which belong to nobody are at the nine hundred and third day. The bench beneath that pump lay bare again on its six hundred and sixty-ninth night. The two lights about a mile off that point are at their four hundred and eighty-seventh day. The six-line sheet at the back of that end stall is at the four hundred and forty-second day. The paper of four lines in Fish Street is at the four hundred and fifty-seventh day. The nine-line sheet with a mark on it in that front street is at the four hundred and seventy-fourth day. The man of about fifty-one is at the five hundred and fifth day of not speaking about six names. The leaf from the Cinder Bell is on its ninety-first day. That pair at that end stall has stood ninety-six days side by side with the wrong figure still left showing under the correction and not struck out. The rule in the inside of that door is on its sixty-fourth day and it has six lines on it and it is not on any wall. The honest total of four hundred and six pence is on its sixty-third day of being on that community's own page. The wall of that room over that chandler's store has stood bare for one hundred and ten days. Over the middle of those four columns is a boy's hand of twenty-two with nothing under it, and he is off the water for the eighth time. The fourth of them stands at one hundred and twenty-five and has taken no line in five days. Three hundred and sixty-third days is what the left side of that man of about twenty-nine's head has gone without, and he is not on the water.**

---

That first line settled at about four at seven hundred and ninety-four pence, being four marks, twelve shillings and tenpence, and about four of that nine stood round that bench stone at the end of that mole while it was being done.

The keeper wrote the settlement against that line and not against any other line, and that is the whole of what a settlement does to a fitting-out sheet, and it moves two figures and does not touch a third.

That foot has gone up and has not come down at any hour in five weeks, and about four of that nine said out loud that the whole of what that sheet has done in five weeks is go up, and that a thing that only goes one way is not a thing anybody is watching.

The ordinary work of that Friday was three boats off that mole before noon on that hard bright water, and the boiler at the head of the mole taken up at about one for the second time this month, and not one of those came near that fitting-out sheet, and nothing in the world is set against either half of it.

---

At about a quarter past four the keeper read the whole of the foot of that fitting-out sheet out loud in about nine people, and he read it after that settlement and not before it, as he has read it on every day there has been one since September.

He read out that foot standing at one hundred and thirty-three thousand nine hundred and eighty-two pence out, being eight hundred and thirty-seven marks, five shillings and twopence, and one hundred and twenty-three thousand three hundred and forty-two pence short, being seven hundred and seventy marks, eleven shillings and tenpence, against ten thousand six hundred and forty pence in.

Nobody wrote that figure down out loud in that shed and nobody repeated it after him. About four of that nine had it before he said it and about four of that nine did not.

---

The wooden slate was in the coat of the man of about thirty-two and did not come out of it, and about four of that nine did not know it was in his coat, and one of that nine knew.

And while that reading was still going on, the man of about thirty-two said out loud, to nobody in particular, that he does not know who puts that wall up.

About four people heard all of it and nobody asked him how long he has been counting it, and he did not say, and about four of that nine heard the man say it and went on standing exactly where they were standing.

---

About four of that nine said out loud, in the ordinary way and to nobody in particular, that a figure somebody keeps in his own head about a thing on the other side of a bench is the same as a figure somebody keeps in his own head about a page.

The man of about thirty-two said out loud that it is not the same, and did not say why, and nobody asked him why, and that is a statement and not a question and is not counted.

About four of those nine then asked, in the ordinary way and to nobody in particular, whether that week's coal is the coal of last month, and nobody in that shed bought it and nobody answered that.

One of about four of them asked whether a line worked on a Wednesday has ever settled on a Saturday, and the keeper said out loud that he does not know and does not intend to find out, and that the foot of that sheet has been read after a settlement on every day there has been one and never before one.

Nobody answered the second of those two either, and nobody asked a third question at any hour of that Friday.

---

From about ten until about two a man of about thirty-four of the second lay sat at that bench, and not one person in that lane asked him a thing, and that is the hundred and fifteenth day of that run of days he has not missed.

The woman of thirty, who holds no office, was at that bench stone from about ten until about eleven and read the face of that line off it with her own eyes and did not touch it, and the man of about thirty-two did not know that and was not told.

Eight sets of hands went onto the faces of three sheets that left that harbor that day, and the hand that wrote what each of those sheets went against was on none of them, and the page of days carries eight against this day.

---

**LINE ONE SETTLED AT ABOUT FOUR AT SEVEN HUNDRED AND NINETY-FOUR PENCE AND THE FOOT WAS READ AFTER THAT SETTLEMENT AND NOT BEFORE IT, AT ONE HUNDRED AND THIRTY-THREE THOUSAND NINE HUNDRED AND EIGHTY-TWO PENCE OUT AND ONE HUNDRED AND TWENTY-THREE THOUSAND THREE HUNDRED AND FORTY-TWO PENCE SHORT. TWO QUESTIONS WENT INTO THE COLUMN OF THE ASKED ON THIS DAY, BEING WHETHER THAT WEEK'S COAL IS THE COAL OF LAST MONTH AND WHETHER A LINE WORKED ON A WEDNESDAY HAS EVER SETTLED ON A SATURDAY, AND NEITHER OF THEM WAS ANSWERED. THE COLUMN WAS NOT ADDED UP. THE BAG HOLDS TWENTY-EIGHT PENCE, BEING TWO SHILLINGS AND FOURPENCE, AND THIS YARD IS TWO HUNDRED AND SEVENTY-EIGHTH DAYS WITHOUT A TIN IN IT.**

that line one settled at about four at seven hundred and ninety-four pence, being four marks, twelve shillings and tenpence, and that about four of that nine stood round that bench stone while it was being done; *Second column, not checkable from a mole, entered on the said Friday at about six in the evening in that shed, and no name is against it: that the reason a man can say out loud in about nine people that he does not know who puts a wall up and be let stand there is that this harbor has no office anybody could put that name into, and that a thing with no office behind it cannot be given to anybody, and that six weeks of figures have taught this harbor what happens when a name goes under a number; that the reason about four of that nine said out loud that a figure kept in a man's own head about a bench is the same as one kept about a page is that both of them are figures nobody can check, and that they are not the same because one of them has a date on it and the other one has a man in it, and that nobody in that shed put those two reasons side by side; that the reason nobody answered whether that week's coal is the coal of last month is that every man in that shed has burned coal and no man in that shed has bought it, and that a question about the buyer of a thing is a question about a person and this harbor has spent six weeks not asking about persons; that the reason the foot of that sheet is read after a settlement and never before one is that the two figures at the foot of it are a debt and a bill, and that a debt read out before it has been agreed is an accusation, and that this yard has agreed it since September and does not read it early.
