# Chapter 871

*Thursday the Eighth Day of December, the Eleventh Day of a Run of Fifty Days, a Rota Day and the Two Hundred and Eighth Charge on That Cistern at About Ten Past Ten and Fourteen Trips by Hand and Walked On by None of It, the Old Man of About Sixty-Eight Going Up Ten of Those Fourteen With the Barrow as He Has Done Since the Middle of August, the Wooden Slate Out of the Coat of the Man of About Thirty-Two at About Half Past Two and Into It Again Before Three and Nobody in That Shed Knowing, the Bag at Twenty-Eight Pence, the Four Columns Closing at Six Hundred and Four and Two Thousand Eight Hundred and Ninety-Four and None and One Hundred and Twenty-Five, the First Column at Six Hundred and Three and the Second at Two Thousand Eight Hundred and Eighty-Nine*

Thursday the eighth day of December, with that market open from six and a wet morning gone to sleet by nine and cleared again by one with the cold coming back into the shade of that mole, a yard below that market with a shed standing in it, and about nine people standing in that shed at half past five that afternoon.

**This said Thursday is a rota day and carries a charge on that cistern and no wipe and moves nothing on a wage, and the next wipe inside this run is on Sunday the eleventh day of December and the next charge is on Monday the twelfth day of December and the next wage Tuesday is on Tuesday the thirteenth day of December. That cistern went its fourteenth trip at about ten past ten and it was the two hundred and eighth time that butt has been filled in this harbor, and it was walked on by none of it. Nothing came into the foot of that fitting-out sheet today and no line was worked on it and none is owed to it, that foot standing at one hundred and thirty-five thousand and fifty pence out, being eight hundred and forty-four marks and tenpence, and one hundred and twenty-four thousand four hundred and ten pence short, being seven hundred and seventy-seven marks, seven shillings and sixpence, against ten thousand six hundred and forty pence in. A hundred and sixty-eight pence came into that yard at about ten and a hundred and sixty-eight went out of it at about four, and under that slate is twenty-eight pence, being two shillings and fourpence, and that shed is two hundred and eighty-fourth days without a tin opened in it.**

**Nine counters were carried to the close of this eleventh day. That notice of the seventeenth of May past is at its five hundred and sixty-ninth day and no answer has come back off it. The bellwright of this harbor is into the five hundred and seventy-first day of not being asked a question. Those two objects upon that crown which belong to nobody are at the nine hundred and ninth day. The bench beneath that pump lay bare again on its six hundred and seventy-fifth night. The two lights about a mile off that point are at their four hundred and ninety-third day. The six-line sheet at the back of that end stall is at the four hundred and forty-eighth day. The paper of four lines in Fish Street is at the four hundred and sixty-third day. The nine-line sheet with a mark on it in that front street is at the four hundred and eightieth day. The man of about fifty-one is at the five hundred and eleventh day of not speaking about six names. The leaf from the Cinder Bell is on its ninety-seventh day. That pair at that end stall has stood one hundred and two days side by side with the wrong figure still left showing under the correction and not struck out. The rule in the inside of that door is on its seventieth day and it has six lines on it and it is not on any wall. The honest total of four hundred and six pence is on its sixty-ninth day of being on that community's own page. The wall of that room over that chandler's store has stood bare for one hundred and sixteen days. Over the middle of those four columns is a boy's hand of twenty-two with nothing under it, and he is off the water for the eighth time. The fourth of them stands at one hundred and twenty-five and has taken no line in eleven days. Three hundred and sixty-ninth days is what the left side of that man of about twenty-nine's head has gone without, and he is not on the water.**

---

That butt's fourteenth load went up at about ten past ten with two men and a barrow, and it was the two hundred and eighth time that butt has been filled in this harbor, and its covers went on at about eleven and came off at about four, and nobody put a stick into it at any hour of that day.

The old man of about sixty-eight is the second man on those pails and he went up ten of those fourteen times with the barrow on his shoulder, as he has gone up ten of them on every Monday and Thursday since the middle of August, and he did not once put the barrow down in the middle.

He put it down against that door at about one and stood against the boards beside it for the length of a smoke, and nobody in that shed asked him a thing, and about four of that nine did not know he had come up with the water at all.

---

The ordinary work of that Thursday was two boats off that mole before noon on that sleet and a barrow of sheet copper from the store to the back door of that shed, and not one of those came near that fitting-out sheet, and nothing in the world is set against either half of it.

Nine hands went onto the faces of two sheets that left that harbor that day, and not one of those nine was the hand that wrote what either sheet went against, and the page of days carries nine against this day.

---

At about half past two, at the back of that shed behind the door where the light does not reach, the man of about thirty-two took a wooden slate out of the inside of his coat and carried it in his own two hands and did not put it down on anything.

Down one face of that wood there are figures in his own hand, a pair of minutes at every entry and a third figure under the pair, going from the bottom of the slate up to about two-thirds of the way, and there is no day written against any of them except the last five or six, which were done on a slate with a corner broken off and are not the same slate.

About four of that nine did not know that wood was in that room and one of that nine knew and said nothing about it then and has not said anything about it since.

He went up the column from the bottom looking for the first entry on it, and the wood is worn smooth at the top left corner and the first figure standing at that corner is half gone down into the grain, and there is no day against it and no day against the eleven under it.

He turned that slate over and looked at the back of it, and the back of that wood is blank and has been blank for all the time he has been carrying it.

---

The thing he wanted out of that wood was a day, and there was no day on it anywhere, and a figure with no day against it is not a record of a thing on a day.

A man who cannot say which morning he began does not know what the column on that slate is a column of, and this one had never noticed that before that Thursday at about half past two.

He said nothing out loud to anybody about it, and he put that slate back inside his coat at about ten to three, and he went back out to the bench and picked up the other end of a length of line, and about four of that nine went on doing what they had been doing and did not know that anything had happened at the back of that shed.

---

About four of that nine said out loud, in the ordinary way and to nobody in particular, that nineteen winters is the longest run of anything anybody in this harbor can put a number to that is not a day, and that nobody has ever agreed which nineteen they were.

One of about four of those nine said out loud that a number everybody has heard and nobody has agreed to is worth having, and the keeper said out loud that it is worth having and is worth nothing, and nobody in that shed said anything back to that.

---

From about ten until about two a man of about thirty-four of the second lay sat at that bench, and not one person in that lane asked him a thing, and that is the hundred and twenty-first day of that run of days he has not missed.

The woman of thirty, who holds no office, worked that yard over her own page from about ten until about one, and the man of about thirty-two was in that shed at about half past two with that slate in his coat and neither of the two of them said one word to the other one about anything at all.

---

About half past five on that Thursday the day's whole was said out loud at that stone standing up, in about one thousand and seventy-three words, and that cistern came first in it and the slate came in the middle of it and the four columns came at the end of it, and the only figure of that thing behind that reeve's bench inside a hundred yards of that shed is on that piece of wood and there is no day against any of it.

Nobody was thanked for a Thursday on which a man looked for a day at the top of a piece of wood and did not find one, and that shed stood empty by about six with its door standing open on a slip nobody was on, and that page of that week had eighty-eight lines on it.

THE MAN OF ABOUT THIRTY-TWO TOOK THE WOODEN SLATE OUT OF HIS COAT AT ABOUT HALF PAST TWO AND FOUND NO DAY AGAINST THE FIRST FIGURE ON IT AND SAID NOTHING OUT LOUD TO ANYBODY ABOUT IT AND PUT IT BACK IN HIS COAT AT ABOUT TEN TO THREE. NO FIGURE OF THAT THING BEHIND THAT REEVE'S BENCH IS ON ANY PAGE IN THIS HARBOR AND THE ONLY FIGURES OF IT ARE ON THAT PIECE OF WOOD. THE BAG HOLDS TWENTY-EIGHT PENCE, BEING TWO SHILLINGS AND FOURPENCE, AND THIS YARD IS TWO HUNDRED AND EIGHTY-FOURTH DAYS WITHOUT A TIN IN IT.**

*Second column, not checkable from a mole, entered on the said Thursday at about six in the evening in that shed, and no name is against it: that the reason a man takes a piece of wood out of his coat at about half past two on a Thursday is that a thing he has carried a long time becomes furniture, and that furniture is the one kind of thing a man cannot see the state of; that the reason there is no day against the figures at the top of that slate is that he did not write days on it, and that a man who does not write a day under a figure is not keeping a record and does not know he is not, and that the not-knowing is the part that lasts; that the reason about four of that nine did not know that wood was in that room is that a thing nobody has asked a man for is invisible in a room of nine people, and that this is the ordinary condition of everything a harbor knows about itself; that the reason nineteen winters was said out loud on that Thursday is that a run of winters is the nearest thing in that shed to the thing that wood carries, and that a run of winters has never once come up a minute and nobody in that shed compared the two.
