# Chapter 886

*Friday the Twenty-Third Day of December, the Twenty-Sixth Day of a Run of Fifty Days, Line Five Settled at About Four at One Thousand Four Hundred and Forty-Two Pence, Being Nine Marks and Twopence, the Whole of That Foot Read Out Loud After That Settlement and Not Before It at About a Quarter Past Four, at One Hundred and Thirty-Eight Thousand Four Hundred and Forty-Two Pence Out and One Hundred and Twenty-Seven Thousand Eight Hundred and Two Pence Short, at About Ten Past Four the Slate Brought into That Shed and Left Face Up on That Bench with a Hundred and Thirty-Seven Mornings of Figures on It and the Hundred and Thirty-Eighth Not Written On It, and the Man of About Thirty-Two Saying Out Loud That He Is Not Going to Count That Thing Again, the Bag at Twenty-Eight Pence, the Four Columns Closing at Six Hundred and Nineteen and Two Thousand Nine Hundred and Sixty-Nine and None and One Hundred and Twenty-Five, the First Column at Six Hundred and Eighteen and the Second at Two Thousand Nine Hundred and Sixty-Four*

Friday the twenty-third day of December, with that market open from six and a sleet coming off that point until about eleven and then turning to a cold rain that went on all afternoon without stopping once, a yard below that market with a shed standing in it, and about nine people standing in that shed at half past five that afternoon.

**This said Friday is an ordinary working Friday and carries no charge on that cistern and no wipe and moves nothing on a wage, and it settles one line on that fitting-out sheet and works none, and the next wipe inside this run is on Sunday the twenty-fifth day of December and the next charge is on Monday the twenty-sixth day of December and the next wage Tuesday is on Tuesday the twenty-seventh day of December. That fifth line settled at about four at one thousand four hundred and forty-two pence, being nine marks and twopence, and that foot is read after that settlement and on no other day of this week. A hundred and sixty-eight pence came into that yard at about ten and a hundred and sixty-eight went out of it at about four, and under that slate is twenty-eight pence, being two shillings and fourpence, and that shed is two hundred and ninety-ninth days without a tin opened in it.**

**Nine counters were carried through this twenty-sixth day. The notice of the seventeenth of May past is at its five hundred and eighty-fourth day and no answer has come back off it. That bellwright goes into the five hundred and eighty-sixth day of not being asked a question. The two objects upon that crown which belong to nobody stand at the nine hundred and twenty-fourth day. That bench beneath that pump was bare again on its six hundred and ninetieth night. Those two lights about a mile off that point are at their five hundred and eighth day. The six-line sheet at the back of that end stall is at the four hundred and sixty-third day. That paper of four lines in Fish Street stands at its four hundred and seventy-eighth day. That nine-line sheet with a mark on it in that front street stands at its four hundred and ninety-fifth day. The man of about fifty-one is at the five hundred and twenty-sixth day of not speaking about six names. The leaf from the Cinder Bell is on its hundred and twelfth day. That pair at that end stall has stood one hundred and seventeenth days side by side with the wrong figure still left showing under the correction and not struck out. The rule in the inside of that door is on its eighty-fifth day and it carries six lines and it is on no wall in this harbor. The honest total of four hundred and six pence has been on that community's own page for eighty-four days. The wall of that room over that chandler's store has stood bare for one hundred and thirty-first days. Over the middle of those four columns is a boy's hand of twenty-two with nothing under it, and he is off the water for the eighth time. The fourth of them stands at one hundred and twenty-five and has taken no line in twenty-six days. Three hundred and eighty-fourth days is what the left side of that man of about twenty-nine's head has gone without, and he is not on the water.**

---

That fifth line settled at about four at one thousand four hundred and forty-two pence, being nine marks and twopence, and about four of that nine stood round that bench stone at the end of that mole while it was done.

At about a quarter past four the keeper read the whole of that foot out loud in about nine people, after that settlement and not before it, and that foot standing at one hundred and thirty-eight thousand four hundred and forty-two pence out, being eight hundred and sixty-five marks, three shillings and sixpence, and one hundred and twenty-seven thousand eight hundred and two pence short, being seven hundred and ninety-eight marks, ten shillings and twopence.

About four of that nine had that figure before he said it and about four of that nine did not, and nobody wrote it down out loud and nobody repeated it after him.

The keeper said out loud that that sheet has carried five lines since the second of December, and that three of the five settled on a Friday and two settled on a Monday, and that he settles a line on no other day and never has.
The sheet with the fifteen gaps stands at fifteen and has stood there since the twenty-fifth day of September, and that is the one figure in this harbor that four people agree about and nobody can account for.

---

At about ten past four he brought that piece of wood into that shed in his own two hands and put it down face up on that bench, and there were a hundred and thirty-seven mornings of figures on it in his own hand and the hundred and thirty-eighth was not written on it.

Then he said out loud that he is not going to count that thing again, and about four of that nine heard every word of that and about four of that nine were at the far end of that yard and did not.

He put his hands in his pockets after that and stood where he had put it and did not pick it up again at any hour of that Friday and did not give it to anybody and nobody in that shed asked him for it.

---

One of about four of that nine said out loud that a count handed over is not a count, and that a figure nobody has counted in their own head belongs to nobody who has counted anything.

One of about four of that nine said out loud that the four of them had been in that lane every one of those hundred and thirty-seven Sundays anyway and had never needed him for a single figure of any of it.

One of about four of that nine said out loud that they had known what that wood was for a fortnight ago and had said nothing at the time, and that saying nothing at the time is the same as saying nothing now.

Nobody answered any of those three things, and he said nothing back to any of them, and about four of that nine went on with what they were doing.

---

That bench stone at the end of that mole had a hundred and thirty-seven mornings of figures lying face up on it from about ten past four until that shed stood empty at about six, and about four people walked past it in that half hour and every one of them looked down at it and not one of them put a hand on it.

The slate is still on that bench and has not been taken off it by anybody, and there is no page in this harbor and no board and no drawer in any room in this harbor that has one figure of that thing on it.

The back of that slate is blank and has been blank for as long as he has been carrying it, and the top corner of it is worn smooth where a thumb goes, and about four of that nine have looked at the front of that wood and not one of them has asked to see the back of it.
The pencil the keeper put into his hand on Wednesday is still in that coat and has not been used on anything at all, and about four of that nine noticed that and about four of that nine did not.

---

The woman of thirty, who holds no office, worked that yard over her own page from about ten until about two and then came through that shed at about half past two for her own book, and he was at the other end of that yard and did not tell her one word about a piece of wood or about any of it.

They are still married and neither of the two of them said one word to the other about any part of that Friday, and she does not know that he has been counting anything.

The ordinary work of that Friday was two boats off that mole before noon in that rain and a barrow of ash taken out to the far end of that quay and left standing there in the wet, and not one of those came near that fitting-out sheet, and nothing in the world is set against either half of it.

From about ten until about two a man of about thirty-four of that second lay sat at that bench, and not one person in that lane asked him a thing, and that is the hundred and thirty-sixth day of that run of days he has not missed.

The man of about thirty-one of the Lowfen Reed's own place had been in that harbor fifty-two days by that Friday and has asked nobody for anything on any of the fifty-two.

Twelve hands went onto the faces of two sheets that left that harbor that day, and the page of days carries twelve against this day.

---

*Second column, not checkable from a mole, entered on the said Friday at about six in the evening in that shed, and no name is against it: that the reason four people said out loud that a count handed over is not a count is that a count handed over is a count anybody else may start keeping, and that the four of them have each of them been keeping two of the figures on that wood in their own heads for six weeks without ever saying so, and that a figure kept by two people is a figure that can be wrong by two different amounts at once; that the reason nobody answered those three sentences is that the only answer to any of them would have been a claim, and this harbor has four kinds of thing it may put up in a room and a man who has just put down a piece of wood is not one of those four; that the reason not one of about four people put a hand on that wood is that every one of them had understood in the same quarter of an hour what it was, and that a hand on a piece of wood is a thing two of them had talked about and had both decided against; that the reason the woman of thirty has not been told is that telling her is a thing that can be done at any hour of any day, and that a man who has put a figure down in a shed at ten past four on a Friday in that rain has not got round to it and has not been asked.*
