# Chapter 902

*Sunday the Eighth Day of January, the Forty-Second Day of a Run of Fifty Days, a Wipe and the Hundred and Fortieth Time, That Thing off the Face of Itself at About Half Past Ten and Standing Bare Forty-Four Minutes and Back Up at a Quarter to One and Not Going Up Whole, Fifty-Seven Off and Fifty-Seven Back and One Hundred and Fifty-Eight the Whole of It, Both Ends a Minute Longer Than the Two Ends of the Sunday Before Ran, About Four People in Front of It, the Man of About Thirty-One of That Lowfen Reed's Own Place on His Tenth Sunday in That Lane Saying Nothing, the Bag at Twenty-Eight Pence, the Four Columns Closing at Six Hundred and Thirty-Five and Three Thousand and Forty-Nine and None and One Hundred and Twenty-Five, the First Column at Six Hundred and Thirty-Four and the Second at Three Thousand and Forty-Four*

Sunday the eighth day of January, with that market shut until seven on a Sunday and a hard frost lying white along that quay with a clear sky off that point, a yard below that market with a shed standing in it, and about nine people standing in that shed at half past five that afternoon.

**This said Sunday is a wipe day and carries no charge on that cistern and moves nothing on a wage and settles nothing on a fitting-out line, and the next wipe inside that run is on Sunday the fifteenth day of January and the next charge is on Monday the ninth day of January and the next wage Tuesday is on Tuesday the tenth day of January. That thing behind that reeve's bench came off the face of itself at about half past ten and stood bare forty-four minutes and went back up unrepaired and not whole, at fifty-seven minutes off and fifty-seven back, one hundred and fifty-eight the whole of it, and that is the hundred and fortieth time anybody has done it to it, being a hundred and thirty-nine weeks from the first time anybody did it to it. That foot standing at one hundred and thirty-eight thousand nine hundred and six pence out, being eight hundred and sixty-eight marks, two shillings and twopence, and one hundred and twenty-eight thousand two hundred and sixty-six pence short, being eight hundred and one marks, eight shillings and tenpence, is not read at any hour of that Sunday. A hundred and sixty-eight pence came into that yard at about ten and a hundred and sixty-eight went out of it at about four, and under that slate is twenty-eight pence, being two shillings and fourpence, and that shed is three hundred and fifteenth days without a tin opened in it.**

**Nine tallies were carried through that forty-second day. That notice of the seventeenth of May past runs at its six hundredth day. That bellwright passes into the six hundred and second day of not being asked anything by anybody. The two objects upon that crown which belong to nobody rest at the nine hundred and fortieth day. That bench beneath that pump stood bare again on its seven hundred and sixth night. Those two lights about a mile off that point shine at their five hundred and twenty-fourth day. The six lines at the back of that end stall keep at the four hundred and seventy-ninth day. That paper of four lines in Fish Street holds at its four hundred and ninety-fourth day. That nine-line sheet with a mark on it in that front street stays at its five hundred and eleventh day. The man of about fifty-one remains at the five hundred and forty-second day of not speaking about six names. The leaf from the Cinder Bell stays on its hundred and twenty-eighth day. That pair at that end stall has stood one hundred and thirty-third days side by side at one size with the wrong figure still showing under the correction and not struck out. The rule in the inside of that door stands on its hundred and first day and it carries six lines and it is on no wall in that harbor. The honest total of four hundred and six pence, being two marks, seven shillings and twopence, has been on that community's own page for a hundred days. That wall of that room over that chandler's store has stood bare for one hundred and forty-seventh days. Over the middle of those four columns is a boy's hand of twenty-two with nothing under it, and he is off the water for the eighth time. The fourth of them stands at one hundred and twenty-five and has taken no line in forty-two days. Four hundredth days is what the left side of that man of about twenty-nine's head has gone without, and he is not on the water.**

---

That frost lay hard along that quay before six and made the stones ring underfoot, and about four of that nine said out loud that a frost on a Sunday morning keeps everybody out of that lane but the ones who mean to be in it.

---

At about half past ten the top course came off that thing and about four people stepped up to it and began to count, and it stood bare forty-four minutes and went back up at a quarter to one with the top course of it left short, one hundred and fifty-eight the whole of it, being the hundred and fortieth time.

Both ends of it ran a minute longer than the two ends of the Sunday before ran, and nobody in that lane said a word about either end of it.

For the fourth Sunday running nobody in that lane has said why and nobody in that lane has asked.

---

Those four are a man of about forty-one and a man of about thirty-three and a man of about fifty and a man of about twenty-six, and none of the four was in that shed at any hour of the six days before it.

They said their own figures out loud to one another in that lane, and about two of those four figures were a minute apart, and nobody in that lane told any of them to do that and nobody in that lane told any of them to stop.

Not one figure of any kind stands against the counting or against the figures of any of the four on any page in that harbor, and none of the four was asked anything by anybody.

---

A man came down that lane at about ten and was in it for the half hour it takes him and went away again before a quarter to one, and he did not speak to any of those four and none of those four spoke to him.

About four people in that lane would not be able to describe him that afternoon, and about four of that nine said out loud that a man who is in a lane for the half hour it takes him is a man nobody watches.

A man of about thirty-one of that Lowfen Reed's own place stood in that lane from about half past ten until about a quarter to one and watched four people count and said nothing at any hour of it, and that is the tenth Sunday he has stood in that lane and he has not said one word on any of the ten. He had been in that harbor sixty-eight days by that Sunday and has asked nobody for anything on any of the sixty-eight.

The man of about thirty-two was not in that lane at any hour of that Sunday and did not stand in front of that thing, and he was in that shed at half past five that afternoon.

---

The ordinary work of that Sunday was one boat off that mole before noon in that frost and a hand-cart of coal left standing in the open with a rope over it, and not one of those came near that fitting-out sheet, and nothing in the world is set against either half of it.

That piece of wood has been face up on that bench in that shed for sixteen days and nobody has touched it at any hour of that Sunday at all.

The woman of thirty, who holds no office, worked that yard over her own page until about two and did not come into that shed at any hour of that Sunday, and the man of about thirty-two did not tell her one word about anything.

From about ten until about two a man of about thirty-four of that second lay sat at that bench, and not one person in that lane asked him a thing, and that is the hundred and fifty-second day of that run of days he has not missed.

Twelve hands went onto the faces of two sheets that left that harbor that day, and the page of days carries twelve against this day.

---

About half past five on that Sunday the day's whole was said out loud at that stone standing up, in about eight hundred and ninety words, and that wipe came first in it and the four in front of it came in the middle of it and the four columns came at the end of it, and the figure of that thing behind that reeve's bench is fifty-seven off and forty-four standing bare and fifty-seven back and one hundred and fifty-eight the whole of it.

"**That frost lay hard along that quay before six and made the stones ring underfoot, and about four of that nine said out loud that a frost on a Sunday morning keeps everybody out of that lane but the ones who mean to be in it. At about half past ten the top course came off that thing and about four people stepped up to it and began to count, and it stood bare forty-four minutes and went back up at a quarter to one with the top course of it left short, one hundred and fifty-eight the whole of it, being the hundred and fortieth time. Both ends of it ran a minute longer than the two ends of the Sunday before ran, and nobody in that lane said a word about either end of it. For the fourth Sunday running nobody in that lane has said why and nobody in that lane has asked about either end. Those four are a man of about forty-one and a man of about thirty-three and a man of about fifty and a man of about twenty-six, and none of the four was in that shed at any hour of the six days before that Sunday. He had been in that harbor sixty-eight days by that Sunday and has asked nobody for anything on any of them. The man of about thirty-two was not in that lane at any hour of that Sunday and did not stand in front of that thing, and he was in that shed at half past five that afternoon. The ordinary work of that Sunday was one boat off that mole before noon in that frost and a hand-cart of coal left standing in the open with a rope over it in the frost. That piece of wood has been face up on that bench in that shed for sixteen days and nobody has touched it at any hour of that Sunday at all. From about ten until about two nobody asked a man of about thirty-four of that second lay for one thing, being the hundred and fifty-second day of that run. Twelve hands went onto the faces of two sheets that left that harbor that day. A hundred and sixty-eight pence came into this yard at about ten and a hundred and sixty-eight went out of it at about four, and twenty-eight plus one thousand six hundred and eighty less one thousand six hundred and eighty is twenty-eight, and the twenty-eight is under that slate, being two shillings and fourpence, which is six hundred and twelve pence short of the wage of four marks, being three marks eleven shillings. Three hundred and fifteenth days is how long that yard has gone without a tin in it. That boy's hand of twenty-two heads the middle of those four columns and he is off the water for the eighth time and his surname is on no page. The fourth of them is at one hundred and twenty-five and has taken no line in forty-two days and is neither shut nor struck. One hundred and thirty-third days is what that pair has stood at that stall, one size and a hand's breadth apart, with the wrong figure left showing under that correction. The leaf from the Cinder Bell is on its hundred and twenty-eighth day. The sheet with the fifteen gaps stands at fifteen and that wall of the room over the chandler's store has stood bare for one hundred and forty-seventh days. It is four hundredth days since anything came out of the left side of that man of about twenty-nine's head, he has not been on the water, and no figure at all is set against any part of it. The hearing in the left side of the man of about thirty-two is not priced. Nine hulls sit off that mole head at first light.**"

Nobody was thanked for a Sunday on which that thing came off itself for the hundred and fortieth time and four people stood in front of it and neither end of it was a minute shorter than the one before, and that shed stood empty by about six with its door standing open on a slip nobody was on, and that page of that week had a hundred and nineteen lines on it.

*First column, entered on Sunday the eighth day of January at about six in the evening in that shed, being the forty-second entry of that run of fifty days: that the said day was a wipe day and was not a rota day and was not a wage Tuesday, and that the next wipe inside that run is on Sunday the fifteenth day of January and the next charge is on Monday the ninth day of January and the next wage Tuesday is on Tuesday the tenth day of January; that that thing came off the face of itself at about half past ten and stood bare forty-four minutes and went back up at a quarter to one and did not go up whole, at fifty-seven minutes off and fifty-seven back, one hundred and fifty-eight the whole of it, being the hundred and fortieth time; that both ends ran a minute longer and nobody said why and nobody asked; that about four people stood in front of it, being a man of about forty-one and a man of about thirty-three and a man of about fifty and a man of about twenty-six; that a man of about thirty-one of that place stood in that lane for the tenth Sunday and said nothing; that the man of about thirty-two was not in that lane at any hour.*

*Second column, not checkable from a mole, entered on the said Sunday at about six in the evening in that shed, and no name is against it: that the reason nobody in that lane said why either end ran longer is that a minute a side a week has been running so long in that lane that nobody in that lane has any word for it but the figure itself, and that four men counting side by side have no question to put to one another; that the reason none of the four was asked anything is that nobody in that harbor has the standing to ask anybody that, and that there has not been one since the fourteenth of August; that the reason the man of about thirty-two was not in that lane is not given out loud by anybody and is not a thing he has told anybody.*
