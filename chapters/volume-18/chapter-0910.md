# Chapter 910

*Monday the Sixteenth Day of January, the Fiftieth Day of a Run of Fifty Days, a Rota Day and the Two Hundred and Nineteenth Charge on That Cistern at About Ten Past Ten and Fourteen Trips by Hand and Walked On by None of It, Line Seven Settling at About Four and the Whole of That Sheet Read After That Settlement and Not Before It, the Outside of That Drawer Written On Again in a Keeper's Hand, That Piece of Wood Twenty-Four Days Face Up on That Bench, the Bag at Twenty-Eight Pence, the Four Columns Closing at Six Hundred and Forty-Three and Three Thousand and Eighty-Nine and None and One Hundred and Twenty-Five, the First Column at Six Hundred and Forty-Two and the Second at Three Thousand and Eighty-Four*

Monday the sixteenth day of January, with that market open from six and a lane lying empty below that bench before six with a cold light on it, a yard below that market with a shed standing in it, and about nine people standing in that shed at half past five that afternoon.

**This said Monday is a rota day and carries a charge on that cistern and no wipe and moves nothing on a wage, and it settles line seven at about four with the sixteenth day of January on its face and the foot is read after that settlement and not before it, and there is no wipe after it inside that run and there is no charge after it and there is no wage Tuesday after it. That cistern went its fourteenth trip at about ten past ten and it was the two hundred and nineteenth time and it was walked on by none of it. Line seven settles at about four at seven hundred and sixty-two pence, being four marks, ten shillings and twopence, in five parts, being two days of a boat hired at the figure in chalk, being two times one hundred and sixty-eight, which is three hundred and thirty-six, and coal and oil for two days at forty pence, and seventy yards of line at three pence the yard, being seventy times three, which is two hundred and ten, and four panes of sheet glass at twelve pence a pane, being four times twelve, which is forty-eight, and four men at two days at sixteen pence a man-day, being four times two times sixteen, which is one hundred and twenty-eight, and the foot after that settlement stands at one hundred and thirty-nine thousand six hundred and sixty-eight pence out, being eight hundred and seventy-two marks, twelve shillings and fourpence, and one hundred and twenty-nine thousand and twenty-eight pence short, being eight hundred and six marks, five shillings and eightpence, against ten thousand six hundred and forty pence in. A hundred and sixty-eight pence came into that yard at about ten and a hundred and sixty-eight went out of it at about four, and under that slate is twenty-eight pence, being two shillings and fourpence, and that shed is three hundred and twenty-third days without a tin opened in it.**

**Nine closings stood through that fiftieth day. That notice of the seventeenth of May past is closed at its six hundred and eighth day. That bellwright is closed into the six hundred and tenth day of not being asked anything by anybody. The two objects upon that crown which belong to nobody are closed at the nine hundred and forty-eighth day. That bench beneath that pump was closed bare again on its seven hundred and fourteenth night. Those two lights about a mile off that point are closed at their five hundred and thirty-second day. The six lines at the back of that end stall are closed at the four hundred and eighty-seventh day. That paper of four lines in Fish Street is closed at its five hundred and second day. That nine-line sheet with a mark on it in that front street is closed at its five hundred and nineteenth day. The man of about fifty-one is closed at the five hundred and fiftieth day of not speaking about six names. The leaf from the Cinder Bell is closed on its hundred and thirty-sixth day. That pair at that end stall has stood one hundred and forty-first days side by side at one size with the wrong figure still showing under the correction and not struck out. The rule in the inside of that door is closed on its hundred and ninth day and it carries six lines and it is on no wall in that harbor. The honest total of four hundred and six pence, being two marks, seven shillings and twopence, has been on that community's own page for a hundred and eight days. That wall of that room over that chandler's store had stood bare for one hundred and fifty-fourth days until Sunday and now carries seven figures at one size in a keeper's hand, being on its hundred and fifty-fifth day. Over the middle of those four columns is a boy's hand of twenty-two with nothing under it, and he is off the water for the eighth time. The fourth of them stands at one hundred and twenty-five and has taken no line in fifty days. Four hundred and eighth days is what the left side of that man of about twenty-nine's head has gone without, and he is not on the water.**

---

That lane lay empty below that bench before six and showed nobody in it, and about four of that nine said out loud that an empty lane before six on a Monday means the week begins without anybody watching it.

---

That cistern was filled for the two hundred and nineteenth time at about ten past ten with two men on the handles, and the covers went on at about eleven and came off at about four, and nobody walked on one single trip of it.

The old man of about sixty-eight went up ten of those fourteen with that barrow on his shoulder and did not once put it down in the middle, and not one figure of any kind stands in any page in that harbor against that man or against anything that he does.

About four of that nine said out loud that the last charge of a run of fifty days is still fourteen trips by hand like any other, and about four of that nine said nothing back to that.

---

At about four line seven settled and the keeper read the whole of that sheet after that settlement and not before it, being one hundred and thirty-nine thousand six hundred and sixty-eight pence out and one hundred and twenty-nine thousand and twenty-eight pence short, and about four of that nine said out loud that a foot read after is a foot that has taken the line in.

About four of that nine said out loud that seven hundred and sixty-two pence for two days of a boat and coal and line and glass and four men is smaller than what was asked for, and about four people in that room know it.

The count of the figures put up on a wall was taken once in writing at about six, and its ten days stand at nothing and nothing and nothing and nothing and nothing and nothing and nothing and nothing and seven and nothing, which is seven.

---

That piece of wood has been face up on that bench in that shed for twenty-four days and nobody has touched it at any hour of that Monday.

The man of about thirty-two was in that shed from about ten until about half past five and said almost nothing at any hour of that Monday.

The woman of thirty, who holds no office, worked that yard over her own page from about ten until about four, and the two of them are still married, and neither of the two of them said one word to the other about any part of any of it.

---

At about half past four the keeper went up to that room over that store and wrote on the outside of that drawer again in his own hand what is in it and the day it went in, and about four people standing in that room read it and said out loud that the figures are on that wall and not in that drawer, and that this is the first thing that has gone on a wall in that room for one hundred and fifty-four days.

Nothing in that harbor explains that drawer and nothing said out loud in that room explained it.

---

The man of about thirty-one of that Lowfen Reed's own place had been in that harbor seventy-six days by that Monday and stood at the back of that shed for about two hours of it and said nothing.

The leaf from the Cinder Bell has been on that board for its hundred and thirty-sixth day, and it is on the face of every chapter since that run began and was not dropped on any of them.

The ordinary work of that Monday was two boats off that mole before noon on a flat sea and a barrow of ash taken out to the far end of that quay, and not one of those came near that fitting-out sheet, and nothing in the world is set against either half of it.

From about ten until about two a man of about thirty-four of that second lay sat at that bench, and not one person in that lane asked him a thing, and that is the hundred and sixtieth day of that run of days he has not missed.

Ten hands went onto the faces of two sheets that left that harbor that day, and the page of days carries ten against this day.

---

At about a quarter to ten that night the man of about thirty-two walked up that lane from the end of it on his way home and did not stop at that reeve's bench, and behind that bench about four people were standing in front of that thing behind that reeve's bench in about nine people who were not him counting something out loud together, and they were still counting when he had gone up the lane, and nobody in that lane asked them what they were doing or who told them to.

---

At about eleven the keeper weighed the glass and the line for that settlement and found them right, and about four of that nine said out loud that a settlement found right at about eleven will read right at about four.

About four of that nine said out loud at about noon that a Monday that settles a line and fills a cistern is a Monday with two hours in it that pull against one another, and the keeper did both without hurry and wrote nothing down before the hour.

Nine buoys of nine shapes lay off that mole head that Monday and answered one another at two hours of it, and the sound did not run together into one note and nobody on that quay tried to make it one.

---

About half past five on that Monday the day's whole was said out loud at that stone standing up, in about one thousand and fifty words, and that cistern came first in it and that line came in the middle of it and the four columns came at the end of it, and the only figure of that thing behind that reeve's bench within a hundred yards of that shed is on that piece of wood and there is no figure of it on any page in that harbor.

Nobody was thanked for a Monday on which line seven settled at about four and the foot was read after it and a drawer was written on again and four people counted behind a bench at a quarter to ten and nobody asked them anything, and that shed stood empty by about six with its door standing open on a slip nobody was on, and that page of that week had a hundred and twenty-seven lines on it.

*First column, entered on Monday the sixteenth day of January at about six in the evening in that shed, being the fiftieth entry of that run of fifty days: that the said day was a rota day and was not a wipe day and was not a wage Tuesday; that that cistern went its fourteenth trip at about ten past ten and it was the two hundred and nineteenth time and the old man of about sixty-eight went up ten; that line seven settled at about four at seven hundred and sixty-two pence and the foot after it stands at one hundred and thirty-nine thousand six hundred and sixty-eight pence out and one hundred and twenty-nine thousand and twenty-eight pence short and was read after and not before; that at about half past four the keeper wrote on the outside of that drawer again what is in it and the day it went in; that the count of the figures put up on a wall is seven with ten days at nothing and nothing and nothing and nothing and nothing and nothing and nothing and nothing and seven and nothing; that at about a quarter to ten the man of about thirty-two walked up that lane and did not stop and behind that bench about four people were still counting out loud together and nobody asked them what they were doing.*

*Second column, not checkable from a mole, entered on the said Monday at about six in the evening in that shed, and no name is against it: that the reason line seven settled smaller than what was asked for is that a line settled is a line paid at what it cost and not at what was wanted for it, and that about four people in that room know it; that the reason the foot was read after and not before is that a foot read before a settlement is a foot without the line in it, and that this harbor reads its feet after; that the reason nobody asked those four behind that bench what they were doing is that nobody in that harbor has the standing to ask anybody that, and that there has not been one since the fourteenth of August.*
