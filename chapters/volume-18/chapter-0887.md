# Chapter 887

*Saturday the Twenty-Fourth Day of December, the Twenty-Seventh Day of a Run of Fifty Days, Line Six onto That Fitting-Out Sheet at About Eight With the Twenty-Sixth Day of December Written on the Face of It for the Day It Settles on a Monday, Six Men Out at About Eight and In at About Half Past Three, One Day of a Boat Hired at the Figure in Chalk and Coal and Oil for One Day and Fifty Yards of Line and a Case and Four Nails and Six Men at One Day, the Whole of That Line Four Hundred and Sixty-Four Pence, Being Two Marks and Twelve Shillings, and Not One Penny of It in the Foot of That Sheet Until Monday, the Piece of Wood Still Face Up on That Bench Where It Went Down on Friday, the Bag at Twenty-Eight Pence, the Four Columns Closing at Six Hundred and Twenty and Two Thousand Nine Hundred and Seventy-Four and None and One Hundred and Twenty-Five, the First Column at Six Hundred and Nineteen and the Second at Two Thousand Nine Hundred and Sixty-Nine*

Saturday the twenty-fourth day of December, with that market shut until seven on a Saturday and a fog off that point that did not lift until about noon and left the roofs of that yard white all day with it, a yard below that market with a shed standing in it, and about nine people standing in that shed at half past five that afternoon.

**This said Saturday is an ordinary working Saturday and carries no charge on that cistern and no wipe and settles nothing on a wage, and it puts one line onto that fitting-out sheet and settles nothing on it, and the next wipe inside this run is on Sunday the twenty-fifth day of December and the next charge is on Monday the twenty-sixth day of December and the next wage Tuesday is on Tuesday the twenty-seventh day of December. That sixth line went onto that sheet at about eight with the twenty-sixth day of December written on the face of it for the day it settles, and that day is a Monday. That foot is not read at any hour of that Saturday and is not read out loud before that Monday afternoon at any hour. That foot stands at one hundred and thirty-eight thousand four hundred and forty-two pence out, being eight hundred and sixty-five marks, three shillings and sixpence, and one hundred and twenty-seven thousand eight hundred and two pence short, being seven hundred and ninety-eight marks, ten shillings and twopence, against ten thousand six hundred and forty pence in. A hundred and sixty-eight pence came into that yard at about ten and a hundred and sixty-eight went out of it at about four, and under that slate is twenty-eight pence, being two shillings and fourpence, and that shed is three hundredth days without a tin opened in it.**

**Nine counters were carried through this twenty-seventh day. The notice of the seventeenth of May past is at its five hundred and eighty-fifth day and no answer has come back off it. That bellwright goes into the five hundred and eighty-seventh day of not being asked a question. The two objects upon that crown which belong to nobody stand at the nine hundred and twenty-fifth day. That bench beneath that pump was bare again on its six hundred and ninety-first night. Those two lights about a mile off that point are at their five hundred and ninth day. The six-line sheet at the back of that end stall is at the four hundred and sixty-fourth day. That paper of four lines in Fish Street stands at its four hundred and seventy-ninth day. That nine-line sheet with a mark on it in that front street stands at its four hundred and ninety-sixth day. The man of about fifty-one is at the five hundred and twenty-seventh day of not speaking about six names. The leaf from the Cinder Bell is on its hundred and thirteenth day. That pair at that end stall has stood one hundred and eighteenth days side by side with the wrong figure still left showing under the correction and not struck out. The rule in the inside of that door is on its eighty-sixth day and it carries six lines and it is on no wall in this harbor. The honest total of four hundred and six pence has been on that community's own page for eighty-five days. The wall of that room over that chandler's store has stood bare for one hundred and thirty-second days. Over the middle of those four columns is a boy's hand of twenty-two with nothing under it, and he is off the water for the eighth time. The fourth of them stands at one hundred and twenty-five and has taken no line in twenty-seven days. Three hundred and eighty-fifth days is what the left side of that man of about twenty-nine's head has gone without, and he is not on the water.**

---

Six men went out on that sixth line at about eight and came in at about half past three, and that line is the first one in this run that has been worked for one day only, and about four of that nine said out loud that one day is not a line and that it is a day somebody has written down.

That sixth line went onto that sheet at about eight with the twenty-sixth day of December written on the face of it for the day it settles, and that day is a Monday, and not one penny of it comes into the foot of that sheet before that Monday afternoon.

Its five parts are one day of a boat hired at the figure in chalk at one hundred and sixty-eight for one hundred and sixty-eight pence, being one mark and eight pence; coal and oil for one day at twenty pence, being one shilling and eightpence; fifty yards of line at three pence the yard at fifty times three for one hundred and fifty pence, being twelve shillings and sixpence; a case and four nails at thirty pence, being two shillings and sixpence; and six men at one day at sixteen pence a man-day at six times one times sixteen for ninety-six pence, being eight shillings.

The whole of that line is four hundred and sixty-four pence, being two marks and twelve shillings, and the keeper read the total back to nobody.

---

That piece of wood was still lying face up on that bench at about eight that morning and was still lying face up on that bench at about six that evening, and nobody in that shed picked it up at any hour of that day.

The man of about thirty-two looked down at it once at about eleven and did not touch it, and about four of that nine did not know where that wood was and one of that nine knew and said nothing about it.

The keeper looked at it once at about four and said nothing about it, and about four of that nine have not mentioned it to anybody since that day.

A man of about fifty-eight of that market came through that shed at about two, looked down at that piece of wood for about as long as it takes to count something small, said nothing at all about it, and went on to his own stall with his own slate under his arm. He was in that shed for about a minute and a half and nobody spoke to him and he spoke to nobody, and about four of that nine worked out in that minute and a half that a man can stand in a room and not be in it at all.
About four of that nine said out loud that the covers came off that cistern on Thursday at about four for the two hundred and twelfth time, and that no page in this harbor has one line about a cistern in it, and that a thing nobody writes down still gets done two hundred and twelve times.

---

A man of about thirty-one of the Lowfen Reed's own place came into that shed at about nine that morning and stood in that doorway for about ten minutes, and he had been in that harbor fifty-three days and had not been in that shed before ten on any of them.

About four of that nine said out loud that a man who has stood at the bottom of a lane on seven Sundays has a right to stand in a doorway on a Saturday, and about four of those nine said nothing back to that, and he went out again at about ten and said nothing to anybody at all on his way out.

The woman of thirty, who holds no office, worked that yard over her own page from about ten until about one and then put a lamp on that bench beside that piece of wood and left it burning until about half past five, and about four of that nine said out loud that no lamp in that yard has been left burning after four in this run and about four of that nine said nothing about it.

---

The lamp on that bench was still burning at about half past five and about four of that nine had worked round it all day without moving it, and a bench with a lamp on one side of it and a hundred and thirty-seven mornings of figures on the other side of it is not a thing anybody in that yard had been in the way of seeing before.

The ordinary work of that Saturday was three boats off that mole before noon in that fog and a barrow of fifty yards of that line run out to the second lay and brought back with nine yards of it in a piece, and not one of those came near that fitting-out sheet, and nothing in the world is set against either half of it.

The woman of thirty, who holds no office, and the man of about thirty-two said one word to each other about that lamp when it was put down and nothing at all about any other part of that Saturday.

From about ten until about two a man of about thirty-four of that second lay sat at that bench, and not one person in that lane asked him a thing, and that is the hundred and thirty-seventh day of that run of days he has not missed.

Eleven hands went onto the faces of two sheets that left that harbor that day, and the page of days carries eleven against this day.

*First column, entered on Saturday the twenty-fourth day of December at about six in the evening in that shed, being the twenty-seventh entry of this run of fifty days: that the said day was an ordinary working Saturday and was not a rota day and was not a wipe day and was not a wage Tuesday, and that the next wipe inside this run is on Sunday the twenty-fifth day of December and the next charge is on Monday the twenty-sixth day of December and the next wage Tuesday is on Tuesday the twenty-seventh day of December; that six men went out on that sixth line at about eight and came in at about half past three, and that it is the first line in this run worked for one day only; that line six went onto that fitting-out sheet at about eight with the twenty-sixth day of December written on the face of it for the day it settles, and that day is a Monday, and that not one penny of it comes into the foot of that sheet before that Monday afternoon; that its five parts are one day of a boat at the figure in chalk at one hundred and sixty-eight, coal and oil for one day at twenty pence, fifty yards of line at three pence the yard at fifty times three, a case and four nails at thirty pence, and six men at one day at sixteen pence a man-day at six times one times sixteen; that the man of about thirty-two looked down at it once at about eleven and did not touch it, and that about four of that nine did not know where that wood was and one of that nine knew; that the keeper looked at it once at about four and said nothing about it; *Second column, not checkable from a mole, entered on the said Saturday at about six in the evening in that shed, and no name is against it: that the reason nobody picked that wood up in that shed on that Saturday is that four people looked at it at four different hours of that day and each of them worked out the same thing and none of them said it, and that a room that has reached the same conclusion four times in one day has nothing left to say about it; that the reason he did not go up that lane at any hour of that Saturday is that there is nothing in that lane until half past ten tomorrow and a man who has given up a thing does not go and look at where he gave it up; that the reason a man who has stood at the bottom of that lane on seven Sundays was put in a doorway on a Saturday morning is that about four of that nine have worked out that he is one of the four who has been standing there all along, and that nobody said so to him and nobody said so to themselves out loud; that the reason a lamp was left burning on that bench until half past five is that a woman who works over her own page in that yard has wanted better light in that yard since the middle of November and has not said so, and that putting that lamp down beside a piece of wood nobody explained is not a remark about the wood.*
