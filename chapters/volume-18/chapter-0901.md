# Chapter 901

*Saturday the Seventh Day of January, the Forty-First Day of a Run of Fifty Days, an Ordinary Working Saturday Carrying No Charge on That Cistern and No Wipe and Moving Nothing on a Wage and Settling Nothing on a Fitting-Out Line, That Piece of Wood Fifteen Days Face Up on That Bench and Nobody Going Near It, the Bag at Twenty-Eight Pence, the Four Columns Closing at Six Hundred and Thirty-Four and Three Thousand and Forty-Four and None and One Hundred and Twenty-Five, the First Column at Six Hundred and Thirty-Three and the Second at Three Thousand and Thirty-Nine*

Saturday the seventh day of January, with that market open from six and a pale morning off that point with a thin mist hanging low along that quay and no rain in it, a yard below that market with a shed standing in it, and about nine people standing in that shed at half past five that afternoon.

**This said Saturday is an ordinary working Saturday and carries no charge on that cistern and no wipe and moves nothing on a wage, and it settles nothing on a fitting-out line and works none, and the next wipe inside that run is on Sunday the eighth day of January and the next charge is on Monday the ninth day of January and the next wage Tuesday is on Tuesday the tenth day of January. Nothing came into the foot of that fitting-out sheet on that Saturday and nothing is owed to it, that foot standing at one hundred and thirty-eight thousand nine hundred and six pence out, being eight hundred and sixty-eight marks, two shillings and twopence, and one hundred and twenty-eight thousand two hundred and sixty-six pence short, being eight hundred and one marks, eight shillings and tenpence, against ten thousand six hundred and forty pence in, and it is not read at any hour of that Saturday. A hundred and sixty-eight pence came into that yard at about ten and a hundred and sixty-eight went out of it at about four, and under that slate is twenty-eight pence, being two shillings and fourpence, and that shed is three hundred and fourteenth days without a tin opened in it.**

**Nine tallies were kept through that forty-first day. That notice of the seventeenth of May past stands at its five hundred and ninety-ninth day. That bellwright moves into the six hundred and first day of not being asked anything by anybody. The two objects upon that crown which belong to nobody sit at the nine hundred and thirty-ninth day. That bench beneath that pump lay bare again on its seven hundred and fifth night. Those two lights about a mile off that point burn at their five hundred and twenty-third day. The six lines at the back of that end stall run at the four hundred and seventy-eighth day. That paper of four lines in Fish Street rests at its four hundred and ninety-third day. That nine-line sheet with a mark on it in that front street lies at its five hundred and tenth day. The man of about fifty-one keeps at the five hundred and forty-first day of not speaking about six names. The leaf from the Cinder Bell sits on its hundred and twenty-seventh day. That pair at that end stall has stood one hundred and thirty-second days side by side at one size with the wrong figure still showing under the correction and not struck out. The rule in the inside of that door runs on its hundredth day and it carries six lines and it is on no wall in that harbor. The honest total of four hundred and six pence, being two marks, seven shillings and twopence, has been on that community's own page for ninety-nine days. That wall of that room over that chandler's store has stood bare for one hundred and forty-sixth days. Over the middle of those four columns is a boy's hand of twenty-two with nothing under it, and he is off the water for the eighth time. The fourth of them stands at one hundred and twenty-five and has taken no line in forty-one days. Three hundred and ninety-ninth days is what the left side of that man of about twenty-nine's head has gone without, and he is not on the water.**

---

That rain had cleared that quay before six and left the stones dark and shining, and about four of that nine said out loud at about ten that a Saturday after a wet night shows every mark on that quay and keeps none of them by noon.

---

The man of about thirty-two was in that shed from about ten until about half past five and said almost nothing at any hour of that Saturday, and about four of that nine said out loud that a man who works without speaking is still working.

That piece of wood has been face up on that bench in that shed for fifteen days and nobody has touched it at any hour of that Saturday at all, and about four of that nine have stopped looking down at it when they pass it.

The woman of thirty, who holds no office, worked that yard over her own page from about ten until about four, and the two of them are still married, and neither of the two of them said one word to the other about any part of any of it, and she does not know that he has been counting anything.

---

The man of about thirty-one of that Lowfen Reed's own place had been in that harbor sixty-seven days by that Saturday and stood at the back of that shed for about two hours of it and said nothing and asked nobody for anything.

About four of that nine said out loud at about three that sixty-seven days at the back of a shed without asking for one thing is a thing nobody in that yard has seen before, and about four of that nine said nothing back to that.

The leaf from the Cinder Bell has been on that board for its hundred and twenty-seventh day, and about four of that nine said out loud that nobody in that yard can read the other side of it, and about four of that nine said nothing back to that.

---

The ordinary work of that Saturday was two boats off that mole before noon on a flat sea and a barrow of sand run up to that second lay and left standing by that door, and not one of those came near that fitting-out sheet, and nothing in the world is set against either half of it.

From about ten until about two a man of about thirty-four of that second lay sat at that bench, and not one person in that lane asked him a thing, and that is the hundred and fifty-first day of that run of days he has not missed.

Eleven hands went onto the faces of two sheets that left that harbor that day, and the page of days carries eleven against this day.

---

That slate with a hundred and thirty-seven mornings on it lay face up on that bench while that bench was occupied from about ten until about two, and the man of about thirty-four sat at that same bench with his sleeve near the edge of it and never once put a hand on it.

About four of that nine said out loud that a bench that is sat at for four hours a day for a hundred and fifty-one days is a bench nobody needs to ask about, and about four of that nine said nothing back to that.

The keeper swept that shed at about four and left that piece of wood where it lay and swept round it, and about four of that nine watched him do that and wrote nothing down.

---

That bell in that shed rang the hour at about four and nobody in that shed lifted a hand to it, and about four of that nine said out loud that a bell that rings without a hand on it is still a bell that marks the hour for working men.

About four of that nine said out loud that a Saturday with no charge and no wipe is still a day somebody worked from about ten until about four, and about four of that nine said nothing back to that at any hour.

---

About half past five on that Saturday the day's whole was said out loud at that stone standing up, in about eight hundred and eighty words, and the rain came first in it and the bench came in the middle of it and the four columns came at the end of it, and the only figure of that thing behind that reeve's bench within a hundred yards of that shed is on that piece of wood and there is no figure of it on any page in that harbor.

"**That rain had cleared that quay before six and left the stones dark and shining, and about four of that nine said out loud at about ten that a Saturday after a wet night shows every mark on that quay and keeps none of them by noon. The man of about thirty-two was in that shed from about ten until about half past five and said almost nothing at any hour of that Saturday, and about four of that nine said out loud that a man who works without speaking is still working and is not thinking of nothing. That piece of wood has been face up on that bench in that shed for fifteen days and nobody has touched it at any hour of that Saturday at all, and about four of that nine have stopped looking down at it when they pass it by that bench. About four of that nine said out loud that a bench that is sat at for four hours a day for a hundred and fifty-one days is a bench nobody needs to ask about in that lane. The keeper swept that shed at about four and left that piece of wood where it lay and swept round it carefully in that shed. That bell in that shed rang the hour at about four and nobody in that shed lifted a hand to it, and about four of that nine said out loud that a bell that rings without a hand on it is still a bell that marks the hour for working men in that shed. About four of that nine said out loud that a Saturday with no charge and no wipe is still a day somebody worked from about ten until about four, and about four said nothing back to that at any hour of that Saturday. A hundred and sixty-eight pence came into this yard at about ten and a hundred and sixty-eight went out of it at about four, and twenty-eight plus one thousand six hundred and eighty less one thousand six hundred and eighty is twenty-eight, and the twenty-eight is under that slate, being two shillings and fourpence, which is six hundred and twelve pence short of the wage of four marks, being three marks eleven shillings. Three hundred and fourteenth days is how long that yard has gone without a tin in it. That boy's hand of twenty-two heads the middle of those four columns and he is off the water for the eighth time and his surname is on no page. The fourth of them is at one hundred and twenty-five and has taken no line in forty-one days and is neither shut nor struck. One hundred and thirty-second days is what that pair has stood at that stall, one size and a hand's breadth apart, with the wrong figure left showing under that correction. The leaf from the Cinder Bell is on its hundred and twenty-seventh day. The sheet with the fifteen gaps stands at fifteen and that wall of the room over the chandler's store has stood bare for one hundred and forty-sixth days. It is three hundred and ninety-ninth days since anything came out of the left side of that man of about twenty-nine's head, he has not been on the water, and no figure at all is set against any part of it. The hearing in the left side of the man of about thirty-two is not priced. Nine hulls sit about two sea miles off that mole head.**"

Nobody was thanked for a Saturday on which a piece of wood lay fifteen days on a bench and a bench was sat at for a hundred and fifty-one days and nobody touched either, and that shed stood empty by about six with its door standing open on a slip nobody was on, and that page of that week had a hundred and eighteen lines on it.

*First column, entered on Saturday the seventh day of January at about six in the evening in that shed, being the forty-first entry of that run of fifty days: that the said day was an ordinary working Saturday and was not a rota day and was not a wipe day and was not a wage Tuesday, and that the next wipe inside that run is on Sunday the eighth day of January and the next charge is on Monday the ninth day of January and the next wage Tuesday is on Tuesday the tenth day of January; that nothing came into the foot of that fitting-out sheet on that day and that nothing is owed to it and that the keeper did not read that foot at any hour of that Saturday; that that piece of wood has been face up on that bench for fifteen days and that nobody touched it; that the man of about thirty-two was in that shed from about ten until about half past five and said almost nothing; that the woman of thirty worked that yard over her own page and neither said one word to the other; that eleven hands went onto two sheets; that from about ten until about two nobody asked a man of about thirty-four for one thing.*

*Second column, not checkable from a mole, entered on the said Saturday at about six in the evening in that shed, and no name is against it: that the reason nobody has touched that piece of wood for fifteen days is that a thing left face up on a bench in a working shed becomes part of that shed before anybody has decided what to do with it, and that about four people pass it four times a day and have stopped seeing it; that the reason the man of about thirty-two said almost nothing is not given out loud by anybody in that harbor and is not a thing he has told anybody; that the reason nobody asked a man of about thirty-four for one thing is that a bench sat at from about ten until about two for a hundred and fifty-one days is a bench nobody in that lane has any question for.*
